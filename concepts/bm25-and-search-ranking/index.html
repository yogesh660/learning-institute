BM25 and search ranking

BM25 (Best Matching 25, or Okapi BM25) is the industry-standard probabilistic ranking function for lexical search engines such as Lucene and Elasticsearch. It overcomes the two core weaknesses of classical TF-IDF: unbounded term frequency runaway and aggressive length distortion. By introducing asymptotic term frequency saturation via hyperparameter k1 and penalising verbose documents relative to the average corpus length via hyperparameter b, BM25 provides resilient, high-precision search ranking.

01 The idea

If a user searches for "machine learning", a document that repeats the word "learning" one thousand times should not automatically outrank a concise, perfectly targeted technical tutorial. Under vanilla TF-IDF, term frequency grows linearly or logarithmically without an upper bound, making keyword-stuffed pages dominate search results. Okapi BM25 fixes this fundamental flaw: it models the probabilistic odds of relevance, caps the scoring contribution of any single term via an asymptotic saturation curve, and balances document length penalisation against average corpus depth.

The diminishing returns of term frequency: Imagine reading an introductory paper on neural networks. The first mention of the word "backpropagation" is enormously informative—it signals that the document is genuinely about neural training. The fourth mention confirms its centrality. But does the fiftieth mention make the document fifty times more relevant than the first? Obviously not. BM25 introduces term frequency saturation: the relevance boost granted by each additional occurrence of a query term rapidly tapers off, approaching an asymptotic upper ceiling (k1 + 1).

Document length and the dual nature of verbosity: A long document can arise for two very different reasons: verbosity (the author used 3,000 words to explain what could be said in 300) or scope (the document is a comprehensive handbook covering 50 distinct subtopics). If a search engine ignores document length, long documents win every query by sheer token volume. If it divides by document length naively (like Euclidean normalisation in TF-IDF), comprehensive articles are unfairly penalised. BM25 uses hyperparameter b to calibrate length normalisation against the corpus mean length, penalising repetitive verbosity while preserving fair scoring for comprehensive documents.

Why BM25 anchors the modern search stack: Despite the rise of dense neural embeddings and vector databases, BM25 remains indispensable in production information retrieval. Dense vector models struggle with exact product serial numbers, rare medical jargon, and specific names where semantic similarity fails. Production search architectures almost universally rely on hybrid retrieval: an inverted index running BM25 retrieves high-precision candidate documents in microseconds, which are then fused with dense embeddings using reciprocal rank fusion before neural reranking.

In everyday terms

Think of judging candidate job applicants based on a required skill like "Python programming". Candidate A mentions Python twice across a tidy one-page curriculum vitae. Candidate B is a spammer who copy-pasted the word "Python" five hundred times at the bottom of their CV in tiny font. Under linear TF-IDF, Candidate B scores 250 times higher than Candidate A and gets hired immediately. BM25 acts like an experienced hiring manager: hearing "Python" twice is great evidence of competence, but hearing it twenty more times adds almost no extra conviction (saturation via k1). Furthermore, the manager judges whether the applicant's CV is abnormally long compared to typical CVs (length normalisation via b), easily exposing spam while rewarding concise, relevant experience.

03 The maths

  1. Robertson-Spärck Jones IDF (Lucene non-negative formulation)

    \operatorname{IDF}(q_i) = \ln \left( 1 + \frac{N - n(q_i) + 0.5}{n(q_i) + 0.5} \right)

    Here $N$ is total corpus size and $n(q_i)$ is the number of documents containing term $q_i$. Adding $1$ inside the natural logarithm guarantees that $\operatorname{IDF}(q_i) \ge 0$ for all terms, eliminating the risk of negative scores on common words.

  2. Okapi BM25 scoring function

    \operatorname{BM25}(D, Q) = \sum_{i=1}^{|Q|} \operatorname{IDF}(q_i) \cdot \frac{f(q_i, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(q_i, D) + k_1 \left( 1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\operatorname{avgdl}} \right)}

    Here $f(q_i, D)$ is raw within-document term frequency, $|D|$ is document length in tokens, $\operatorname{avgdl}$ is the collection mean length, $k_1 \in [1.2, 2.0]$ controls saturation speed, and $b \in [0.5, 0.8]$ calibrates document length normalisation.

  3. Term frequency asymptotic upper bound

    \lim_{f(q_i, D) \to \infty} \frac{f(q_i, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(q_i, D) + K} = k_1 + 1, \quad \text{where } K = k_1 \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\operatorname{avgdl}}\right)

    Unlike vanilla TF-IDF which grows without bound, the term frequency component in BM25 is strictly bounded above by $(k_1 + 1)$, ensuring diminishing marginal returns and neutralising keyword-stuffing attacks.

04 Key takeaways

  • Okapi BM25 is a probabilistic ranking function that resolves the runaway term frequency and length distortion issues of TF-IDF.
  • Hyperparameter k1 enforces term frequency saturation, establishing an asymptotic ceiling on any single term's score contribution.
  • Hyperparameter b penalises overly verbose documents relative to the collection average length avgdl while fairly scoring comprehensive documents.
  • Lucene-style non-negative IDF prevents terms appearing in more than 50% of documents from generating negative relevance scores.
  • BM25 is the universal sparse lexical baseline in modern search engines and the foundational lexical retrieval leg in hybrid RAG systems.

05 In code

python
import math
import re
from collections import Counter

class BM25Okapi:
    """
    Self-contained implementation of Okapi BM25 ranking algorithm
    with Lucene-style non-negative IDF and length normalisation.
    """
    def __init__(self, corpus, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
        self.k1 = k1
        self.b = b
        self.corpus_size = len(corpus)
        self.doc_lengths = []
        self.doc_freqs = Counter()
        self.doc_term_freqs = []
        self.avgdl = 0.0
        self.idf = {}
        
        self._build_index(corpus)
        
    def _tokenize(self, text):
        return [tok for tok in re.split(r'\W+', text.lower()) if tok]

    def _build_index(self, corpus):
        total_len = 0
        for doc in corpus:
            tokens = self._tokenize(doc)
            length = len(tokens)
            self.doc_lengths.append(length)
            total_len += length
            
            tf = Counter(tokens)
            self.doc_term_freqs.append(tf)
            
            for term in tf.keys():
                self.doc_freqs[term] += 1
                
        self.avgdl = total_len / self.corpus_size if self.corpus_size > 0 else 0.0
        
        # Precompute Lucene-style non-negative Robertson-Spärck Jones IDF
        for term, df in self.doc_freqs.items():
            numerator = self.corpus_size - df + 0.5
            denominator = df + 0.5
            self.idf[term] = math.log(1.0 + (numerator / denominator))

    def score(self, query_tokens, doc_index):
        score = 0.0
        doc_len = self.doc_lengths[doc_index]
        tf_dict = self.doc_term_freqs[doc_index]
        
        len_factor = 1.0 - self.b + self.b * (doc_len / self.avgdl)
        
        for q in query_tokens:
            if q not in self.idf:
                continue
            freq = tf_dict.get(q, 0)
            if freq == 0:
                continue
                
            idf_val = self.idf[q]
            tf_comp = (freq * (self.k1 + 1.0)) / (freq + self.k1 * len_factor)
            score += idf_val * tf_comp
            
        return score

    def rank(self, query, top_k=5):
        q_tokens = self._tokenize(query)
        scores = []
        for doc_idx in range(self.corpus_size):
            s = self.score(q_tokens, doc_idx)
            scores.append((doc_idx, s))
            
        scores.sort(key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
        return scores[:top_k]

# Sample corpus
corpus = [
    "Machine learning algorithms build mathematical models based on sample training data.",
    "Deep neural networks and convolutional networks excel at computer vision tasks.",
    "Natural language processing and search engines use inverted indices and ranking functions.",
    "Search engines use BM25 to rank documents based on query term frequency and document length.",
    "Machine learning search engines combine dense embeddings and lexical BM25 for hybrid retrieval.",
    "The weather in London is cloudy with occasional showers in late autumn."
]

bm25 = BM25Okapi(corpus, k1=1.5, b=0.75)

print(f"Corpus size: {bm25.corpus_size} documents")
print(f"Average document length: {bm25.avgdl:.2f} tokens")
print("\nSample IDF weights:")
for sample_term in ["search", "engines", "bm25", "learning", "weather"]:
    print(f"  IDF('{sample_term}'): {bm25.idf.get(sample_term, 0.0):.4f}")

test_query = "search engines BM25 ranking"
print(f"\nTop search results for query: '{test_query}'")
rankings = bm25.rank(test_query, top_k=3)
for rank, (doc_idx, score) in enumerate(rankings, 1):
    print(f"{rank}. [Score: {score:.4f}] Doc {doc_idx}: '{corpus[doc_idx]}'")

Common pitfalls

  • Using unfloored original RSJ IDF: The original 1976 formula produces negative IDF values when a term appears in more than half the corpus, causing documents matching common terms to lose rank. Always use the modern non-negative Lucene formulation.
  • Setting b = 1 unconditionally: Complete length normalisation severely penalises long, authoritative reference articles covering multiple related topics. Standard practice sets b = 0.75 as an optimal empirical balance.
  • Mismatched query and index tokenisation: If index terms are lowercased and lemmatised but queries are processed verbatim with capital letters and punctuation, BM25 lookup misses matching posting lists entirely. Preprocessing pipelines must be identical.
  • Ignoring zero-count query terms in sparse scoring: Query terms absent from the dictionary have undefined IDF; ensure the query evaluator gracefully defaults unknown tokens to zero score without raising key errors.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does BM25 utilise term frequency saturation controlled by hyperparameter k1 instead of linear term frequency?

Why: Linear TF allows a document repeating a keyword 100 times to dominate rankings regardless of quality. By enforcing an asymptotic upper limit (k1 + 1), BM25 ensures that additional keyword occurrences yield diminishing marginal gains.

Q2What does setting the length normalisation parameter b = 0 in BM25 achieve?

Why: In the term K = k1 * (1 - b + b * (|D| / avgdl)), setting b = 0 collapses K to the constant k1. Document length |D| is completely eliminated from the scoring calculation.

Q3What is the purpose of the +1 offset inside the logarithm in modern Lucene-style BM25 IDF: ln(1 + (N - n(q) + 0.5) / (n(q) + 0.5))?

Why: In the original RSJ IDF formula, any word appearing in more than 50% of the corpus produced a negative value. Adding 1 inside the natural log guarantees that the argument is always >= 1, ensuring non-negative weights for all terms.
