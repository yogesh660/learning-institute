Maximum Likelihood Estimation

01 The idea

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) asks: of all possible parameter values, which one makes the data we actually observed most probable? Many familiar losses are just negative log-likelihoods in disguise.

Flip a coin 10 times and see 7 heads. If the coin’s bias were $p = 0.5$, that outcome has probability 0.117; if $p = 0.7$, it is 0.267, the highest of any $p$. So the MLE is $\hat p = 0.7$: the observed frequency.

We maximise the log-likelihood because products of many small probabilities underflow, and logs turn products into sums without moving the maximum. Assume Gaussian noise and the negative log-likelihood becomes squared error, so OLS is MLE. Assume Bernoulli labels and you get log-loss, so Logistic regression is MLE.

In everyday terms

A detective choosing the explanation that makes the evidence least surprising.

02 Step by step

  1. Write down a probability model $p(x \mid \theta)$ for one observation.
  2. Assume independence: the likelihood is the product over observations.
  3. Take logs: the log-likelihood is a sum.
  4. Maximise it, analytically (set derivative to zero) or numerically (gradient-based).

03 Try it

Flip a virtual coin. The curve is the log-likelihood of every possible bias $p$; its peak is the MLE.

Things to try
  1. Flip 5 times, then 100 times: the peak sharpens as evidence accumulates.
  2. Drag the probe to $p = 0.5$ and compare its log-likelihood with the peak.

04 The maths

  1. Likelihood and log-likelihood

    $$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta), \qquad \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n\log p(x_i \mid \theta)$$

    $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta\ell(\theta)$.

  2. Bernoulli example

    $$\ell(p) = k\log p + (n - k)\log(1 - p), \qquad \frac{d\ell}{dp} = \frac kp - \frac{n-k}{1-p} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat p = \frac kn$$

    The MLE is the sample proportion. Its negative per-example version is exactly binary cross-entropy.

  3. Gaussian noise gives least squares

    $$y_i = x_i^\top\beta + \varepsilon_i,\;\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) \;\Rightarrow\; -\ell(\beta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2 + \text{const}$$

    Maximising likelihood is minimising squared error.

05 Key takeaways

  • MLE is consistent and efficient for large samples, but can overfit small ones (7/10 heads is not strong evidence of a biased coin).
  • Adding a prior and maximising the posterior (MAP) gives regularisation.
  • Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model.

06 In code

python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

flips = np.array([1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1])        # 7 heads out of 10
nll = lambda p: -(flips.sum() * np.log(p) + (len(flips) - flips.sum()) * np.log(1 - p))
res = minimize_scalar(nll, bounds=(1e-6, 1 - 1e-6), method="bounded")
print("numerical MLE:", round(res.x, 4), "closed form:", flips.mean())

Common pitfalls

  • Treating the MLE from a tiny sample as reliable.
  • Forgetting the independence assumption behind the product.

07 Check yourself

Q1Why maximise the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood?

Why: $\log$ is monotonic, so the arg max is unchanged.

Q2Minimising binary cross-entropy is equivalent to:

Why: Cross-entropy is the negative Bernoulli log-likelihood per example.

Q3What critical assumption about the data points $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ allows the total likelihood to be written as a simple product $\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta)$?

Why: By the probability product rule for independent events, $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. If observations are independent and identically distributed, the joint likelihood factors into the product of individual marginal densities.

Q4Maximising the likelihood of a linear regression model with independent additive Gaussian noise $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ is mathematically identical to minimising which loss function?

Why: The Gaussian probability density includes the term $\exp\left(-\frac{(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$. Taking the negative logarithm yields $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \text{constant}$, which is exactly proportional to the squared error loss.

Q5If the noise in a linear regression model follows a Laplace distribution $p(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{2b}\exp\left(-\frac{|\varepsilon|}{b}\right)$ rather than a Gaussian distribution, what loss function does Maximum Likelihood Estimation yield?

Why: Taking the negative logarithm of the Laplace density $-\ln p(\varepsilon) = \frac{|\varepsilon|}{b} + \ln(2b)$ produces an objective proportional to $\sum |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$, which is Mean Absolute Error (MAE). This explains why MAE regression corresponds to assuming Laplace-distributed errors.

Q6What is the connection between Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation and regularised empirical loss?

Why: By Bayes' theorem, the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior: $p(w \mid D) \propto p(D \mid w) p(w)$. In log-space, this is $\log L(w) + \log p(w)$. A Gaussian prior $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_w^2)$ gives Ridge regularisation ($\lambda \sum w_j^2$), and a Laplace prior gives Lasso ($\lambda \sum |w_j|$).

Q7A coin is tossed 3 times and lands on Heads every time ($k = 3, n = 3$). What is the Maximum Likelihood Estimate of the probability of heads $\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}}$, and what severe flaw does this illustrate?

Why: The Bernoulli MLE is $\hat{p} = k/n = 3/3 = 1.0$. Because MLE incorporates no prior knowledge, it claims that getting tails is impossible ($P(\text{Tails}) = 0$). In small samples, this extreme overfitting causes infinite log-loss if tails occurs in test data.

Q8Why does multiplying raw probabilities directly in the likelihood $L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta)$ cause catastrophic failure in computer programs for datasets with hundreds of rows?

Why: For example, $0.1^{100} = 10^{-100}$, which quickly underflows 64-bit double precision (limit $\sim 10^{-308}$). In contrast, log-likelihood sums values: $\sum \log(0.1) = 100 \times (-2.30) = -230$, which is completely numerically stable.

Q9What is the asymptotic consistency property of Maximum Likelihood Estimators as the sample size $n \to \infty$?

Why: Consistency means $\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta_0| > \epsilon) = 0$. Given enough data and an appropriately specified model, MLE is guaranteed to recover the true data-generating parameters.

Q10Suppose you observe 10 independent coin tosses with $k = 7$ heads and $n - k = 3$ tails. If the true parameter is $p$, the probability of this outcome is $P(k=7 \mid p) = \binom{10}{7} p^7 (1-p)^3$. What is the ratio of the likelihood under $p = 0.70$ compared to $p = 0.50$? (Given $P(k=7 \mid p=0.7) \approx 0.2668$ and $P(k=7 \mid p=0.5) \approx 0.1172$).

Why: Likelihood ratio $= \frac{L(0.70)}{L(0.50)} = \frac{0.2668}{0.1172} \approx 2.276 \approx 2.28$. The observed evidence is more than 2.2 times more probable under the MLE hypothesis ($p = 0.70$) than under a fair coin hypothesis ($p = 0.50$).

Q11For an exponential distribution with probability density $f(x \mid \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ ($x \ge 0$), the log-likelihood for $n$ independent observations is $\ell(\lambda) = n\ln(\lambda) - \lambda \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$. Solving $\frac{d\ell}{d\lambda} = 0$, what is the analytical Maximum Likelihood Estimator $\hat{\lambda}$?

Why: Derivative: $\frac{d\ell}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{\lambda} - \sum x_i = 0 \implies \frac{n}{\lambda} = \sum x_i \implies \hat{\lambda} = \frac{n}{\sum x_i} = \frac{1}{\bar{x}}$. The rate parameter MLE is the inverse of the sample mean.

Q12A dataset of 4 independent observations from a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with known variance $\sigma^2 = 1$ has values $\{1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 6.0\}$. What is the Maximum Likelihood Estimate of the mean $\hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}}$, and what is the value of the log-likelihood $\ell(\hat{\mu}) = -\frac{1}{2}\sum (x_i - \hat{\mu})^2 + C$?

Why: Sample mean $\hat{\mu} = (1.0 + 2.0 + 3.0 + 6.0) / 4 = 12.0 / 4 = 3.0$. Squared deviations from 3.0: $(1 - 3)^2 = 4$, $(2 - 3)^2 = 1$, $(3 - 3)^2 = 0$, $(6 - 3)^2 = 9$. Sum of squared deviations $= 4 + 1 + 0 + 9 = 14$. Thus $-\frac{1}{2}\sum (x_i - \hat{\mu})^2 = -\frac{1}{2}(14) = -7.0$. Total log-likelihood $= -7.0 + C$.
