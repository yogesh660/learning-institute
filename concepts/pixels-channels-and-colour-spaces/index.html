Pixels, channels and colour spaces

Digital images are discrete multidimensional arrays of sampled spatial irradiance measurements. Understanding coordinate conventions, intensity quantisation across bit depths, memory layout differences ($H \times W \times C$ vs $C \times H \times W$), and mathematical conversions between colour spaces (RGB, Grayscale, HSV, and CIE $L^*a^*b^*$) forms the essential foundation for computer vision algorithms and convolutional neural network architectures.

01 The idea

Every digital photograph or video frame begins life as photons striking a silicon sensor grid, converted into an array of electrical voltages and discretised into numerical matrices. Before a machine learning algorithm can recognise an object, segment a medical scan, or guide an autonomous drone, it must interpret these raw numeric tensors. How an image is represented—whether as raw primary additive colour intensities, decoupled luminance and chrominance, or perceptually uniform coordinates—directly determines how effectively downstream filters and neural layers learn invariant visual representations.

Spatial sampling and radiometric quantisation: A continuous visual scene is sampled on a rectangular lattice of discrete picture elements ("pixels") over a grid of height $H$ and width $W$. Each pixel's brightness is quantised into a discrete integer value governed by bit depth. In standard 8-bit imaging, intensities range over $2^8 = 256$ discrete levels from $0$ (absence of light, pure black) to $255$ (sensor saturation, pure white). Higher bit depths, such as 12-bit or 16-bit used in medical imaging and astronomy, offer expanded dynamic range to prevent clipping in intense highlights or deep shadows.

Additive trichromacy and the Bayer mosaic: Human colour perception is trichromatic, mediated by retinal cone cells sensitive to short (blue), medium (green), and long (red) optical wavelengths. Digital camera sensors capture this trichromatic signal using an overlaid mosaic filter (most commonly the Bayer colour filter array). Because human photopic vision possesses peak spatial and luminance acuity in the green spectral band, Bayer arrays allocate twice as many green sensor elements as red or blue (a $2 \times 2$ repeating pattern of $R, G, G, B$). Demosaicing algorithms then interpolate these sensor readings into three co-registered channel grids: Red, Green, and Blue.

Tensor layouts and channel ordering: In spatial matrix coordinates, the origin $(0, 0)$ is situated at the top-left corner of the image, with row indices $y \in [0, H-1]$ pointing vertically downwards and column indices $x \in [0, W-1]$ pointing horizontally to the right. Numerical libraries differ in how colour channels are arranged in memory. Image processing toolkits like OpenCV and NumPy store arrays in channel-last layout ($H \times W \times C$), whereas deep learning frameworks like PyTorch standardise on channel-first tensors ($C \times H \times W$ or batch-first $N \times C \times H \times W$) to optimise parallel matrix multiplications on GPU tensor cores.

The limitation of RGB and the power of decoupled colour spaces: RGB entangles illumination intensity (luminance) directly with chromatic hue. If an illuminant dims or a shadow falls across a surface, all three RGB colour values drop simultaneously, frustrating simple colour segmentation. Transforming to colour spaces like HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) or CIE $L^*a^*b^*$ decouples perceived lightness from chromaticity. This allows computer vision systems to achieve lighting invariance by operating purely on the chrominance coordinates while remaining robust to harsh lighting shifts.

In everyday terms

Think of colour spaces as different vocabularies for mixing paint. RGB is like ordering paint by specifying the exact millilitres of red, green, and blue base pigments poured from three mechanical dispensers. This formulation tells the dispensing machine exactly what to mix, but it makes intuitive adjustments difficult: if an artist asks for "the same paint shade, but lit by bright sunshine rather than twilight," you would have to calculate complex proportional changes across all three pigment valves simultaneously. HSV, by contrast, describes paint the way an artist thinks: "Which pure pigment angle on the colour wheel (Hue)? How saturated is it, or is it muted with white (Saturation)? And how much light is shining on the canvas (Value)?" Both vocabularies specify identical colours, but HSV lets you adjust lighting without altering the hue.

03 The maths

  1. Perceptual grayscale luminance (ITU-R BT.601)

    Y = 0.299\,R + 0.587\,G + 0.114\,B

    The weights reflect the human eye's spectral sensitivity curve: green light contributes approximately $58.7\%$ of perceived luminance, red contributes $29.9\%$, and blue contributes only $11.4\%$. In high-definition video standards (ITU-R BT.709), updated coefficients $Y = 0.2126\,R + 0.7152\,G + 0.0722\,B$ are used.

  2. HSV colour transformation

    V = \max(R, G, B), \quad S = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } V = 0 \\ \frac{V - \min(R, G, B)}{V} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

    Value $V \in [0, 1]$ measures brightness. Saturation $S \in [0, 1]$ quantifies colour purity (with $S=0$ representing neutral greyscale). Hue $H \in [0^\circ, 360^\circ)$ represents angular colour tone computed piecewise depending on whether $R$, $G$, or $B$ is maximal.

  3. Perceptual colour difference in CIE L*a*b* space

    \Delta E^*_{ab} = \sqrt{(\Delta L^*)^2 + (\Delta a^*)^2 + (\Delta b^*)^2}

    In non-linear colour spaces like RGB, Euclidean distance does not align with human perceptual colour difference: two RGB vectors with distance 20 might appear indistinguishable, while another pair with distance 20 appears starkly different. CIE $L^*a^*b^*$ is designed to be perceptually uniform, such that Euclidean metric distance $\Delta E^*$ directly mirrors human perceptual difference.

04 Key takeaways

  • Digital images are 3D numeric tensors: Height $\times$ Width $\times$ Channels, typically quantised as 8-bit unsigned integers ($[0, 255]$).
  • Grayscale luminance conversion requires perceptual weighting ($0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B$) rather than a simple unweighted arithmetic mean.
  • RGB entangles lighting with chromaticity; HSV and CIE $L^*a^*b^*$ separate luminance from chrominance, enabling illumination-invariant feature extraction.
  • OpenCV reads images in BGR channel order by default, whereas PIL, matplotlib, and PyTorch expect standard RGB order.
  • Deep neural networks expect channel-first memory layout ($C \times H \times W$), requiring transposition from channel-last ($H \times W \times C$) numpy arrays.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Create a synthetic 4x4 RGB image with distinct primary and secondary colours
# Shape: Height=4, Width=4, Channels=3 (uint8)
img_rgb = np.zeros((4, 4, 3), dtype=np.uint8)
img_rgb[0, 0] = [255, 0, 0]     # Pure Red
img_rgb[0, 1] = [0, 255, 0]     # Pure Green
img_rgb[0, 2] = [0, 0, 255]     # Pure Blue
img_rgb[0, 3] = [255, 255, 0]   # Yellow (Red + Green)
img_rgb[1, 0] = [0, 255, 255]   # Cyan (Green + Blue)
img_rgb[1, 1] = [255, 0, 255]   # Magenta (Red + Blue)
img_rgb[1, 2] = [255, 255, 255] # White
img_rgb[1, 3] = [128, 128, 128] # Mid Grey

# 1. Grayscale conversion: ITU-R BT.601 perceptual luminance weights
# Y = 0.299 * R + 0.587 * G + 0.114 * B
rgb_norm = img_rgb.astype(np.float32) / 255.0
r = rgb_norm[..., 0]
g = rgb_norm[..., 1]
b = rgb_norm[..., 2]
gray_bt601 = 0.299 * r + 0.587 * g + 0.114 * b

print("RGB top-left 2x2 patch (R, G, B):")
print(img_rgb[:2, :2])

print("\nPerceptual Grayscale (BT.601, [0.0, 1.0]):")
print(np.round(gray_bt601[:2, :2], 3))
print(f"Pure Red luminance:   {gray_bt601[0, 0]:.3f} (matches 0.299)")
print(f"Pure Green luminance: {gray_bt601[0, 1]:.3f} (matches 0.587 - human eyes are most sensitive to green)")
print(f"Pure Blue luminance:  {gray_bt601[0, 2]:.3f} (matches 0.114)")

# 2. RGB to HSV conversion from scratch
v = np.max(rgb_norm, axis=-1)
c_min = np.min(rgb_norm, axis=-1)
delta = v - c_min

# Saturation: delta / v (0 if v == 0)
s = np.zeros_like(v)
non_zero = v > 0
s[non_zero] = delta[non_zero] / v[non_zero]

# Hue: piecewise angle [0, 360)
h = np.zeros_like(v)
mask_r = (delta > 0) & (v == r)
mask_g = (delta > 0) & (v == g) & (~mask_r)
mask_b = (delta > 0) & (v == b) & (~mask_r) & (~mask_g)

h[mask_r] = 60.0 * (((g[mask_r] - b[mask_r]) / delta[mask_r]) % 6)
h[mask_g] = 60.0 * (((b[mask_g] - r[mask_g]) / delta[mask_g]) + 2)
h[mask_b] = 60.0 * (((r[mask_b] - g[mask_b]) / delta[mask_b]) + 4)

print("\nHSV Channels (Hue in degrees, Saturation [0,1], Value [0,1]):")
print(f"Red:    H={h[0,0]:5.1f} deg, S={s[0,0]:.2f}, V={v[0,0]:.2f}")
print(f"Green:  H={h[0,1]:5.1f} deg, S={s[0,1]:.2f}, V={v[0,1]:.2f}")
print(f"Blue:   H={h[0,2]:5.1f} deg, S={s[0,2]:.2f}, V={v[0,2]:.2f}")
print(f"Yellow: H={h[0,3]:5.1f} deg, S={s[0,3]:.2f}, V={v[0,3]:.2f}")
print(f"Cyan:   H={h[1,0]:5.1f} deg, S={s[1,0]:.2f}, V={v[1,0]:.2f}")
print(f"Grey:   H={h[1,3]:5.1f} deg, S={s[1,3]:.2f}, V={v[1,3]:.2f} (Saturation is zero)")

# 3. Layout transposition: HWC to CHW for PyTorch
tensor_chw = np.transpose(rgb_norm, (2, 0, 1))
print(f"\nNumPy layout shape (HWC):   {rgb_norm.shape}")
print(f"PyTorch layout shape (CHW): {tensor_chw.shape}")

Common pitfalls

  • OpenCV BGR versus RGB confusion: Forgetting that cv2.imread loads images in BGR format leads to reversed colour channels when passed to PyTorch or matplotlib, causing dramatic drops in inference accuracy.
  • Unintended integer overflow in uint8 arithmetic: In 8-bit unsigned integer arithmetic, $250 + 10 = 4$ due to modulo 256 wrap-around; always cast arrays to float32 before performing additions, subtractions, or filtering.
  • Calculating Euclidean distance across the angular Hue seam: In HSV space, pure red lies at both $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$. A naive Euclidean distance between $5^\circ$ and $355^\circ$ yields $350^\circ$ rather than the true angular distance of $10^\circ$.
  • Feeding unnormalised $[0, 255]$ integers into neural networks: Passing raw uint8 integers into neural layers causes exploding activations and saturated non-linearities; always scale to $[0.0, 1.0]$ and standardise channel statistics.
  • Assuming equal perceptual sensitivity across channels: Performing image processing tasks (such as edge filtering or thresholding) on unweighted channel averages ignores human luminous sensitivity, losing critical contrast detail in green and red regions.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does standard grayscale conversion weight green light much higher ($0.587$) than red ($0.299$) and blue ($0.114$)?

Why: Human eyes possess far more green-sensitive cones (M-cones) than blue (S-cones) or red (L-cones). The ITU-R BT.601 weights reflect this biological luminous efficiency, producing natural perceived brightness.

Q2What unintended visual defect occurs if an image loaded with OpenCV is displayed directly using a library expecting standard RGB order?

Why: OpenCV loads images in BGR format by default. If read without converting to RGB, channel 0 (blue) is interpreted as red, and channel 2 (red) is interpreted as blue.

Q3What primary advantage does the HSV colour space offer over RGB for computer vision tasks like colour-based object tracking?

Why: In RGB, changing illumination shifts all three channel values simultaneously. In HSV, illumination variations primarily alter Value, while Hue remains invariant, simplifying colour-based segmentation under variable lighting.

Q4What happens when two uint8 pixel values 200 and 70 are added together in standard C-style or NumPy unsigned 8-bit arithmetic?

Why: Unsigned 8-bit integers (uint8) span $0$ to $255$. Arithmetic overflow wraps around modulo 256: $(200 + 70) \pmod{256} = 14$, causing severe visual artifacts unless arrays are explicitly cast to floating-point types first.
