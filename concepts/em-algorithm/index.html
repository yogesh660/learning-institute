General iterative framework for finding maximum likelihood parameter estimates in probabilistic models with hidden latent variables or incomplete observations.

01 The idea

In many machine learning problems, the observed data depends on unobserved latent variables, such as unknown cluster memberships, hidden states, or missing sensor values. Directly maximising the marginal log-likelihood is intractable because the sum over latent variables appears inside the logarithm. The Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm circumvents this by alternating between estimating the posterior distribution over latent states (E-step) and maximising the expected complete-data log-likelihood (M-step), guaranteeing non-decreasing likelihood at every iteration.

Suppose you run a survey measuring heights, but the survey accidentally omits whether each participant is male or female. The pooled data forms a bimodal curve—a mixture of two separate normal distributions. If you knew everyone's sex, computing the average height and variance for each group would be trivial (standard maximum likelihood). Conversely, if you already knew the true male and female averages, you could easily calculate the probability that any given participant is male or female. The dilemma is that you know neither.

The EM algorithm resolves this chicken-and-egg deadlock through clever alternation. First, make an educated guess for the parameters (the group means and variances). In the Expectation step (E-step), use these guessed parameters to assign each participant a soft probabilistic responsibility—such as 85% chance female and 15% chance male for someone who is 162 cm tall.

Next, in the Maximisation step (M-step), use these soft weights to re-estimate the group parameters. Instead of a hard binary assignment, each data point contributes fractionally to updating the means and variances of both groups. These updated parameters yield a better model, which in turn produces more accurate responsibilities in the next E-step. Mathematically, the E-step constructs a tight lower bound (the Evidence Lower Bound, or ELBO) on the log-likelihood function, and the M-step climbs to the peak of that bound. Repeating this cycle guarantees steady progress until reaching a local maximum.

02 Step by step

  1. Initialise the model parameters $\theta^{(0)}$ randomly or using a fast heuristic (such as $k$-means for mixture model centroids).
  2. Perform the Expectation step (E-step): compute the posterior probability distribution over latent variables given current parameters: $q^{(t)}(Z) = p(Z \mid X, \theta^{(t)})$.
  3. Formulate the expected complete-data log-likelihood (the $Q$-function): $Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}) = \mathbb{E}_{Z \mid X, \theta^{(t)}} [\ln p(X, Z \mid \theta)]$.
  4. Perform the Maximisation step (M-step): update parameters by maximising the $Q$-function: $\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_\theta Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)})$.
  5. Evaluate the marginal log-likelihood $\ln p(X \mid \theta^{(t+1)})$ or check parameter convergence $\\|\theta^{(t+1)} - \theta^{(t)}\\| < \epsilon$.
  6. Repeat the E-step and M-step until the improvement in log-likelihood drops below the convergence tolerance.

03 The maths

  1. Marginal log-likelihood decomposition and ELBO

    $$\ln p(X \mid \theta) = \sum_{Z} q(Z) \ln \frac{p(X, Z \mid \theta)}{q(Z)} + \sum_{Z} q(Z) \ln \frac{q(Z)}{p(Z \mid X, \theta)} = \operatorname{ELBO}(q, \theta) + \operatorname{KL}\big(q(Z) \parallel p(Z \mid X, \theta)\big)$$

    By Jensen's inequality, the Kullback–Leibler divergence $\operatorname{KL} \ge 0$, establishing that $\operatorname{ELBO}(q, \theta) \le \ln p(X \mid \theta)$ is a rigorous lower bound. In the E-step, setting $q(Z) = p(Z \mid X, \theta^{(t)})$ makes $\operatorname{KL} = 0$, causing the lower bound to touch the true log-likelihood exactly at $\theta^{(t)}$.

  2. The Q-function in the E-step

    $$Q\big(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}\big) = \sum_{Z} p\big(Z \mid X, \theta^{(t)}\big) \ln p(X, Z \mid \theta)$$

    Evaluating the complete-data log-likelihood $\ln p(X, Z \mid \theta)$ moves the summation outside the logarithm. For Gaussian Mixture Models, this simplifies to computing soft responsibilities $\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}$.

  3. Parameter update in the M-step

    $$\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_{\theta} Q\big(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}\big)$$

    Differentiating $Q$ with respect to $\theta$ and setting to zero yields closed-form analytical updates for exponential family distributions: $\mu_k = \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik} x_i}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}$ and $\Sigma_k = \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)(x_i - \mu_k)^T}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Solves maximum likelihood estimation in models with unobserved latent variables or incomplete observations.
  • Guarantees monotonic non-decrease of the marginal log-likelihood at every iteration ($\ln p(X \mid \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \ln p(X \mid \theta^{(t)})$).
  • Tightens the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) in the E-step and climbs to its peak in the M-step.
  • Forms the mathematical foundation for Gaussian Mixture Models, Hidden Markov Models, and topic models.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# 1D two-component Gaussian mixture data
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
X = np.concatenate([rng.normal(-2.0, 0.8, 100), rng.normal(3.0, 1.2, 100)])

# Initial guesses for weights, means, and variances
pi = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
mu = np.array([-0.5, 0.5])
var = np.array([1.0, 1.0])

for _ in range(50):
    # E-step: calculate responsibilities (posterior over latent cluster)
    pdf = np.exp(-0.5 * (X[:, None] - mu) ** 2 / var) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)
    gamma = (pdf * pi) / np.sum(pdf * pi, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    
    # M-step: update parameters via weighted maximum likelihood
    N_k = np.sum(gamma, axis=0)
    pi = N_k / len(X)
    mu = np.sum(gamma * X[:, None], axis=0) / N_k
    var = np.sum(gamma * (X[:, None] - mu) ** 2, axis=0) / N_k

ll = np.sum(np.log(np.sum(pi * np.exp(-0.5 * (X[:, None] - mu) ** 2 / var) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var), axis=1)))
print(f"Estimated weights: {pi.round(3)}")
print(f"Estimated means: {mu.round(3)}")
print(f"Estimated stds: {np.sqrt(var).round(3)}")
print(f"Final log-likelihood: {ll:.2f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Convergence to local optima: The marginal log-likelihood surface is non-convex. EM is guaranteed to monotonically increase likelihood, but it can converge to suboptimal local maxima depending on initialisation. Multiple random restarts or $k$-means seeding are essential.
  • Singularities and variance collapse: In Gaussian mixture models, if a single component's mean collapses onto a single data point, its variance approaches zero and its likelihood explodes to infinity. Regularising the covariance diagonal with a small ridge ($\sigma^2 + \epsilon$) prevents numerical explosion.
  • Slow convergence near saddle points: EM has a first-order convergence rate, meaning progress can crawl when components overlap heavily or eigenvalues of the Fisher information matrix are near-zero.
  • Intractable E-step for complex priors: In deep probabilistic models or non-conjugate Bayesian networks, calculating exact posterior responsibilities $p(Z \mid X, \theta)$ is intractable, necessitating Variational EM or Monte Carlo approximations.

06 Check yourself

Q1What mathematical property guarantees that the EM algorithm's log-likelihood never decreases from one iteration to the next?

Why: Jensen's inequality guarantees that the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is a valid lower bound on the marginal log-likelihood. In the E-step, setting $q(Z) = p(Z \mid X, \theta)$ drives $\operatorname{KL} = 0$, and the M-step maximises the ELBO, ensuring the log-likelihood monotonically increases or stays constant.

Q2What occurs during the Expectation step (E-step) of the EM algorithm?

Why: The E-step calculates the conditional distribution of the hidden latent variables given the observed data and current parameters, computing the expected values (responsibilities) needed for the M-step.

Q3Why is directly maximising the marginal log-likelihood $\ln p(X \mid \theta) = \sum_i \ln \big(\sum_z p(x_i, z \mid \theta)\big)$ analytically difficult without EM?

Why: When a sum appears inside a logarithm, expanding the derivative yields coupled equations where parameters cannot be isolated into independent closed-form maximum likelihood solutions.

Q4What is a well-known failure mode when fitting Gaussian Mixture Models with EM without covariance regularisation?

Why: If a Gaussian component centres exactly on an isolated sample and captures only that point, its sample variance shrinks toward 0. The normal density $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \to \infty$, creating a numerical singularity.
