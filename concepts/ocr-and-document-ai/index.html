OCR and document AI

Optical Character Recognition (OCR) and Document AI transform unstructured raster document images into structured machine-readable text, tabular hierarchies, and semantic key-value records. Modern industrial Document AI pipelines operate across two tightly coupled stages: text detection (localising curved and multi-oriented text lines via segmentation-driven differentiable binarisation, such as DBNet and CRAFT) and text recognition (transcribing cropped image text strips into character sequences via Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks with Connectionist Temporal Classification (CRNN-CTC) or autoregressive Vision-Language decoders like TrOCR and Donut).

01 The idea

Every business, legal firm, hospital, and government agency operates on paper: invoices, tax forms, receipts, clinical prescriptions, and identity cards. Yet to a standard database or machine learning pipeline, a scanned PDF or photograph is merely a dead grid of pixels. Bridging the physical-to-digital divide requires more than simple character recognition: an intelligent document system must detect oriented text in cluttered backgrounds, transcribe multilingual typography and cursive handwriting, parse tabular layouts, and extract semantic relationships ("Total Due: £142.50").

The two-stage Document AI paradigm: Reading a document comprises two distinct sub-problems: (1) Text Detection—where is the text located on the page? and (2) Text Recognition—what characters do those text regions represent? While early systems attempted end-to-end character classification via sliding windows, contemporary pipelines decouple the tasks: a robust detection network locates arbitrarily oriented word or text-line polygons, crops and rectifies the text strips into horizontal patches, and passes them to a specialised sequence recognition engine.

Differentiable Binarisation for text detection (DBNet): Traditional segmentation-based text detectors generate probability heatmaps and apply a fixed threshold to extract bounding polygons. However, standard step-function thresholding is non-differentiable, preventing the binarisation step from being optimised end-to-end. DBNet (Differentiable Binarisation Network) solves this by reformulating thresholding as an adaptive, differentiable sigmoid approximation: $\hat{B}_{i, j} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-k(P_{i, j} - T_{i, j}))}$, where $P$ is the predicted probability map and $T$ is an adaptively learned spatial threshold map. This allows the network to jointly optimise text probability boundaries and edge thresholds directly with gradient descent.

Sequence transcription without alignment: CRNN and CTC: Text lines have variable lengths, and character boundaries in cursive handwriting or compressed fonts cannot be easily segmented into discrete letters. Baoguang Shi et al.'s Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN) processes an entire horizontal text strip through a CNN backbone, producing a sequence of feature vectors along the horizontal axis. A bidirectional LSTM models sequential linguistic context. To train the network without requiring tedious character-level bounding box annotations, Alex Graves's Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss introduces a special blank token ($\epsilon$). The CTC objective marginalises over all possible alignments that collapse to the ground-truth text string, allowing the model to learn character alignment and language spelling simultaneously.

The modern frontier: Vision-Language Document Transformers: While CRNN-CTC pipelines excel at isolated text-line transcription, complex documents (such as financial balance sheets or insurance claims) encode crucial meaning in 2D layout geometry and spatial typography. Modern Document AI models (such as LayoutLM, Donut, and TrOCR) integrate image pixel embeddings, 2D spatial bounding box coordinates, and pre-trained Transformer language models. By treating Document AI as a multi-modal sequence generation task, these architectures directly output structured JSON schemas ("vendor": "Acme Corp", "total": "£500") directly from raw document scans.

In everyday terms

Think of an administrative assistant processing incoming physical mail. Text detection is like the assistant picking up a yellow highlighter and drawing clean rectangular boxes around every printed paragraph, handwritten note, and invoice total on the invoice. Text recognition is like typing those highlighted snippets into a word processor letter-by-letter. CRNN with CTC loss acts like a court stenographer listening to continuous spoken speech: the stenographer does not need a pause between every individual phoneme; they transcribe a continuous rhythmic stream of sound, automatically collapsing repeated sounds and omitting silent pauses to form clean words. Vision-Language Document AI is like an experienced accountant reading the whole page: they do not just read words in isolation, but immediately recognise that a number positioned in the bottom-right corner beneath a bold double-line represents the grand total amount payable.

03 The maths

  1. Differentiable Binarisation (DBNet) formulation

    \hat{B}_{i, j} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-k(P_{i, j} - T_{i, j})\right)}

    Here $P_{i, j}$ is the predicted text probability at coordinate $(i, j)$, $T_{i, j}$ is the adaptively learned threshold map, and $k$ is an amplification factor (typically $k=50$). This smooth, differentiable approximation of the standard Heavyside step function allows end-to-end backpropagation through the binarisation operation.

  2. Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) conditional probability

    p(\mathbf{l} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{l})} p(\pi \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{l})} \prod_{t=1}^T y_{\pi_t}^t

    Here $\mathbf{l}$ is the target text label, $\pi$ is an alignment path across $T$ time steps, $y_{\pi_t}^t$ is the emission probability of label $\pi_t$ at time step $t$, and $\mathcal{B}$ is the collapse mapping that removes sequential duplicate characters and strips out blank tokens $\epsilon$ (e.g. $\mathcal{B}(\text{c-a-a-t}) = \text{cat}$).

  3. Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER)

    \text{CER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N_{\text{ref}}} = \frac{\operatorname{Lev}(\mathbf{l}_{\text{pred}}, \mathbf{l}_{\text{ref}})}{|\mathbf{l}_{\text{ref}}|}, \quad \text{WER} = \frac{S_w + D_w + I_w}{N_{\text{words}}}

    Here $S$ is substitutions, $D$ is deletions, $I$ is insertions, and $N_{\text{ref}}$ is the total number of characters in the ground-truth reference string. CER evaluates character-level Levenshtein edit distance, providing a granular benchmark of OCR transcription accuracy.

04 Key takeaways

  • Document AI pipelines operate as a two-stage process: text detection (where is text?) followed by text recognition (what is written?).
  • DBNet enables end-to-end backpropagation for text detection by approximating hard step-function binarisation with a differentiable sigmoid.
  • CRNN combines convolutional spatial feature extraction with bidirectional LSTMs to transcribe variable-length horizontal text strips.
  • CTC loss enables training text recognition networks on full words without requiring costly character-level bounding box annotations.
  • Modern multi-modal Document Transformers (LayoutLM, Donut) combine 2D spatial layouts, text semantics, and visual cues to extract structured key-value entities.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Simulate Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) Greedy Best-Path Decoding
# Character vocabulary: index 0 is blank token ('-'), indices 1-4 are letters ['c', 'a', 't', 'o']
vocab = ['-', 'c', 'a', 't', 'o']
blank_idx = 0

# Simulated emission logits from a CRNN over T=8 time steps for vocabulary size 5
# Target ground-truth word: "cat"
np.random.seed(42)
T = 8
num_classes = len(vocab)
logits = np.random.randn(T, num_classes).astype(np.float32)

# Bias logits to strongly emit sequence: 'c', 'c', '-', 'a', 'a', 'a', '-', 't'
# Which should collapse to: "cat"
logits[0, 1] += 5.0  # 'c'
logits[1, 1] += 4.5  # 'c'
logits[2, 0] += 5.0  # '-' (blank)
logits[3, 2] += 4.0  # 'a'
logits[4, 2] += 5.5  # 'a'
logits[5, 2] += 4.2  # 'a'
logits[6, 0] += 4.8  # '-' (blank)
logits[7, 3] += 5.2  # 't'

# 1. Softmax probability computation over time steps
exp_logits = np.exp(logits - np.max(logits, axis=-1, keepdims=True))
probs = exp_logits / np.sum(exp_logits, axis=-1, keepdims=True)

# 2. Greedy best-path decoding: argmax at each time step
raw_path_indices = np.argmax(probs, axis=-1)
raw_path_chars = [vocab[idx] for idx in raw_path_indices]

print(f"Raw frame-level emission path (T={T}):")
print(f"Indices: {raw_path_indices.tolist()}")
print(f"Tokens:  {' '.join(raw_path_chars)}")

# 3. CTC Collapse Function B(pi):
# Step A: Collapse consecutive identical characters
collapsed_consecutive = []
for i, token in enumerate(raw_path_indices):
    if i == 0 or token != raw_path_indices[i - 1]:
        collapsed_consecutive.append(token)

# Step B: Remove blank tokens (index 0)
final_indices = [idx for idx in collapsed_consecutive if idx != blank_idx]
final_transcription = "".join([vocab[idx] for idx in final_indices])

print(f"\nAfter collapsing consecutive duplicates: {[vocab[i] for i in collapsed_consecutive]}")
print(f"Final CTC decoded transcription:        '{final_transcription}'")

# 4. Character Error Rate (CER) calculation
def levenshtein_distance(s1, s2):
    m, n = len(s1), len(s2)
    dp = np.zeros((m + 1, n + 1), dtype=int)
    for i in range(m + 1): dp[i, 0] = i
    for j in range(n + 1): dp[0, j] = j
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for j in range(1, n + 1):
            cost = 0 if s1[i-1] == s2[j-1] else 1
            dp[i, j] = min(dp[i-1, j] + 1, dp[i, j-1] + 1, dp[i-1, j-1] + cost)
    return dp[m, n]

ground_truth = "cat"
edit_dist = levenshtein_distance(final_transcription, ground_truth)
cer = edit_dist / len(ground_truth)
print(f"Ground-truth text:                     '{ground_truth}'")
print(f"Levenshtein edit distance:             {edit_dist}")
print(f"Character Error Rate (CER):            {cer:.1%}")
assert final_transcription == ground_truth, "CTC decoding mismatch!"
print("Verified: CTC greedy best-path decoding executed successfully!")

Common pitfalls

  • Running OCR on unrectified skewed documents: Passing text rotated by even $5^\circ$ to $10^\circ$ causes horizontal CRNN feature sequences to slice across multiple text lines simultaneously, producing garbled transcription outputs; always deskew pages beforehand.
  • Ignoring the CTC blank token: Omitting the blank token ($\epsilon$) prevents CTC from transcribing words with legitimate consecutive duplicate letters (e.g. distinguishing 'book' from 'bok').
  • Evaluating OCR solely with Word Error Rate (WER): In specialized technical domains (such as serial numbers or chemistry formulas), a single character typo in a 20-character identifier marks the entire word as an error; Character Error Rate (CER) provides a much fairer metric.
  • Assuming linear reading order in multi-column layouts: Reading multi-column documents strictly top-to-bottom across the full page width merges text across columns; layout analysis must partition the page into columns before extracting reading order.
  • Relying on classical rule-based regex parsing for key-value extraction: Using static regex to parse invoices fails when document templates change; multi-modal Document Transformers generalise across unseen vendor invoice layouts.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) incorporate a dedicated "blank" token ($\epsilon$) in its character vocabulary?

Why: Without a blank token, consecutive identical characters would always collapse into a single character ('o-o' would become 'o'). Inserting an intermediate blank token ('o-\epsilon-o') signals that the two letters are distinct instances.

Q2How does DBNet (Differentiable Binarisation Network) make the text detection binarisation step trainable end-to-end with gradient descent?

Why: Standard hard thresholding ($P \ge T$) has zero gradient everywhere. DBNet approximates thresholding using a steep sigmoid function $\hat{B} = 1 / (1 + e^{-k(P-T)})$, enabling gradients to flow backward through the threshold map and probability map simultaneously.

Q3Why is Character Error Rate (CER) often preferred over Word Error Rate (WER) when evaluating OCR on technical documents (such as serial numbers or clinical dosages)?

Why: WER is an all-or-nothing metric: a 15-character serial number with one misplaced letter receives a $100\%$ word error. CER measures character-level Levenshtein edit distance, providing a granular and fair assessment of recognition fidelity.

Q4What advantage do modern multi-modal Document Transformers (such as LayoutLM) have over traditional OCR pipelines when extracting key-value pairs from receipts?

Why: In structured documents, visual layout conveys meaning (e.g. an address is always located in the header). Multi-modal transformers encode 2D bounding box positions along with token embeddings, generalising across diverse layouts where static rules fail.
