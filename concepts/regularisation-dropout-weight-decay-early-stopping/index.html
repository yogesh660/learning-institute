Regularisation: Dropout, Weight Decay, and Early Stopping

Overparameterised neural networks have enough capacity to memorise training noise verbatim. Regularisation bounds model complexity through stochastic feature dropout, weight shrinkage penalties, and validation-based early stopping.

01 The idea

Modern deep neural networks often contain far more parameters than training observations, granting them the capacity to achieve zero training error even on completely randomised labels. To ensure high test-time generalisation, practitioners combine three complementary regularisation pillars: weight decay to shrink parameter norms, inverted dropout to break co-adaptations between neurons, and early stopping to halt optimisation before noise memorisation begins.

Consider a dense neural network where two neurons in layer 2 always activate together to compensate for each other's errors. This co-adaptation creates fragile internal representations: if test data exhibits a slightly different distribution, both neurons misfire. Dropout shatters co-adaptations by randomly deactivating a subset of neurons (with probability $p$, typically $0.1$ to $0.5$) during each training forward pass. Because no neuron can rely on the presence of any other specific neuron, each is forced to learn robust, self-sufficient features. Mathematically, dropout acts as an implicit ensemble over $2^N$ possible thinned subnetworks.

Weight Decay adds an $L_2$ penalty on parameter magnitudes, directly shrinking weights toward zero at every optimisation step. Keeping weights small ensures that small perturbations in input features cannot trigger massive, uncontrolled swings in downstream activations, effectively bounding the model's curvature. In adaptive optimisers such as AdamW, this shrinkage must be decoupled from gradient scaling to prevent parameters with historically large gradients from escaping regularisation.

Early Stopping exploits the learning dynamics of gradient descent. When training deep networks, optimisers typically capture broad, low-frequency patterns first before memorising high-frequency sample quirks and labelling errors. By continuously evaluating loss on a separate validation split and stopping training when validation loss stops improving (after a patience window, such as 5 epochs), early stopping acts as a non-parametric complexity control, preserving the checkpoint with the highest true generalisation power.

In everyday terms

Like training a robust disaster response team: weight decay ensures no single specialist hoards all responsibility, dropout ensures team members can function when colleagues are absent, and early stopping ends drills when team peak performance is reached before exhaustion induces bad habits.

02 Step by step

  1. Partition a validation holdout: reserve 10% to 20% of training data (or establish cross-validation splits) to monitor generalisation independently of the optimiser's loss.
  2. Apply decoupled weight decay: set decay coefficient $\lambda$ (commonly $0.01$ to $0.1$ in AdamW) across 2D/3D weight matrices, while excluding 1D bias vectors and normalisation scales.
  3. Insert inverted dropout layers: position dropout modules ($p \in [0.1, 0.5]$) after activations or attention projections, scaling active units by $1 / (1-p)$ during training.
  4. Toggle evaluation mode: invoke `model.eval()` during inference to disable dropout, allowing all neurons to contribute deterministically without test-time scaling.
  5. Configure early stopping monitor: track validation loss with a patience threshold $P$ (e.g. 5 epochs), automatically rolling back to the best checkpoint when patience is exhausted.

03 The maths

  1. Decoupled weight decay parameter update

    $$w_t = (1 - \eta \lambda) w_{t-1} - \eta \Delta w_t$$

    $w_t$ is the weight vector at iteration $t$, $\eta$ is the learning rate, $\lambda$ is the weight decay coefficient, and $\Delta w_t$ is the gradient update direction (either the standard gradient or Adam's preconditioned gradient). The factor $(1 - \eta \lambda)$ shrinks the parameter toward zero at every step independently of gradient magnitudes.

  2. Inverted Dropout forward transform

    $$\tilde{h}_i = \frac{m_i}{1 - p} \cdot h_i, \qquad m_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(1 - p)$$

    $h_i$ is the original pre-dropout activation, $p$ is the dropout drop probability ($1-p$ is the survival probability), and $m_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is a random binary mask. Multiplying by the inverted scale factor $\frac{1}{1-p}$ ensures $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{h}_i] = h_i$, meaning expected activation magnitudes are preserved and test-time evaluation requires no post-scaling.

  3. Early stopping decision criterion

    $$\text{Halt if } t - \arg\min_{k \le t} \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(k) \ge P$$

    $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(k)$ denotes validation loss evaluated at epoch $k$, and $P$ is the patience window. Training terminates when the number of epochs elapsed since the historical minimum validation loss reaches or exceeds $P$, preventing late-stage overfitting on idiosyncratic training artifacts.

04 Key takeaways

  • Weight decay penalises large parameter norms, smoothing the model's decision boundaries and bounding sensitivity to input noise.
  • Inverted dropout trains an exponential ensemble of sub-architectures by randomly silencing units during forward passes, preventing co-adaptation.
  • Early stopping serves as a compute-efficient regulariser that halts training before the network expends capacity memorising sample-specific label noise.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# 1. Inverted Dropout: preserve expectation across 10,000 activations
np.random.seed(42)
h = np.ones(10000) * 4.0
p_drop = 0.25
mask = (np.random.rand(10000) >= p_drop).astype(float)
h_drop = (h * mask) / (1.0 - p_drop)

print("Original activation mean: ", round(float(np.mean(h)), 2))
print("Inverted dropout mean:   ", round(float(np.mean(h_drop)), 2), "(expectation preserved)")

# 2. Decoupled weight decay step
w = 2.0
lr, wd = 0.1, 0.05
w_decayed = w * (1.0 - lr * wd)
print(f"Weight after decay step (lr={lr}, wd={wd}): {w_decayed:.4f}")

# 3. Early stopping loop with patience = 2
val_losses = [1.20, 0.90, 0.70, 0.72, 0.75, 0.80]
patience, best_loss, counter, stop_epoch = 2, float('inf'), 0, -1

for epoch, loss in enumerate(val_losses):
    if loss < best_loss:
        best_loss = loss
        counter = 0
    else:
        counter += 1
        if counter >= patience:
            stop_epoch = epoch
            break

print(f"Early stopping triggered at epoch {stop_epoch} (restoring best loss {best_loss:.2f})")

Common pitfalls

  • Leaving dropout active during inference, which introduces unwanted stochastic randomness and degraded deterministic test accuracy.
  • Applying weight decay to bias parameters and normalisation scales ($\gamma, \beta$), which underfits the model by unnecessarily constraining affine shifts.
  • Using $L_2$ regularisation inside Adam instead of decoupled weight decay (AdamW), where adaptive gradient denominators distort the intended shrinkage rate.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does inverted dropout multiply surviving activations by $1 / (1 - p)$ during training?

Why: Because each neuron has probability $1-p$ of surviving, the expected sum of activations drops by a factor of $1-p$. Pre-scaling by $1/(1-p)$ ensures $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{h}] = h$, meaning the model can be used directly at test time without any rescaling.

Q2Why should weight decay typically be applied to weight matrices but excluded from bias vectors and normalisation scales?

Why: Biases and normalisation parameters represent a tiny fraction of total model capacity. Penalising them forces the model's activations toward an arbitrary zero baseline, risking underfitting without improving overfitting protection.

Q3What is the primary mechanism through which dropout prevents overfitting in deep neural networks?

Why: Dropout ensures that no neuron can rely on a specific partner neuron to fix its errors. Neurons must develop standalone, generalisable features, functioning like an implicit ensemble over vast sub-networks.

Q4When implementing early stopping, what parameter state should be deployed to production?

Why: Early stopping continues past the optimum to confirm that validation loss has truly stopped improving. Once patience is exhausted, the training process must restore the checkpoint corresponding to the minimum validation loss.
