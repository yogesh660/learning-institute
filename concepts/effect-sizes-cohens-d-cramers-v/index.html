Effect Sizes: Cohen's d, Cramér's V

Quantify the practical magnitude of experimental findings beyond mere p-values using standardised metrics for continuous and categorical differences.

01 The idea

A tiny p-value tells you an effect is unlikely to be pure noise, but says nothing about whether it actually matters. Effect size metrics decouple statistical significance from sample size, quantifying the real-world strength of relationships on standardised scales. Cohen's d, Hedges' g, and Cramér's V provide universal, scale-free benchmarks for comparing continuous mean shifts and categorical associations across experiments.

Suppose an online education platform evaluates a new tutorial on test scores. In a massive sample of 100,000 students, the new tutorial improves the average score from 75.00 to 75.15 points (a 0.15 point lift out of 100), with a pooled standard deviation $s = 15.0$ points. Because the sample size is enormous ($N = 100,000$), the standard error is $\approx 0.067$, producing $z = 2.24$ and $p = 0.025$. A naive team might announce a "statistically significant breakthrough".

However, dividing the 0.15 point difference by the standard deviation of 15.0 yields a Cohen's $d$ of just $0.01$—a minuscule shift of one-hundredth of a standard deviation. Over 99% of student scores overlap completely between the two cohorts. The effect is practically indistinguishable from zero. Conversely, a tiny pilot study of 20 users might show a massive 12-point jump ($d = 0.80$), but have $p = 0.08$ due to low sample size.

Effect sizes protect teams from both traps. For continuous two-group comparisons, Cohen's $d$ expresses the difference in terms of pooled standard deviations ($0.2$ is small, $0.5$ is medium, $0.8$ is large). Hedges' $g$ applies an exact small-sample correction. For categorical contingency tables, Cramér's $V$ bounds association between 0 (complete independence) and 1 (perfect correlation), converting unscaled chi-square statistics into interpretable association strengths.

In everyday terms

A p-value is like a telescope telling you whether a celestial body exists; the effect size is the measurement that tells you whether it is a pebble or a planet.

02 Step by step

  1. Determine the data type: continuous measurements between two groups (Cohen's $d$ or Hedges' $g$) or categorical counts in a contingency table (Cramér's $V$).
  2. For continuous variables, compute the mean difference $\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2$ and the pooled standard deviation $s_{\text{pooled}}$.
  3. Divide the mean difference by $s_{\text{pooled}}$ to obtain Cohen's $d$; if $n < 20$, multiply by the Hedges correction factor $J \approx 1 - \frac{3}{4(n_1 + n_2) - 9}$.
  4. For categorical contingency tables, compute the Pearson chi-square statistic $\chi^2$ and total sample size $N$.
  5. Compute Cramér's $V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N \min(R - 1, C - 1)}}$.
  6. Interpret the magnitude against established domain standards rather than treating p-values as proxies for impact.

03 The maths

  1. Cohen's d and Pooled Standard Deviation

    $$d = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{s_{\text{pooled}}}, \qquad s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}}$$

    Here $\bar{X}_1, \bar{X}_2$ are group sample means, $s_1^2, s_2^2$ are sample variances, and $n_1, n_2$ are sample sizes. The pooled standard deviation $s_{\text{pooled}}$ weights variances by their degrees of freedom, standardising the mean difference into units of population standard deviations.

  2. Hedges' g Small-Sample Correction

    $$g = d \cdot \left(1 - \frac{3}{4(n_1 + n_2) - 9}\right)$$

    Cohen's $d$ exhibits a slight positive bias when estimated from small sample sizes ($n_1 + n_2 < 30$). Larry Hedges introduced this exact scaling factor to produce an unbiased estimator $g$, which converges to $d$ as sample sizes increase.

  3. Cramér's V for Categorical Association

    $$V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N \cdot \min(R - 1, C - 1)}}$$

    Here $\chi^2$ is Pearson's chi-square test statistic, $N$ is total observations, and $R$ and $C$ are the row and column dimensions of the contingency table. Dividing by $N \cdot \min(R - 1, C - 1)$ normalises the metric into the interval $[0, 1]$, where 0 indicates statistical independence and 1 indicates perfect complete association.

04 Key takeaways

  • Effect sizes measure the substantive magnitude of a difference or relationship completely independent of sample size.
  • A finding can achieve statistical significance ($p < 0.05$) while remaining practically negligible ($d < 0.05$), or carry huge real-world impact ($d > 0.80$) while lacking significance in a small study.
  • Jacob Cohen's classic thresholds ($0.2$ small, $0.5$ medium, $0.8$ large) provide general heuristics, but must always be interpreted in domain-specific context.
  • Reporting effect sizes with confidence intervals gives readers both the magnitude of an effect and the precision of its estimation.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# Continuous comparison: tutorial vs control test scores
scores_tut = rng.normal(loc=78.5, scale=12.0, size=25)
scores_ctrl = rng.normal(loc=71.2, scale=11.5, size=25)
n1, n2 = len(scores_tut), len(scores_ctrl)
s1, s2 = np.std(scores_tut, ddof=1), np.std(scores_ctrl, ddof=1)
s_pooled = np.sqrt(((n1 - 1) * s1**2 + (n2 - 1) * s2**2) / (n1 + n2 - 2))

cohens_d = (np.mean(scores_tut) - np.mean(scores_ctrl)) / s_pooled
hedges_g = cohens_d * (1.0 - 3.0 / (4.0 * (n1 + n2) - 9.0))

# Categorical association: 2x3 contingency table
table = np.array([[120, 80, 50], [180, 50, 20]])
chi2_val, _, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(table)
cramers_v = np.sqrt(chi2_val / (np.sum(table) * (min(table.shape) - 1)))

print(f"Mean difference: {np.mean(scores_tut) - np.mean(scores_ctrl):.2f}")
print(f"Cohen's d:       {cohens_d:.3f} (medium effect)")
print(f"Hedges' g:       {hedges_g:.3f} (bias-corrected)")
print(f"Cramér's V:      {cramers_v:.3f} (moderate association)")

Common pitfalls

  • Equating a small p-value with a large effect size: with huge datasets, trivial noise differences yield $p < 0.0001$.
  • Using unpooled standard deviations when computing Cohen's $d$ on groups with unequal variances; use Glass's delta or Welch-standardised metrics instead.
  • Ignoring Hedges' small-sample correction ($g$) when reporting results from pilot studies with fewer than 20 observations per group.
  • Comparing raw Cramér's V values across tables of different dimensions without accounting for the degrees of freedom scaling factor.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is it dangerous to rely exclusively on p-values without reporting effect sizes?

Why: The p-value conflates effect size and sample size: with large enough $N$, even an imperceptible difference of 0.01 standard deviations will produce a tiny p-value.

Q2Under Jacob Cohen's conventional benchmarks for continuous mean comparisons ($d$), what value represents a "medium" effect size?

Why: Cohen's widely adopted guidelines define $d = 0.2$ as small, $d = 0.5$ as medium, and $d = 0.8$ as large.

Q3When should an analyst report Hedges' $g$ instead of Cohen's $d$?

Why: Cohen's $d$ slightly overestimates the true effect size in small samples. Hedges' $g$ applies an exact correction factor that removes this small-sample bias.

Q4What range of values can Cramér's $V$ take in an $R \times C$ contingency table?

Why: Cramér's $V$ is normalised by dividing by $N \cdot \min(R - 1, C - 1)$, constraining the metric strictly to $[0, 1]$, where 0 reflects independence and 1 reflects complete association.
