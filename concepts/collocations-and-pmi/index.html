Collocations and Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)

Collocations (e.g. "heavy rain", "pay attention", "machine learning") are multi-word expressions with non-random semantic cohesion. Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) measures the log ratio of joint co-occurrence probability against independent marginal probabilities. Positive PMI (PPMI) truncates negative values to zero, mitigating noise and uninformative negative correlations for low-frequency events. Statistical significance filtering using t-tests or Pearson's chi-squared test prevents rare pairs from dominating PMI rankings.

01 The idea

Collocations are habitual word co-occurrences whose combined meaning exceeds or differs from the sum of their individual parts. Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) and statistical hypothesis tests quantify whether words appear together significantly more often than expected by random chance, forming the mathematical foundation for phrase extraction, idiom detection, and distributional embeddings.

If you see the word "heavy", what words follow? You often say "heavy rain" or "heavy smoker", but rarely "heavy wind" or "heavy worker" (we say "strong wind" and "hard worker" instead). Grammatically, all these pairings are valid adjective-noun combinations, yet native speakers naturally favour specific pairings. These established pairings are called collocations. If two words are completely independent, the probability of seeing them together $P(x, y)$ equals the product of their individual occurrences $P(x)P(y)$. Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) mathematically measures how many times more often words co-occur compared to pure independent chance. A high PMI indicates strong linguistic affinity, whereas a PMI near zero indicates random coincidence.

In everyday terms

Imagine monitoring people entering a coffee shop. If two patrons frequently walk through the door within seconds of each other far more often than their individual visit rates would suggest, you conclude they are friends or colleagues who travel together, rather than two unrelated strangers who happened to arrive at the exact same moment.

02 Step by step

  1. Define a sliding context window of size $k$ across the tokenised corpus (or treat immediate bigram sequences as candidate pairs).
  2. Compute unigram counts $C(w)$ for all vocabulary words and total token count $N$.
  3. Compute co-occurrence counts $C(u, v)$ for all word pairs appearing within the context window.
  4. Filter rare candidates: discard pairs with co-occurrence counts below a minimum frequency threshold (e.g. $C(u, v) < 5$).
  5. Calculate PMI and PPMI values for all surviving candidate pairs.
  6. Rank pairs by PPMI score to extract meaningful collocations, multi-word terminology, or construct term-context association matrices.

04 Key takeaways

  • Collocations represent multi-word phrases whose co-occurrence reflects semantic and idiomatic association beyond random syntactic assembly.
  • PMI contrasts empirical joint occurrence with independent marginal probability, identifying statistically cohesive expressions.
  • Clipping to PPMI and applying smoothing exponents (such as $\alpha = 0.75$) mitigates frequency bias towards rare vocabulary items.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

def extract_collocations(corpus, window_size=2, min_count=2):
    """Computes PPMI scores for word pairs in a sliding window."""
    tokens = [t.lower() for doc in corpus for t in doc.split()]
    N = len(tokens)
    
    unigram_counts = Counter(tokens)
    cooccur_counts = Counter()
    
    # 1. Tally co-occurrences within sliding window
    for i in range(len(tokens)):
        target = tokens[i]
        start = max(0, i - window_size)
        end = min(len(tokens), i + window_size + 1)
        for j in range(start, end):
            if i != j:
                context = tokens[j]
                cooccur_counts[(target, context)] += 1
                
    # 2. Compute PPMI
    ppmi_scores = {}
    for (w1, w2), count in cooccur_counts.items():
        if count < min_count:
            continue
        # Joint and marginal probabilities
        p_joint = count / (N * 2 * window_size)
        p_w1 = unigram_counts[w1] / N
        p_w2 = unigram_counts[w2] / N
        
        pmi = np.log2(p_joint / (p_w1 * p_w2))
        ppmi = max(0.0, pmi)
        ppmi_scores[(w1, w2)] = (ppmi, count)
        
    return ppmi_scores

# Synthetic domain corpus
corpus = [
    "natural language processing models natural language understanding",
    "deep learning and natural language processing techniques",
    "machine learning models process natural language text",
    "deep learning algorithms power modern language processing"
]

results = extract_collocations(corpus, window_size=2, min_count=2)
# Sort by PPMI descending
sorted_collocations = sorted(results.items(), key=lambda x: x[1][0], reverse=True)

print("Top Discovered Collocations by PPMI:")
for (w1, w2), (score, count) in sorted_collocations[:6]:
    print(f"  ({w1:8}, {w2:10}) -> PPMI: {score:.3f} (Count: {count})")

Common pitfalls

  • Applying unregularised PMI to small corpora without frequency thresholds; rare words appearing together once can achieve artificially massive PMI scores.
  • Treating PMI as an absolute probability metric rather than a scale-sensitive relative co-occurrence ratio.
  • Confusing grammatical constituency with collocation; non-collocated common stopwords like "of the" have high co-occurrence counts but low PMI because their marginal frequencies are enormous.

06 Check yourself

Q1What does a Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) value of zero signify between two words?

Why: When $P(x, y) = P(x)P(y)$, their ratio is 1, and $\log_2(1) = 0$. This indicates that co-occurrence occurs at random baseline chance.

Q2Why does standard PMI tend to over-rank rare word pairings over common phrases?

Why: If two rare words occur only once in the entire corpus, but happen to appear in the same window, $P(x,y) = 1/N$ while $P(x)P(y) = 1/N^2$, producing an inflated PMI of $\log_2(N)$.

Q3Why is Positive PMI (PPMI) used instead of raw PMI when building term-context embedding matrices?

Why: Negative PMI indicates that two words occur together less often than chance, which is noisy and difficult to estimate accurately on finite text corpora. Clipping to zero stabilizes downstream representations.
