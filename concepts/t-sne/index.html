01 The idea

Linear projections like PCA inevitably collapse curved or clustered non-linear manifolds because they prioritise preserving large pairwise distances over local neighbourhood relationships. t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding (t-SNE) converts pairwise distances into probabilities of neighbourhood co-occurrence, matching high-dimensional Gaussian affinities with heavy-tailed low-dimensional Student-t distributions to resolve the crowding problem and reveal clustered structures.

In high-dimensional spaces, geometry behaves counter-intuitively: the volume of a sphere grows exponentially with dimension $d$. Points distributed uniformly across a high-dimensional manifold have ample room to stay mutually equidistant from their neighbours. When attempting to project such data onto a two-dimensional sheet, geometry encounters the crowding problem: there simply is not enough area in 2D to accommodate all the moderate-distance neighbours without crushing distinct clusters on top of each other.

t-SNE solves this crowding problem through an elegant distribution mismatch. In the original high-dimensional space, pairwise proximity is modelled using a Gaussian distribution, which decays exponentially fast ($e^{-d^2}$). In the low-dimensional embedding space, t-SNE models proximity using a heavy-tailed Student-t distribution with 1 degree of freedom (the Cauchy distribution, decaying as $(1 + d^2)^{-1}$). Because the Student-t distribution has much heavier tails than the Gaussian, moderate-distance points must be pushed much further apart in 2D to yield the same probability match. This creates repulsive breathing room, allowing distinct clusters to separate cleanly.

Instead of preserving rigid metric distances, t-SNE preserves neighbourhood topology. The perplexity parameter specifies the effective number of nearest neighbours each point considers when establishing its local bandwidth $\sigma_i$. Dense regions automatically adopt a narrow $\sigma_i$ while sparse regions broaden $\sigma_i$. The algorithm then minimises the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between high-dimensional affinities $P$ and low-dimensional affinities $Q$ using gradient descent. Points that are close neighbours in high dimensions exert strong attractive forces, while low-dimensional normalisation creates global repulsion.

In everyday terms

Unravelling a tangled ball of wool onto a flat tabletop: short threads between neighbouring fibres act as elastic bands pulling nearby loops together, while an overall outward pressure forces separate skeins of yarn to unfurl into isolated clusters.

02 Step by step

  1. Calculate pairwise Euclidean distances $\|x_i - x_j\|$ between all sample pairs in the original high-dimensional feature space.
  2. For each data point $x_i$, perform binary search to determine a unique Gaussian bandwidth $\sigma_i$ such that the Shannon entropy of its conditional probability distribution matches the user-defined perplexity: $\operatorname{Perp}(P_i) = 2^{H(P_i)}$.
  3. Compute asymmetric conditional probabilities $p_{j|i}$ and symmetrise them into joint probabilities $p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}}{2n}$, setting diagonal self-affinities $p_{ii} = 0$.
  4. Initialise low-dimensional coordinates $y_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ randomly from a Gaussian distribution with small variance (or using the first two PCA components for stable global layout).
  5. Compute low-dimensional affinities $q_{ij}$ using the Cauchy / Student-t kernel $(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}$, normalised over all pairs.
  6. Compute the analytical gradient of the KL divergence $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y_i} = 4 \sum_j (p_{ij} - q_{ij})(y_i - y_j)(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}$.
  7. Update coordinates $y_i$ via gradient descent with momentum and early exaggeration (multiplying high-dimensional probabilities $p_{ij}$ by a factor of 4 to 12 during initial iterations to encourage compact cluster formation).

03 The maths

  1. High-dimensional conditional probabilities and perplexity

    $$p_{j \mid i} = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\|x_i - x_j\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right)}{\sum_{k \ne i} \exp\left(-\frac{\|x_i - x_k\|^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right)}, \qquad \operatorname{Perp}(P_i) = 2^{-\sum_{j} p_{j \mid i} \log_2 p_{j \mid i}}$$

    The variance $\sigma_i^2$ is determined independently for each sample via binary search so that the perplexity matches the user specification (typically between 5 and 50). This adapts to varying local data density.

  2. Symmetrisation and low-dimensional Student-t kernel

    $$p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j \mid i} + p_{i \mid j}}{2n}, \qquad q_{ij} = \frac{\left(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{k} \sum_{l \ne k} \left(1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2\right)^{-1}}$$

    Symmetrisation ensures that outliers (which have tiny $p_{j|i}$ for all $j$) still exert meaningful gradients. The low-dimensional Cauchy distribution decays as the inverse square of distance, preventing crowding.

  3. Cost function and analytical gradient

    $$\mathcal{L}_{KL} = \sum_{i} \sum_{j \ne i} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y_i} = 4 \sum_{j} (p_{ij} - q_{ij})(y_i - y_j)\left(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2\right)^{-1}$$

    The asymmetry of KL divergence heavily penalises false negatives (large $p_{ij}$ modelled by small $q_{ij}$), which pulls high-dimensional neighbours together. False positives (small $p_{ij}$ with moderate $q_{ij}$) receive very mild penalties, allowing distant clusters to position themselves freely.

04 Key takeaways

  • Replaces metric distance preservation with probability distribution matching via Kullback-Leibler divergence.
  • Heavy-tailed Student-t kernel in low-dimensional space resolves the high-dimensional crowding problem.
  • Adaptive Gaussian bandwidth $\sigma_i$ dynamically scales to match local density via the perplexity hyperparameter.
  • Designed primarily as an exploratory visualisation technique rather than a feature extraction pipeline for downstream models.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Synthetic dataset: two well-separated clusters in 4D space
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
X = np.vstack([rng.normal(-3, 0.5, (20, 4)), rng.normal(3, 0.5, (20, 4))])
n = len(X)

# 1. High-dimensional pairwise Gaussian affinities and symmetrisation
D2 = np.sum((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2, axis=-1)
np.fill_diagonal(D2, np.inf)
P_cond = np.exp(-D2 / 2.0)
P_cond = P_cond / P_cond.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
P = np.maximum((P_cond + P_cond.T) / (2 * n), 1e-12)

# 2. Initialise low-dimensional embedding coordinates
Y = rng.normal(0, 1e-4, (n, 2))
lr, n_iter = 50.0, 120

# 3. Minimise KL divergence via gradient descent
for _ in range(n_iter):
    dist_y = np.sum((Y[:, None, :] - Y[None, :, :]) ** 2, axis=-1)
    inv = 1.0 / (1.0 + dist_y)
    np.fill_diagonal(inv, 0.0)
    Q = np.maximum(inv / np.sum(inv), 1e-12)
    grad = 4 * np.sum((P - Q)[:, :, None] * inv[:, :, None] * (Y[:, None, :] - Y[None, :, :]), axis=1)
    Y -= lr * grad

kl_div = np.sum(P * np.log(P / Q))
print(f"Final KL divergence: {kl_div:.4f}")
print(f"Cluster 1 centre in 2D: {Y[:20].mean(axis=0).round(2)}")
print(f"Cluster 2 centre in 2D: {Y[20:].mean(axis=0).round(2)}")

Common pitfalls

  • Inter-cluster distances are meaningless: t-SNE prioritises local neighbourhood preservation; the distance between two distant cluster centres in the 2D plot does not reflect true metric separation in high dimensions.
  • Cluster sizes and densities reflect perplexity: t-SNE expands dense clusters and contracts sparse clusters so they appear to have similar spread in 2D. Apparent cluster radius cannot be interpreted as geometric variance.
  • Non-parametric and non-inductive: Standard t-SNE does not learn a functional mapping $f(x) \to y$. New test samples cannot be projected without rerunning the entire optimisation from scratch.
  • Stochastic non-convex optimisation: The KL divergence objective has many local minima. Different random initialisations and perplexity values can yield visually distinct layouts.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does t-SNE use a Student-t distribution in the low-dimensional space instead of a Gaussian distribution?

Why: In low dimensions, area is constrained. The heavy inverse-square tails of the Student-t distribution ($1/(1+d^2)$) require points with small to moderate affinities to be mapped further apart than a Gaussian would, preventing separate clusters from collapsing into a dense central clump.

Q2What is the primary role of the perplexity hyperparameter in t-SNE?

Why: Perplexity represents the effective continuous degrees of freedom or target neighbourhood size $2^{H(P_i)}$. Binary search tunes $\sigma_i$ per point so its conditional entropy matches $\log_2(\text{perplexity})$.

Q3Why can the distance between two widely separated clusters in a t-SNE plot NOT be interpreted as true high-dimensional geometric distance?

Why: The asymmetric term $p_{ij} \log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})$ approaches zero whenever $p_{ij} \approx 0$. When points belong to different clusters, their $p_{ij}$ is virtually zero, meaning the loss exerts negligible attractive or metric constraints on their relative positions.

Q4Why can standard t-SNE not directly transform a single new, unseen test sample into 2D coordinates?

Why: t-SNE does not learn an encoder or projection matrix $W$; it directly optimises the array of 2D coordinates $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 2}$ via gradient descent over all pairs. Projecting a new point requires rerunning optimisation or using parametric extensions.
