Time-Series Cross-Validation

Evaluating sequential forecasters with expanding or rolling temporal windows, enforcing causal forward testing without lookahead contamination.

01 The idea

Standard k-fold cross-validation shuffles observations at random, which permits models predicting past events to learn from future data. Time-series cross-validation respects the chronological arrow of time by training strictly on historical windows and testing on subsequent future horizons. Selecting between expanding windows, rolling fixed-length windows, and inserting purge/embargo buffers guarantees that backtest metrics faithfully mirror live operational accuracy.

Imagine evaluating a weather model that predicts daily rainfall. If you randomly partition 365 calendar days into 5 standard cross-validation folds, fold 1 might test on a rainy Tuesday in April while training on Monday in April, Wednesday in April, and November storms. The model effectively interpolates between known adjacent data points. In reality, on 15 April you only possess data up to 14 April. Shuffled cross-validation dramatically overestimates accuracy because the model exploits future information that will never exist at forecast origin.

Walk-forward evaluation (also known as rolling-origin evaluation) replicates true operational deployment. In the first split, you train on months 1 to 6 and forecast month 7. In the second split, you expand the training window to include months 1 to 7 and forecast month 8. In the third split, you train on months 1 to 8 and forecast month 9. At every step, the forecast origin moves forward in time, generating out-of-sample predictions strictly on future periods.

The choice between an expanding window and a rolling window depends on stationarity. An expanding window retains all historical data, maximising sample size for stable long-term processes. A rolling window maintains a fixed duration (e.g. exactly 180 days), dropping older observations as new ones arrive. When underlying economic regimes, consumer tastes, or operational processes drift, rolling windows prevent obsolete historical patterns from misleading contemporary forecasts.

In everyday terms

Testing an investment fund manager day-by-day as the financial calendar advances, rather than letting them trade past quarters using tomorrow's newspaper.

02 Step by step

  1. Define forecast horizon $H$: fix the exact number of future time steps the model must predict simultaneously in production (e.g. 7 days or 12 weeks).
  2. Determine initial training length $T_0$ and stride $S$: establish the minimum warm-up history required for stable model estimation and the step size between evaluation origins.
  3. Choose window geometry: adopt an expanding window if older data reinforces seasonal baselines, or a rolling fixed window if historical regimes become stale.
  4. Enforce purge and embargo margins: purge observations whose feature lookback windows overlap with training data, and embargo between consecutive folds if labels have multi-step duration.
  5. Aggregate out-of-fold performance: compute metrics like MAE, WAPE, or MASE across all evaluation origins to produce a robust temporal generalisation score.

03 The maths

  1. Expanding window walk-forward split partition

    \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}^{(k)} = \{ (x_t, y_t) \mid t \le T_0 + k \cdot S \}, \qquad \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}^{(k)} = \{ (x_t, y_t) \mid T_0 + k \cdot S < t \le T_0 + k \cdot S + H \}

    Where $T_0$ is the initial training duration, $S$ is the stride between forecast origins, and $H$ is the forecast horizon. Every split strictly enforces $\max(t_{\text{train}}) < \min(t_{\text{val}})$, ensuring complete temporal causality.

  2. Rolling fixed-width window partition

    \mathcal{D}_{\text{train, rolling}}^{(k)} = \{ (x_t, y_t) \mid T_0 + k \cdot S - W < t \le T_0 + k \cdot S \}

    Where $W$ is the fixed historical window length. Observations older than $t - W$ are discarded, preventing obsolete structural regimes from biasing model parameters.

  3. Multi-horizon aggregate forecast loss

    \mathcal{L}_{\text{TSCV}} = \frac{1}{K \cdot H} \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \sum_{h=1}^H L\left(y_{T_k + h}, \hat{y}_{T_k + h \mid T_k}\right)

    Where $\hat{y}_{T_k + h \mid T_k}$ is the forecast generated at origin $T_k = T_0 + k \cdot S$ for lead time $h$. Averaging loss across multiple origins reduces evaluation variance caused by single-period anomalies.

04 Key takeaways

  • Walk-forward evaluation preserves temporal causality, completely eliminating lookahead leakage in forecasting benchmarks.
  • Expanding windows maximise training sample size; rolling windows drop outdated historical data in non-stationary series.
  • Multi-origin evaluation exposes models to diverse seasonal phases, holidays, and economic cycles.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
T = 40
t = np.arange(T)
y = 10.0 + 0.5 * t + np.random.normal(0, 1.0, size=T)

# Walk-forward expanding window cross-validation (horizon H=3, step S=3, initial T0=25)
T0, H, S = 25, 3, 3
n_splits = (T - T0) // S
maes = []

for k in range(n_splits):
    split_t = T0 + k * S
    train_idx = np.arange(split_t)
    val_idx = np.arange(split_t, split_t + H)
    
    # Fit simple autoregressive lag-1 model on historical window
    y_tr_y = y[train_idx[1:]]
    y_tr_x = y[train_idx[:-1]]
    a, b = np.polyfit(y_tr_x, y_tr_y, 1)
    
    # 3-step recursive forecast
    preds = []
    curr = y[train_idx[-1]]
    for _ in range(H):
        curr = a * curr + b
        preds.append(curr)
    
    mae_k = np.mean(np.abs(y[val_idx] - np.array(preds)))
    maes.append(mae_k)
    print(f"Fold {k}: Train t=0..{split_t-1}, Test t={split_t}..{split_t+H-1}, MAE: {mae_k:.2f}")

print(f"Average Walk-Forward MAE: {np.mean(maes):.2f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Using standard shuffled k-fold cross-validation on time-series data, which leaks future information into training folds.
  • Relying on a single train-test split: a single holdout month may coincide with an outlier event (e.g. severe blizzard or supply shock), yielding misleading validation metrics.
  • Mismatching evaluation horizon with operational reality: evaluating one-step-ahead forecasts when production demands fourteen-day cumulative supply planning.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is standard randomly shuffled k-fold cross-validation invalid for evaluating time-series forecasting models?

Why: Shuffling destroys chronological ordering, allowing future observations to appear in the training fold when predicting past validation points (temporal lookahead leakage).

Q2Under what condition is a rolling fixed-width window preferred over an expanding window in time-series cross-validation?

Why: Rolling windows discard older observations past a fixed duration W, preventing outdated historical relationships from distorting current predictions when regimes shift.

Q3Why is evaluating a forecasting model across multiple walk-forward origins superior to evaluating on a single fixed train/test split?

Why: A single test split might coincide with an unrepresentative period (such as an unusual promotion or supply chain disruption). Averaging across multiple origins yields a far more reliable generalisation metric.
