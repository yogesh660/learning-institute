Learning-Rate Schedules and Warm-Up

A fixed learning rate is either too aggressive early in training or too coarse near the end. Schedules start gently with warm-up, scale to peak velocity, and anneal downward to settle into sharp, high-generalisation minima.

01 The idea

The learning rate is widely regarded as the most critical hyperparameter in deep learning. Modern training recipes rarely keep it constant: linear warm-up stabilises the network when momentum buffers and running statistics are empty, followed by cosine annealing or 1cycle decay to explore broadly and converge cleanly.

At the start of training ($t=1$), network weights are random, running moments in adaptive optimisers such as AdamW ($m_0=0, v_0=0$) are uninitialised, and intermediate representations fluctuate violently. Taking large gradient steps in this chaotic phase thrusts parameters into distorted, ill-conditioned loss ravines from which recovery is slow or impossible. Linear warm-up ramps the learning rate smoothly from near zero to its target peak over the first 1% to 5% of training steps, allowing gradient statistics to form reliable second-moment estimates before high-velocity updates begin.

Once representations stabilise, maintaining a large learning rate allows the optimiser to cross saddle points and skip shallow, poor local basins. However, as the loss landscape transitions into a narrow valley containing desirable minima, a high step size causes parameters to oscillate back and forth between opposing walls without ever reaching the trough. Decaying the learning rate gradually contracts the step radius, enabling the parameter trajectory to settle smoothly into the lowest point of the basin.

Among decay patterns, step decay (cutting the rate by a factor of 10 every $N$ epochs) produces sharp, discontinuous drops that require manual schedule tuning. By contrast, cosine annealing smoothly interpolates the learning rate along a half-cosine curve from $\eta_{\text{max}}$ down to $\eta_{\text{min}}$, offering smooth deceleration with zero abrupt plateaus. This approach has become the standard default across modern vision architectures and large language models.

In everyday terms

Like landing a spacecraft on an asteroid: thrusters fire delicately at first to establish stable orientation (warm-up), cruise at maximum velocity across space (peak rate), and gradually throttle down to a feathering touch for touchdown without bouncing off (cosine decay).

02 Step by step

  1. Determine peak learning rate $\eta_{\text{max}}$: run an LR range test (Leslie Smith test) or adopt proven architecture defaults ($3 \times 10^{-4}$ for AdamW on Transformers; $10^{-1}$ for SGD on ConvNets).
  2. Configure the warm-up window: designate $T_{\text{warm}}$ (typically 1% to 5% of total iterations, or 1 to 5 epochs) to ramp the learning rate linearly from $\eta_{\text{min}}$ (or 0) to $\eta_{\text{max}}$.
  3. Select decay trajectory: deploy cosine annealing for fixed total training budgets $T_{\text{total}}$, or linear decay for bounded fine-tuning tasks.
  4. Synchronise with batch scale: when scaling effective batch size across multiple GPUs, scale $\eta_{\text{max}}$ proportionally (linear scaling $\eta \propto B$ or square-root scaling $\eta \propto \sqrt{B}$) and extend the warm-up window.
  5. Monitor validation dynamics: verify that loss does not explode in the initial 200 steps and that late-stage annealing coincides with sustained improvements in validation metrics.

03 The maths

  1. Linear warm-up schedule

    $$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{base}} + \frac{t}{T_{\text{warm}}}(\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{base}}), \quad 1 \le t \le T_{\text{warm}}$$

    $t$ is the current training iteration, $T_{\text{warm}}$ is the total number of warm-up iterations, $\eta_{\text{base}}$ is the initial floor rate (often $0$ or $10^{-7}$), and $\eta_{\text{max}}$ is the peak target rate. This linear progression shields uninitialised layers from destabilising gradient shocks during initial steps.

  2. Cosine annealing decay

    $$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{min}} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{min}})\left(1 + \cos\left(\frac{t - T_{\text{warm}}}{T_{\text{total}} - T_{\text{warm}}}\pi\right)\right), \quad T_{\text{warm}} \le t \le T_{\text{total}}$$

    $\eta_{\text{min}}$ is the asymptotic minimum learning rate (commonly $0.01 \cdot \eta_{\text{max}}$ or $0$), and $T_{\text{total}}$ is the planned step count. The argument to $\cos$ ranges smoothly from $0$ (where $\cos(0) = 1$) to $\pi$ (where $\cos(\pi) = -1$), providing continuous deceleration without discrete hyperparameter step thresholds.

  3. Step decay comparison

    $$\eta_k = \eta_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor k / s \rfloor}$$

    $k$ is the completed epoch index, $s$ is the step interval (e.g. drop every 30 epochs), and $\gamma$ is the multiplicative discount factor (typically $0.1$ or $0.2$). While simple, step decay introduces sudden drop discontinuities that make progress sensitive to the exact choice of step boundaries.

04 Key takeaways

  • Linear warm-up prevents early divergence caused by noisy gradients and uncalibrated second-moment statistics in adaptive optimisers.
  • Cosine annealing provides a smooth, schedule-long decay that eliminates the arbitrary epoch milestone tuning of step decay.
  • Cosine schedules are budget-dependent: they require knowing the total step count $T_{\text{total}}$ in advance; interrupting training at 50% budget leaves the learning rate far too high for optimal convergence.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

def schedule_lr(step, total_steps=100, warm_steps=20, lr_max=0.01, lr_min=0.0001):
    # Linear warm-up phase
    if step < warm_steps:
        return lr_min + (lr_max - lr_min) * (step / warm_steps)
    # Cosine annealing decay phase
    progress = (step - warm_steps) / (total_steps - warm_steps)
    return lr_min + 0.5 * (lr_max - lr_min) * (1.0 + np.cos(np.pi * progress))

# Evaluate schedule at representative milestones
milestones = [5, 20, 60, 100]
print("Warm-up & Cosine Annealing Milestones (lr_max=0.01, warm=20, total=100):")
for s in milestones:
    rate = schedule_lr(s)
    phase = "Warm-up" if s < 20 else ("Peak" if s == 20 else "Cosine decay")
    print(f"Step {s:3d} ({phase:12s}): learning rate = {rate:.6f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Omitting warm-up when training deep Transformers with AdamW, frequently resulting in immediate gradient explosion or loss divergence in the opening epochs.
  • Setting $T_{\text{total}}$ in a cosine schedule significantly higher than actual training duration, which truncates training before the learning rate can anneal into its low-variance basin.
  • Decaying the learning rate prematurely, trapping the optimiser in a mediocre local plateau before it has sufficiently traversed the parameter space.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is linear warm-up particularly essential when training Transformers with Adam or AdamW?

Why: In early iterations, the second-moment estimate $v_t$ in Adam is biased near zero. Even with bias corrections, initial steps can be volatile; ramping the learning rate protects parameters until gradient statistics stabilise.

Q2What is the primary operational drawback of a cosine annealing schedule compared to adaptive learning rate methods?

Why: Cosine annealing maps progress along $t / T_{\text{total}}$. If training is stopped early (or extended beyond $T_{\text{total}}$), the schedule does not align with the actual stopping point.

Q3Suppose you train a model with total steps $T_{\text{total}}=100$, warm-up steps $T_{\text{warm}}=20$, peak rate $\eta_{\text{max}}=0.01$, and minimum rate $\eta_{\text{min}}=0.0001$. What is the learning rate at step 60 (halfway through the cosine decay phase)?

Why: At step 60, progress through decay is $(60-20)/(100-20) = 40/80 = 0.5$. With $\cos(0.5\pi) = 0$, the rate is $\eta_{\text{min}} + 0.5(\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{min}}) = 0.0001 + 0.5(0.0099) = 0.005050$.

Q4How does the 1cycle policy differ from standard monotonic cosine decay?

Why: Leslie Smith's 1cycle policy couples learning rate warm-up and decay with inverted momentum cycles (e.g. momentum drops from 0.95 to 0.85 during warm-up, then rises back to 0.95 during decay).
