Exponential Smoothing and the ETS Framework

Exponential smoothing generates forecasts using geometrically decaying weights on past data, generalised by the ETS state-space framework into formal statistical likelihood models.

01 The idea

Moving averages assign equal weight to recent observations and drop older history abruptly at window boundaries. Exponential smoothing replaces this cliff edge with geometrically decaying weights, giving greatest weight to recent data. The ETS (Error, Trend, Seasonal) framework formalises these intuitive heuristics into state-space innovations models with exact likelihoods and probabilistic prediction intervals.

Imagine tracking customer foot traffic at a café. Yesterday's attendance provides a strong signal for today's staffing needs, whereas traffic from three months ago carries much less relevance. If you compute a simple 7-day moving average, an unusual spike on day 1 drops off abruptly on day 8, causing an artificial jump in your forecast. Exponential smoothing eliminates this cliff: the most recent observation receives weight $\alpha$, the preceding day $\alpha(1-\alpha)$, the day before $\alpha(1-\alpha)^2$, and so on, fading into the past along an elegant, continuous curve.

Simple exponential smoothing (SES) handles stationary series with no clear trend or seasonal rhythm. When a series trends steadily upward or downward, Holt's linear method introduces a second parameter $\beta$ to update a local slope (growth rate). When periodic rhythms appear—such as weekend demand spikes—Holt–Winters smoothing incorporates a third parameter $\gamma$ to adjust seasonal indices, operating additively (constant seasonal amplitude) or multiplicatively (seasonal swings proportional to the series level).

The modern ETS taxonomy (Hyndman et al.) classifies time-series models by their three constituent components: Error ($\{A, M\}$), Trend ($\{N, A, A_d, M\}$), and Seasonal ($\{N, A, M\}$). Formulating exponential smoothing as state-space innovations models provides an exact Gaussian likelihood, transforming heuristic smoothing formulas into rigorous statistical estimators that support automated model selection via AIC and analytical prediction intervals.

In everyday terms

Exponential smoothing acts like a vehicular shock absorber: it damps short-term road turbulence while smoothly adjusting to sustained uphill grades (trend) and rhythmic highway ripples (seasonality).

02 Step by step

  1. Decompose the historical series to determine whether trend and seasonal oscillations are absent, additive, or multiplicative.
  2. Select candidate ETS specifications: e.g. $\text{ETS}(A, N, N)$ for level data, $\text{ETS}(A, A, N)$ for trending data, or $\text{ETS}(M, A, M)$ for seasonal series with widening variance.
  3. Formulate state recursive equations for unobserved level $l_t$, trend growth $b_t$, and seasonal factor $s_t$.
  4. Estimate smoothing parameters ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in [0, 1]$) and initial states ($l_0, b_0, s_0$) by minimising the sum of squared one-step forecast errors or maximising likelihood.
  5. Generate recursive one-step-ahead fitted values $\hat{y}_{t|t-1}$ across training observations and perform residual diagnostic checks.
  6. Extrapolate state estimates forward across forecast horizon $h$ and compute probabilistic prediction bounds.

03 The maths

  1. Simple exponential smoothing (SES)

    $$\hat{y}_{t+1|t} = l_t, \quad l_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha) l_{t-1} = l_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - l_{t-1})$$

    $l_t$ represents the estimated series level at time $t$, $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ is the smoothing parameter, and $e_t = y_t - l_{t-1}$ is the one-step-ahead forecast error. As $\alpha \to 1$, the model approaches a naive random walk; as $\alpha \to 0$, it converges to the historical sample mean.

  2. Holt’s linear trend method

    $$l_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)(l_{t-1} + b_{t-1}), \quad b_t = \beta(l_t - l_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta)b_{t-1}$$

    $l_t$ tracks the local level, $b_t$ tracks local slope (trend), and $\beta \in (0, 1)$ governs the adaptation rate of the trend. Forecasts $h$ steps ahead extrapolate linearly: $\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = l_t + h b_t$. Damped trends multiply $b_t$ by damping factor $\phi \in (0, 1)$ to prevent unrealistic long-horizon divergence.

  3. The state-space innovations model: ETS(A, A, N)

    $$y_t = l_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \quad l_t = l_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \alpha\epsilon_t, \quad b_t = b_{t-1} + \alpha\beta\epsilon_t$$

    Writing exponential smoothing in state-space form with a single innovation source $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ defines a joint probability density over the observations. This allows maximum likelihood estimation, information-criterion model selection (AIC, BIC), and exact prediction interval derivations.

04 Key takeaways

  • Weights decay geometrically: past observation $y_{t-k}$ carries weight $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k$, ensuring all historical data contributes while recent data dominates.
  • Damped trend specifications ($\phi \in [0.8, 0.98]$) guard against overly aggressive linear trend extrapolations in long-range forecasting.
  • Multiplicative error models explicitly accommodate heteroscedasticity where forecast uncertainty scales in proportion to series magnitude.
  • ETS models focus on trend, level, and seasonal states; unlike ARIMA, they do not explicitly parameterise stationary cyclical autocorrelations.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 100
# Synthesise time series with shifting level and noise
level = 50.0 + np.cumsum(np.random.normal(0, 0.5, n))
y = level + np.random.normal(0, 1.5, n)

def ses_fit(y_series, alpha):
    n_pts = len(y_series)
    l = np.zeros(n_pts)
    l[0] = y_series[0]
    for t in range(1, n_pts):
        l[t] = alpha * y_series[t-1] + (1 - alpha) * l[t-1]
    errors = y_series[1:] - l[1:]
    return l, errors

# Optimise alpha over grid [0.05, 0.95] by minimising sum of squared errors
alphas = np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 91)
best_alpha, best_sse = None, float('inf')
for a in alphas:
    _, errs = ses_fit(y, a)
    sse = np.sum(errs**2)
    if sse < best_sse:
        best_sse, best_alpha = sse, a

opt_l, opt_errs = ses_fit(y, best_alpha)
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean(opt_errs**2))
final_level = best_alpha * y[-1] + (1 - best_alpha) * opt_l[-1]
forecast_h = [final_level] * 3

print("Optimal alpha:", round(float(best_alpha), 3))
print("In-sample RMSE:", round(float(rmse), 3))
print("Last observed y:", round(float(y[-1]), 2))
print("Estimated final level:", round(float(final_level), 2))
print("3-step ahead forecasts:", [round(float(f), 2) for f in forecast_h])

Common pitfalls

  • Selecting smoothing parameters outside permissible stability bounds, which causes state updates to oscillate unstably or diverge.
  • Unconstrained linear trend extrapolation: projecting undamped linear trends into distant horizons often yields implausibly massive forecasts.
  • Using additive seasonality when seasonal amplitude expands proportionally with the series level: multiplicative seasonality ($\text{ETS}(\cdot, \cdot, M)$) is required.
  • Confusing simple moving averages with exponential smoothing: moving averages have a finite rectangular memory, whereas exponential smoothing has an infinite decaying memory.

06 Check yourself

Q1In Simple Exponential Smoothing, how do the weights assigned to past observations behave as we look further back in time?

Why: Expanding the recursive formula shows that observation $y_{t-k}$ receives weight $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k$, decaying smoothly and geometrically as lag $k$ increases.

Q2When should multiplicative seasonality be chosen over additive seasonality in the ETS framework?

Why: Additive seasonality assumes seasonal fluctuations have constant magnitude regardless of series level, whereas multiplicative seasonality scales seasonal swings proportionally to the local level.

Q3What is the primary benefit of formulating exponential smoothing as a state-space innovations model in ETS?

Why: The state-space innovations formulation defines an exact probability distribution for the series, enabling maximum likelihood estimation, AIC/BIC comparison, and formal probabilistic forecast intervals.

Q4Why is trend damping ($\phi < 1$) frequently introduced into Holt’s linear trend method?

Why: Linear trends tend to produce overly extreme projections on distant horizons; damping factor $\phi \in (0.8, 0.98)$ gradually flattens the trend toward a constant horizon level.
