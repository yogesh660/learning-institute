01 The idea

When analysing aggregated datasets, a statistical trend observed in the population can completely reverse direction when the data is partitioned into subgroups. This statistical phenomenon—Simpson's paradox—occurs when an unobserved or uncontrolled confounding variable distorts the marginal relationship between treatment and outcome. Understanding confounding and applying stratified analysis or causal adjustment prevents disastrously mistaken policy and business decisions.

Consider a clinical trial evaluating two treatments for kidney stones: Treatment A (an invasive open surgical procedure) and Treatment B (a non-invasive ultrasound procedure). Looking at the overall aggregate data across 700 patients, Treatment A succeeds in 273 out of 350 patients (78.0%), while Treatment B succeeds in 289 out of 350 patients (82.6%). A health authority inspecting only this aggregate summary would recommend Treatment B, which appears to boast a 4.6 percentage point advantage.

Now partition the data by stone severity (the confounding variable): for small stones, Treatment A succeeds in 81/87 (93.1%), while Treatment B succeeds in 234/270 (86.7%)—Treatment A wins by 6.4 percentage points! For large stones, Treatment A succeeds in 192/263 (73.0%), while Treatment B succeeds in 55/80 (68.8%)—Treatment A wins again by 4.3 percentage points!

Treatment A is medically superior for small stones AND superior for large stones. Yet when pooled together, Treatment A looks worse! Why does this happen? Stone severity acts as a confounder: severe cases have lower survival rates overall, and doctors disproportionately assigned severe patients to invasive Treatment A (263 vs 80). Treatment B received a flood of easy, mild cases (270 vs 87). Aggregation conflates treatment efficacy with patient sickness, producing an illusory reversal. Standardised adjustment reweighting by marginal stone size restores the true positive treatment effect of +5.4%.

In everyday terms

Simpson's paradox is like comparing two basketball players' overall shooting percentages when one player shoots mostly easy layups and the other takes contested three-pointers: the second player can have a higher percentage from both ranges yet have a lower overall score.

02 Step by step

  1. Identify the treatment variable $X$, the outcome variable $Y$, and potential confounding covariates $Z$.
  2. Inspect the data at both the aggregated level and within stratified subgroups of $Z$.
  3. Test whether the confounder $Z$ is associated with both the treatment allocation $X$ and the outcome $Y$.
  4. Draw a causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) to determine if $Z$ is a true confounder ($X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$) rather than a mediator ($X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y$).
  5. Compute stratum-specific effect sizes and combine them using weighted stratification or standardised adjustment.
  6. Interpret the adjusted causal effect rather than the misleading aggregated marginal association.

03 The maths

  1. Mathematical Formulation of Simpson's Reversal

    $$P(Y \mid X) < P(Y \mid \neg X) \quad \text{while} \quad P(Y \mid X, Z_k) > P(Y \mid \neg X, Z_k) \quad \forall k$$

    By the law of total probability, marginal probability is a weighted sum over subgroups: $P(Y \mid X) = \sum_k P(Y \mid X, Z_k) P(Z_k \mid X)$. When treatment assignment strongly correlates with the confounder $Z$, the subgroup weights $P(Z_k \mid X)$ differ dramatically, allowing the aggregate marginal comparison to reverse sign relative to every subgroup.

  2. Confounder Structure (Causal DAG)

    $$X \longleftarrow Z \longrightarrow Y$$

    A confounder $Z$ is a common cause that affects both treatment assignment $X$ and outcome $Y$. Failing to condition on $Z$ leaves an open backdoor path $X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$, confounding the true causal effect of $X$ on $Y$ with non-causal association.

  3. Standardised Causal Risk Difference

    $$\Delta_{\text{adj}} = \sum_{k=1}^K \left[P(Y=1 \mid X=1, Z=k) - P(Y=1 \mid X=0, Z=k)\right] P(Z=k)$$

    Standardised adjustment eliminates confounding bias by re-weighting stratum-specific treatment differences by the marginal population distribution $P(Z=k)$ rather than the biased treatment-dependent weights $P(Z \mid X)$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Simpson's paradox occurs when an uncontrolled confounding variable is associated with both the treatment and the outcome.
  • A treatment can be superior in every single subgroup yet appear inferior in aggregate data due to imbalanced allocation.
  • True confounders must be controlled for; however, conditioning on a mediator ($X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$) or a collider ($X \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y$) introduces severe bias.
  • Randomised controlled trials (A/B tests) prevent Simpson's paradox by forcing treatment allocation to be statistically independent of all baseline covariates.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Kidney stone treatment data: [Successes, Total]
# Small stones: Treatment A vs B
a_small, a_small_tot = 81, 87      # 93.1%
b_small, b_small_tot = 234, 270    # 86.7%

# Large stones: Treatment A vs B
a_large, a_large_tot = 192, 263    # 73.0%
b_large, b_large_tot = 55, 80      # 68.8%

# Aggregated data
rate_a_agg = (a_small + a_large) / (a_small_tot + a_large_tot)
rate_b_agg = (b_small + b_large) / (b_small_tot + b_large_tot)

# Stratified differences within each stone size
diff_small = (a_small / a_small_tot) - (b_small / b_small_tot)
diff_large = (a_large / a_large_tot) - (b_large / b_large_tot)

# Standardised causal risk difference (reweighting by population stone distribution)
tot_small = a_small_tot + b_small_tot
tot_large = a_large_tot + b_large_tot
w_small = tot_small / (tot_small + tot_large)
w_large = tot_large / (tot_small + tot_large)
adj_causal_diff = w_small * diff_small + w_large * diff_large

print(f"Aggregate Success:  Treatment A={rate_a_agg:.1%}, B={rate_b_agg:.1%} (B appears +{rate_b_agg - rate_a_agg:.1%} better)")
print(f"Small Stones:       Treatment A={a_small/a_small_tot:.1%}, B={b_small/b_small_tot:.1%} (A is {diff_small:+.1%} better)")
print(f"Large Stones:       Treatment A={a_large/a_large_tot:.1%}, B={b_large/b_large_tot:.1%} (A is {diff_large:+.1%} better)")
print(f"Adjusted Causal Difference: {adj_causal_diff:+.1%} (Treatment A is truly superior)")

Common pitfalls

  • Blindly trusting aggregate conversion rates or medical trial results without checking for subgroup imbalances.
  • Controlling for variables that are consequences of the treatment (mediators), which blocks the true causal mechanism.
  • Conditioning on colliders (common effects of treatment and outcome), which artificially manufactures non-existent correlations.
  • Believing that collecting more observational data resolves Simpson's paradox; more unadjusted data simply estimates the biased number with higher precision.

06 Check yourself

Q1What causes Simpson's paradox to occur in observational data?

Why: When an uncontrolled confounder influences both treatment allocation and the outcome, the marginal aggregated average is weighted unevenly, reversing the true subgroup direction.

Q2In the classic kidney stone example, why did Treatment B appear superior in the aggregate data despite being inferior in both stone subgroups?

Why: Doctors disproportionately assigned mild cases to Treatment B and severe cases to Treatment A, confounding treatment efficacy with baseline patient sickness.

Q3Why does running a properly randomised A/B test prevent Simpson's paradox from occurring?

Why: Random assignment breaks the link between confounders and treatment ($X \leftarrow Z$), ensuring both groups have identical distributions of all baseline characteristics.

Q4In a causal DAG, what type of variable is positioned on the causal chain between treatment and outcome ($X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$)?

Why: A mediator lies on the causal path from treatment to outcome. Controlling for a mediator blocks the causal effect rather than removing confounding.
