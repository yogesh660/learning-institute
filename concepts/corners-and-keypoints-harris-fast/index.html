Corners and keypoints: Harris, FAST

Corners and keypoints are salient two-dimensional image interest points where intensity changes abruptly across multiple directions, providing stable spatial landmarks for tracking, visual odometry, and image alignment. The Harris corner detector evaluates the local structure tensor (second-moment matrix), classifying local patches via eigenvalues: two large eigenvalues indicate a corner, one large eigenvalue denotes an edge, and two small eigenvalues reflect a flat uniform region. The FAST (Features from Accelerated Segment Test) algorithm delivers ultra-fast keypoint detection for real-time mobile robotics by evaluating a 16-pixel Bresenham circle around candidate pixels using high-speed decision trees.

01 The idea

If you attempt to track a featureless white wall across video frames, every pixel appears identical, creating total motion ambiguity. If you track a straight horizontal edge, you can measure vertical movement accurately, but horizontal motion remains undetectable—the classical "aperture problem." Only at corners, junctions, and textured keypoints does image intensity vary in all spatial directions, fixing a unique, unambiguous point in two-dimensional space. From Chris Harris and Mike Stephens's second-moment matrix in 1988 to Edward Rosten and Tom Drummond's microsecond FAST detector, corner detection forms the foundation of classical geometric vision and visual SLAM.

The aperture problem and corner uniqueness: Consider shifting a small inspection window by a displacement $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$. Over a flat surface, the window contents do not change regardless of direction. Over a straight edge, shifting the window parallel to the edge produces zero change, whereas shifting perpendicular produces large changes. Only over a corner does shifting the window in any spatial direction cause a steep increase in squared intensity difference. Corners therefore resolve the aperture problem completely.

The structure tensor and eigenvalue geometry: The Harris detector formalises this directional variation by taking a first-order Taylor expansion of the windowed squared difference. This yields a quadratic form governed by the $2 \times 2$ second-moment matrix (structure tensor) $M = \sum w(x, y) \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}$, where $I_x, I_y$ are spatial image gradients and $w(x, y)$ is a Gaussian weighting window. The two eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ of $M$ characterize the principal curvatures of the local auto-correlation surface: if both eigenvalues are large and of similar magnitude, the patch is an unambiguous corner.

The Harris response shortcut: Computing explicit eigenvalues for every single pixel in an image requires expensive matrix square roots. Harris and Stephens proposed an algebraic shortcut: $R = \det(M) - k\,(\operatorname{tr}(M))^2 = (\lambda_1 \lambda_2) - k\,(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^2$, where $k$ is an empirical tuning parameter (typically $k \in [0.04, 0.06]$). A large positive response $R$ signifies a corner, a negative response indicates an edge, and an absolute value near zero denotes a flat region.

The need for speed: FAST and circular segment tests: While Harris detection is rotationally invariant and mathematically elegant, computing Gaussian-weighted tensor products across millions of pixels consumes valuable frame time in real-time robotic systems. In 2006, FAST revolutionised real-time tracking by abandoning gradients altogether. FAST inspects a Bresenham circle of 16 pixels surrounding candidate pixel $p$. If a contiguous arc of $N$ pixels (typically $N=9$ or $12$) are all significantly brighter or all significantly darker than $p$ by a threshold $\tau$, $p$ is flagged as a corner candidate. By testing pixels 1, 9, 5, and 13 first, non-corner candidates are rejected in three to four machine instructions.

In everyday terms

Imagine looking at a blueprint through a tiny circular magnifying peephole. If you look at the middle of a blank plaster wall, sliding your peephole in any direction reveals only uniform white paper: you have no idea where you are. If you look at a straight black boundary line, sliding the peephole along the line looks identical: you cannot tell whether the blueprint is sliding left or right. But if you position the peephole directly over an intersecting corner where two walls meet, any nudge in any direction immediately alters the pattern of lines within the glass. You are anchored to a singular, trackable coordinate. Harris acts like a mathematical sensor measuring the resistance to sliding in all directions; FAST acts like a guard checking whether the boundary cuts a wide continuous arc around the perimeter of the circle.

03 The maths

  1. Autocorrelation error surface and structure tensor

    E(\Delta x, \Delta y) \approx [\Delta x, \Delta y]\, M \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \end{bmatrix}, \quad M = \sum_{(x, y) \in W} w(x, y) \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}

    A second-order Taylor series approximates the change in squared intensity difference $E(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ for a small shift. The second-moment matrix $M$ encapsulates the local gradient distribution inside window $W$.

  2. Harris corner response function

    R = \det(M) - k\,(\operatorname{tr}(M))^2 = (\lambda_1 \lambda_2) - k\,(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^2

    Here $\det(M) = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{tr}(M) = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. If both eigenvalues are large ($\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2 \gg 0$), $\det(M)$ dominates and $R > 0$ (corner). If one eigenvalue dominates ($\lambda_1 \gg \lambda_2$), $(\operatorname{tr}(M))^2$ dominates and $R < 0$ (edge). If both are small, $R \approx 0$ (flat region). Parameter $k$ is usually set to $0.04$ to $0.06$.

  3. FAST contiguous arc criterion

    \exists \text{ contiguous arc of } N \text{ pixels on circle } C_{16} \text{ such that } \forall i, \; I_i > I_p + \tau \quad \text{or} \quad \forall i, \; I_i < I_p - \tau

    Here $C_{16}$ is the 16-pixel perimeter of radius 3 centred on pixel $p$, $\tau$ is an intensity threshold, and $N$ is typically chosen as $9$ or $12$. Decision trees trained via ID3 can classify candidate pixels in very few CPU instructions per location.

04 Key takeaways

  • Corners resolve the aperture problem by exhibiting intense gradient variation in all spatial directions.
  • The Harris structure tensor $M$ summarises local directional gradient energy via its eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$.
  • The Harris response $R = \det(M) - k\,(\operatorname{tr}(M))^2$ classifies corners ($R > 0$), edges ($R < 0$), and flat regions ($R \approx 0$) without computing matrix square roots.
  • Harris corners are invariant to 2D image rotation and affine intensity shifts, but are NOT invariant to significant changes in image scale.
  • FAST accelerates corner detection for real-time mobile robotics and SLAM by inspecting a 16-pixel Bresenham circle and using early-rejection tests.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Create a synthetic 16x16 image containing an L-shaped corner
img = np.zeros((16, 16), dtype=np.float32)
img[4:12, 4:12] = 200.0  # Bright square on dark background
img[8:12, 8:12] = 0.0    # Carve out bottom-right to create inner & outer corners

# 1. Compute spatial derivatives using Sobel filters
Kx = np.array([[-1, 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]], dtype=np.float32)
Ky = np.array([[-1, -2, -1], [0, 0, 0], [1, 2, 1]], dtype=np.float32)

H, W = img.shape
Ix = np.zeros((H-2, W-2), dtype=np.float32)
Iy = np.zeros((H-2, W-2), dtype=np.float32)

for r in range(H-2):
    for c in range(W-2):
        patch = img[r:r+3, c:c+3]
        Ix[r, c] = np.sum(patch * Kx)
        Iy[r, c] = np.sum(patch * Ky)

# 2. Structure tensor components: Ix^2, Iy^2, Ix*Iy with local 3x3 window smoothing
Ix2 = Ix * Ix
Iy2 = Iy * Iy
Ixy = Ix * Iy

# Box smoothing kernel (approximating local window w)
kernel = np.ones((3, 3), dtype=np.float32) / 9.0
sH, sW = Ix.shape
A = np.zeros((sH-2, sW-2), dtype=np.float32)
B = np.zeros((sH-2, sW-2), dtype=np.float32)
C = np.zeros((sH-2, sW-2), dtype=np.float32)

for r in range(sH-2):
    for c in range(sW-2):
        A[r, c] = np.sum(Ix2[r:r+3, c:c+3] * kernel)
        B[r, c] = np.sum(Iy2[r:r+3, c:c+3] * kernel)
        C[r, c] = np.sum(Ixy[r:r+3, c:c+3] * kernel)

# 3. Compute Harris response: R = det(M) - k * (trace(M))^2
k = 0.04
det_M = (A * B) - (C * C)
trace_M = A + B
harris_R = det_M - k * (trace_M ** 2)

# 4. Thresholding and peak extraction
threshold = 0.05 * np.max(harris_R)
corners = harris_R > threshold

print(f"Max Harris response: {np.max(harris_R):.1f}")
print(f"Number of corner response peaks found: {np.sum(corners)}")

# 5. FAST candidate logic check on a single 16-pixel circular perimeter
# Circle radius 3 offsets (16 pixels)
p_center = 200.0  # Candidate corner pixel intensity
# Synthetic perimeter: 9 contiguous pixels are dark (intensity ~ 0)
circle_16 = np.array([200, 200, 200, 200, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 200, 200, 200], dtype=np.float32)
tau = 50.0

is_darker = circle_16 < (p_center - tau)
# Check circular contiguous run of 9
extended = np.concatenate([is_darker, is_darker[:8]])
max_run = max(len(s) for s in "".join(extended.astype(int).astype(str)).split("0"))

print(f"FAST longest contiguous dark arc: {max_run} pixels (threshold N=9)")
print(f"FAST corner confirmed: {max_run >= 9}")

Common pitfalls

  • Scale sensitivity: Harris corners are not scale-invariant; zooming into an image causes a sharp corner to become a smooth, rounded edge at a larger scale, causing Harris detection to fail unless paired with scale-space pyramids.
  • Choosing an un-tuned parameter $k$: Setting $k$ too small ($<0.02$) causes false positive edge detections to be misclassified as corners; setting $k$ too large ($>0.10$) rejects genuine dull corners.
  • Using rectangular box windows instead of Gaussian weights: Box filter windows introduce artificial directional bias along the horizontal and vertical axes; isotropic Gaussian smoothing ensures true rotational invariance.
  • Omitting non-maximum suppression: Without non-maximum suppression, strong corners generate clusters of 10 to 20 adjacent redundant detections around a single physical junction.
  • FAST sensitivity to high-frequency image noise: Because FAST tests individual raw pixel intensities on a circular perimeter without pre-smoothing, electronic sensor noise can trigger spurious corner detections; applying mild Gaussian blur beforehand is recommended in low-light conditions.

06 Check yourself

Q1What does a large positive value ($R > 0$) of the Harris corner response function signify about the underlying image patch?

Why: When both eigenvalues of the structure tensor $M$ are large, $\det(M) = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$ dominates the trace penalty $k(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^2$, yielding a large positive response $R > 0$ that signals an unambiguous corner.

Q2Why is the Harris corner detector invariant to 2D image rotation but sensitive to significant changes in image scale?

Why: The eigenvalues of the structure tensor describe the ellipse of the autocorrelation surface, which rotates with the image without changing size. However, the smoothing window has a fixed spatial size: zooming into a sharp corner widens it into a gradual edge, causing detection failure.

Q3How does the FAST corner detector achieve ultra-fast execution speeds suitable for real-time robotic SLAM?

Why: FAST avoids computing floating-point image derivatives. Instead, it tests the 4 cardinal pixels (1, 5, 9, 13) on a 16-pixel circular perimeter, rejecting up to 90% of non-corner pixels with minimal CPU instructions.

Q4What occurs in optical flow and feature tracking if one attempts to track points located along straight edges rather than at corners (the aperture problem)?

Why: Along a straight edge, spatial gradients exist only perpendicular to the boundary. Any motion parallel to the edge leaves the local window unchanged, preventing the recovery of full 2D velocity (the classical aperture problem).
