01 The idea

Machine learning models trained on historical data often inherit and amplify societal biases. Algorithmic fairness formalises fairness criteria into mathematical constraints, uncovering inherent trade-offs between competing definitions of fairness.

When automated algorithms decide who receives a bank loan, who is interviewed for a job, who receives bail, or who is prioritised for organ transplants, biased decisions inflict grave societal harm. Because machine learning models are trained to optimise accuracy on historical data, they inevitably learn, internalise, and amplify historical prejudices, redlining, and demographic disparities present in the training corpus.

Algorithmic Fairness translates moral and legal concepts of equity into rigorous mathematical definitions, evaluated with respect to a sensitive or protected attribute $A \in \{0, 1\}$ (e.g. race, gender, disability, or age).

Three major mathematical definitions dominate the literature. First, Demographic Parity (Statistical Parity) requires that the decision rate is identical across groups: $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$, formalised in US employment law as the Four-Fifths rule. Second, Equalized Odds requires that the model is equally accurate across groups, demanding identical True Positive Rates and False Positive Rates: $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0, Y=y) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1, Y=y)$. Third, Predictive Parity (Calibration within groups) requires that predicted risk scores mean the same thing across groups: $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=1, A=0) = P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=1, A=1)$.

In 2016, Alexandra Chouldechova and Jon Kleinberg independently proved a foundational impossibility theorem: unless the base rates of the positive outcome are identical across demographic groups ($P(Y=1 \mid A=0) = P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$), it is mathematically impossible for any model to simultaneously satisfy Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, and Predictive Parity. Choosing a fairness metric is therefore an ethical and legal policy choice, not a purely statistical one.

In everyday terms

Imagine two high schools: School A and School B. At School A, 60% of students pass a national physics exam; at School B, only 30% pass due to historical underfunding of science laboratories. If a university adopts Demographic Parity, it admits the top 20% of students from both schools, ignoring the difference in exam pass rates. If the university adopts Equalized Odds, it ensures that every student who actually mastered the subject has an equal chance of admission regardless of school. If it adopts Predictive Parity, it ensures admitted students from both schools succeed at university with equal probability. You cannot achieve all three simultaneously.

03 The maths

  1. Demographic Parity Disparate Impact Ratio

    \mathrm{DI} = \frac{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0)}{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)} \ge 0.80

    The four-fifths (80%) rule: if the selection rate for a protected minority group is less than 80% of the selection rate for the majority group, the model exhibits prima facie disparate impact.

  2. Equalized Odds Definition

    P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0, \; Y = y) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1, \; Y = y) \quad \forall y \in \{0, 1\}

    Demands both equal False Positive Rates (FPR_0 = FPR_1) and equal True Positive Rates (TPR_0 = TPR_1) across demographic groups.

  3. Kleinberg–Chouldechova Impossibility Theorem

    P(Y = 1 \mid A = 0) \neq P(Y = 1 \mid A = 1) \implies \neg \left( \text{Equalized Odds} \wedge \text{Predictive Parity} \right)

    When true underlying prevalence differs between demographic groups, Equalized Odds and Predictive Parity (calibration within groups) are mutually exclusive.

04 Key takeaways

  • Formalises ethical equity into mathematical metrics evaluated across protected demographic attributes.
  • Demographic Parity requires equal positive selection rates across groups ($P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$).
  • Equalized Odds requires equal False Positive and True Positive rates across groups conditional on true label $Y$.
  • Kleinberg–Chouldechova Impossibility Theorem proves that when demographic base rates differ, competing fairness criteria cannot be satisfied simultaneously.
  • Mitigation spans pre-processing (reweighing data), in-processing (adversarial debiasing), and post-processing (group-specific threshold shifting).

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

# Algorithmic Fairness Audit and Post-Processing Threshold Tuning from scratch
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 1000

# Protected attribute A: 0 (Minority, 30%), 1 (Majority, 70%)
A = np.random.binomial(1, 0.70, n_samples)

# Features: Experience and Test Score
# Historical disparity: Group 0 has slightly lower average test score due to systemic bias
score = np.where(A == 1, np.random.normal(65, 10, n_samples), np.random.normal(58, 10, n_samples))
experience = np.random.uniform(1, 10, n_samples)
X = np.column_stack([score, experience])

# Ground truth qualification: y depends purely on merit (score + experience)
latent_merit = 0.08 * score + 0.3 * experience - 6.5
y = (latent_merit + np.random.normal(0, 0.5, n_samples) > 0).astype(int)

# Train a standard logistic regression model (without explicit attribute A)
clf = LogisticRegression()
clf.fit(X, y)
probs = clf.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]

# Baseline: Default single threshold at p = 0.50
preds_default = (probs >= 0.50).astype(int)

def compute_fairness_audit(y_true, y_pred, sensitive_attr):
    mask0 = (sensitive_attr == 0)
    mask1 = (sensitive_attr == 1)
    
    # Selection Rates (Demographic Parity)
    sr0 = np.mean(y_pred[mask0])
    sr1 = np.mean(y_pred[mask1])
    di_ratio = sr0 / max(sr1, 1e-12)
    
    # True Positive Rates (Equal Opportunity)
    tpr0 = np.sum((y_true[mask0] == 1) & (y_pred[mask0] == 1)) / np.sum(y_true[mask0] == 1)
    tpr1 = np.sum((y_true[mask1] == 1) & (y_pred[mask1] == 1)) / np.sum(y_true[mask1] == 1)
    
    # False Positive Rates
    fpr0 = np.sum((y_true[mask0] == 0) & (y_pred[mask0] == 1)) / np.sum(y_true[mask0] == 0)
    fpr1 = np.sum((y_true[mask1] == 0) & (y_pred[mask1] == 1)) / np.sum(y_true[mask1] == 0)
    
    return di_ratio, tpr0, tpr1, fpr0, fpr1

di_def, tpr0_def, tpr1_def, fpr0_def, fpr1_def = compute_fairness_audit(y, preds_default, A)

# Post-Processing: Equal Opportunity Threshold Tuning (Hardt et al.)
# Adjust threshold tau_0 for Group 0 so that TPR_0 matches TPR_1
target_tpr = tpr1_def
thresholds = np.linspace(0.1, 0.9, 200)
best_tau0 = 0.5
best_tpr_diff = float("inf")

mask0 = (A == 0)
for tau in thresholds:
    preds_g0 = (probs[mask0] >= tau).astype(int)
    tpr_g0 = np.sum((y[mask0] == 1) & (preds_g0 == 1)) / np.sum(y[mask0] == 1)
    diff = abs(tpr_g0 - target_tpr)
    if diff < best_tpr_diff:
        best_tpr_diff = diff
        best_tau0 = tau

# Apply tuned group-specific thresholds
preds_fair = np.zeros(n_samples, dtype=int)
preds_fair[A == 1] = (probs[A == 1] >= 0.50).astype(int)
preds_fair[A == 0] = (probs[A == 0] >= best_tau0).astype(int)

di_fair, tpr0_fair, tpr1_fair, fpr0_fair, fpr1_fair = compute_fairness_audit(y, preds_fair, A)

print("--- Standard Baseline Model (Threshold = 0.50) ---")
print(f"Disparate Impact Ratio: {di_def:.3f} (Violates 80% four-fifths rule!)")
print(f"Group 0 TPR: {tpr0_def*100:.1f}% vs Group 1 TPR: {tpr1_def*100:.1f}% (Equal Opportunity Gap: {abs(tpr0_def - tpr1_def)*100:.1f}%)")

print(f"\n--- Post-Processed Fair Model (Tau_1 = 0.50, Tau_0 = {best_tau0:.2f}) ---")
print(f"Disparate Impact Ratio: {di_fair:.3f} (Improved)")
print(f"Group 0 TPR: {tpr0_fair*100:.1f}% vs Group 1 TPR: {tpr1_fair*100:.1f}% (Equal Opportunity Gap: {abs(tpr0_fair - tpr1_fair)*100:.1f}% -> Parity achieved!)")

Common pitfalls

  • Fairness through blindness: Merely dropping protected attributes (race, gender) from feature sets fails completely because correlated proxy features (zip code, university, hobbies) reconstruct the attribute.
  • Ignoring the impossibility theorem: Promising a client or regulator that a model will satisfy both calibration and equalised odds simultaneously is mathematically impossible when base rates differ.
  • Trade-off with global accuracy: Imposing fairness constraints shifts decision boundaries away from Bayes optimal risk minimisation, causing slight reductions in overall unconstrained accuracy.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does removing the sensitive attribute (e.g. Race or Gender) from the feature set fail to prevent algorithmic discrimination?

Why: Known as "fairness through blindness", redundant encodings in high-dimensional datasets allow complex models to infer protected demographics indirectly from correlated proxy features.

Q2What is established by the Kleinberg–Chouldechova Impossibility Theorem in algorithmic fairness?

Why: The theorem mathematically proves that calibration within groups (predictive parity) and equalised false positive/negative rates (equalised odds) cannot both hold when base rates P(Y=1|A) differ.
