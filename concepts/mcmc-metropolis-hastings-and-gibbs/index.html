MCMC: Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms enable numerical sampling from complex, high-dimensional posterior probability distributions whose normalising constants (evidence integrals) are analytically intractable. The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm constructs an ergodic Markov chain whose stationary distribution equals the target posterior by proposing candidate states from an arbitrary proposal distribution and accepting them with a calculated acceptance probability that satisfies detailed balance. Gibbs sampling specialises Metropolis-Hastings by iteratively sampling each variable conditioned on all remaining parameters, achieving a guaranteed 100% acceptance rate.

01 The idea

Bayes' rule is simple in concept: posterior equals likelihood times prior divided by marginal likelihood. But that denominator—the marginal likelihood \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) p(\theta) \, d\theta—is an integral over hundreds or thousands of parameters. In high dimensions, standard numerical grid integration is impossible (requiring more grid points than atoms in the universe). How can we draw samples from a posterior distribution when we can only evaluate its unnormalised numerator? MCMC algorithms solve this profound computational bottleneck.

The Intractable Denominator: We know p(\theta | D) = p(D | \theta) p(\theta) / Z, but normalising constant Z is unknown. Notice that if we take the ratio of posterior densities between two proposed states \theta^* and \theta, the unknown constant Z cancels out completely: p(\theta^* | D) / p(\theta | D) = [p(D | \theta^*) p(\theta^*)] / [p(D | \theta) p(\theta)]. We can compute relative probabilities anywhere in parameter space!

Detailed Balance and the Metropolis-Hastings Acceptance Rule: The algorithm walks through parameter space. At state \theta, it proposes a new state \theta^* ~ q(\theta^* | \theta). If \theta^* has a higher posterior density, we always move there. If \theta^* has a lower posterior density, we do not reject it outright—we accept it with probability equal to the density ratio! This ensures the chain explores the entire distribution without getting stuck at the mode, with the frequency of visits exactly proportional to the true posterior probability.

Gibbs Sampling: Full Conditionals: When parameter vector \boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_D) has high dimension, proposing random vectors often results in high rejection rates. Gibbs sampling updates parameters one coordinate at a time by sampling from the full conditional distribution p(\theta_j | \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}, D). Because the proposal matches the conditional target exactly, the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance ratio is identically 1.0!

In everyday terms

Imagine a blind cartographer mapping the topography of a mountainous island chain at night. The cartographer cannot see the whole island, but at any spot they stand, a handheld altimeter tells them their current elevation. To map the terrain, the cartographer picks a random direction and takes a step. If the new spot is higher, they step up. If the new spot is lower, they roll a die: if the elevation drop is small, they usually accept the step down; if it is a steep cliff, they reject it and stay put. Over months of walking, the fraction of hours spent at any altitude precisely matches the true surface area of the mountains.

03 The maths

  1. Detailed Balance Condition

    p(\theta) \, T(\theta \to \theta^*) = p(\theta^*) \, T(\theta^* \to \theta), \qquad T(\theta \to \theta^*) = q(\theta^* \mid \theta) \, \alpha(\theta, \theta^*)

    Detailed balance is a sufficient condition ensuring that the target posterior distribution p(\theta) is the unique stationary invariant distribution of the Markov chain. In equilibrium, probability flux from \theta to \theta^* exactly matches reverse flux.

  2. Metropolis-Hastings Acceptance Probability

    \alpha(\theta, \theta^*) = \min\left( 1, \; \frac{p(\theta^* \mid \mathcal{D}) \, q(\theta \mid \theta^*)}{p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \, q(\theta^* \mid \theta)} \right) = \min\left( 1, \; \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta^*) p(\theta^*) \, q(\theta \mid \theta^*)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) p(\theta) \, q(\theta^* \mid \theta)} \right)

    Because the normalising constant Z appears in both numerator and denominator, it cancels out completely. This allows sampling from arbitrary unnormalised distributions.

  3. Gibbs Sampling as a Special Case of Metropolis-Hastings

    q(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}, \mathcal{D}) \implies \alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = \min\left(1, \; \frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) \, p(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^*)}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j})} \right) \equiv 1.0

    When proposing each coordinate directly from its full conditional distribution, the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance ratio simplifies algebraically to exactly 1.0. Every proposed Gibbs step is accepted.

04 Key takeaways

  • MCMC draws representative samples from unnormalised posterior distributions where integration of the denominator is intractable.
  • Metropolis-Hastings accepts moves according to the posterior ratio, satisfying detailed balance to ensure convergence to the target distribution.
  • Gibbs sampling updates one variable at a time from its full conditional distribution, achieving a 100% acceptance rate.
  • Burn-in samples from early iterations before the chain reaches stationarity must be discarded prior to posterior inference.

05 In code

python
"""
Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs Sampling Implementations
Sampling from an unnormalised bivariate Gaussian posterior
and evaluating chain mixing and burn-in.
"""

import numpy as np

# Target Distribution: Bivariate Normal with correlation rho = 0.8
# Unnormalised log density: -0.5 * (1 / (1 - rho^2)) * (x1^2 - 2*rho*x1*x2 + x2^2)
rho = 0.8
var_scale = 1.0 - rho**2

def target_log_prob(theta):
    x1, x2 = theta[0], theta[1]
    return -0.5 * (x1**2 - 2.0 * rho * x1 * x2 + x2**2) / var_scale

# 1. Random Walk Metropolis-Hastings Sampler
def run_metropolis_hastings(num_samples=10000, proposal_std=0.6):
    chain = np.empty((num_samples, 2))
    curr_theta = np.array([5.0, -5.0])  # Start far from mode (testing convergence)
    curr_log_p = target_log_prob(curr_theta)
    accepted_count = 0

    for i in range(num_samples):
        # Propose candidate theta* ~ N(curr_theta, proposal_std^2)
        candidate = curr_theta + np.random.normal(0, proposal_std, size=2)
        candidate_log_p = target_log_prob(candidate)
        
        # Log acceptance ratio: log_alpha = candidate_log_p - curr_log_p
        log_alpha = candidate_log_p - curr_log_p
        
        if np.log(np.random.uniform(0, 1)) <= log_alpha:
            curr_theta = candidate
            curr_log_p = candidate_log_p
            accepted_count += 1
            
        chain[i] = curr_theta

    acc_rate = accepted_count / num_samples
    return chain, acc_rate

# 2. Gibbs Sampler
# Full Conditionals for Bivariate Normal:
# x1 | x2 ~ N(rho * x2, 1 - rho^2)
# x2 | x1 ~ N(rho * x1, 1 - rho^2)
def run_gibbs_sampler(num_samples=10000):
    chain = np.empty((num_samples, 2))
    x1, x2 = 5.0, -5.0
    cond_std = np.sqrt(1.0 - rho**2)

    for i in range(num_samples):
        # Sample x1 | x2
        x1 = np.random.normal(loc=rho * x2, scale=cond_std)
        # Sample x2 | x1
        x2 = np.random.normal(loc=rho * x1, scale=cond_std)
        chain[i] = [x1, x2]

    return chain

# Run simulations
np.random.seed(42)
num_steps = 10000
burn_in = 2000

mh_chain, mh_acc_rate = run_metropolis_hastings(num_steps, proposal_std=0.7)
gibbs_chain = run_gibbs_sampler(num_steps)

# Discard burn-in
mh_samples = mh_chain[burn_in:]
gibbs_samples = gibbs_chain[burn_in:]

print("=== 1. Metropolis-Hastings Results ===")
print(f"Total Steps: {num_steps} | Discarded Burn-in: {burn_in}")
print(f"Empirical Acceptance Rate: {mh_acc_rate:.1%} (Target optimal ~23-44%)")
print(f"Estimated Mean:   [{np.mean(mh_samples[:, 0]):.3f}, {np.mean(mh_samples[:, 1]):.3f}] (True: [0.00, 0.00])")
print(f"Estimated Corr:   {np.corrcoef(mh_samples[:, 0], mh_samples[:, 1])[0, 1]:.3f} (True: {rho:.3f})")

print("\n=== 2. Gibbs Sampler Results ===")
print("Acceptance Rate: 100.0% (by mathematical construction)")
print(f"Estimated Mean:   [{np.mean(gibbs_samples[:, 0]):.3f}, {np.mean(gibbs_samples[:, 1]):.3f}] (True: [0.00, 0.00])")
print(f"Estimated Corr:   {np.corrcoef(gibbs_samples[:, 0], gibbs_samples[:, 1])[0, 1]:.3f} (True: {rho:.3f})")

Common pitfalls

  • High autocorrelation and slow mixing: When proposal step size is too small, the chain explores slowly via Brownian diffusion; when too large, proposals are rejected repeatedly.
  • Premature stopping before convergence: A chain starting far from the true mode requires sufficient burn-in to find the typical set; Gelman-Rubin diagnostics across multiple chains are essential.
  • Gibbs sampling in highly correlated dimensions: When variables exhibit extreme correlation, coordinate-wise Gibbs sampling becomes trapped taking tiny zig-zag steps; reparameterisation or HMC is needed.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does the normalising constant Z cancel out in the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance ratio?

Why: The ratio p(\theta^* | \mathcal{D}) / p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) = [p(\mathcal{D}|\theta^*)p(\theta^*)/Z] / [p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)p(\theta)/Z] = [p(\mathcal{D}|\theta^*)p(\theta^*)] / [p(\mathcal{D}|\theta)p(\theta)], bypassing the intractable integral Z.

Q2What condition guarantees that a Markov chain converges to the desired target distribution?

Why: Detailed balance ensures the target distribution is stationary. Ergodicity guarantees the chain can reach any state and will not get trapped in periodic cycles.

Q3Why does Gibbs sampling achieve a 100% acceptance rate?

Why: Substituting the full conditional proposal q(\theta_j^* | \boldsymbol{\theta}) = p(\theta_j^* | \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}) into the MH formula cancels the numerator and denominator completely, yielding \alpha = 1.0.
