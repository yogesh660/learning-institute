Difference-in-differences

Difference-in-Differences (DiD) is a quasi-experimental econometric methodology that estimates causal effects by comparing the pre- and post-treatment changes in outcomes between a treatment group and an untreated control group. By subtracting the control group's temporal trend from the treatment group's observed change, DiD eliminates both time-invariant unobserved confounders (e.g. fixed regional culture or baseline ability) and shared macro-temporal shocks (e.g. economic recessions or nationwide seasonal trends). The foundational cornerstone of DiD validity is the Parallel Trends Assumption.

01 The idea

In 1992, New Jersey raised its state minimum wage while neighbouring Pennsylvania kept its minimum wage constant. Economists Card and Krueger famously evaluated this natural experiment: did employment fall as classical economic theory predicted? Simply comparing New Jersey to Pennsylvania after the law change ignores baseline state differences; comparing New Jersey before and after ignores national economic trends. By calculating the difference of the differences, Card and Krueger revolutionised empirical economics. How does DiD isolate causal effects across time, and what happens when the parallel trends assumption fails?

The 2x2 Grid: - Difference 1 (Within Treatment Group over Time): \Delta_Treat = Y_Treat,Post - Y_Treat,Pre. This captures both the true treatment effect AND any broader macro-temporal trend (inflation, seasons). - Difference 2 (Within Control Group over Time): \Delta_Control = Y_Control,Post - Y_Control,Pre. This captures the pure macro-temporal trend (since the control group never received treatment). - The Difference-in-Differences: \tau_DiD = \Delta_Treat - \Delta_Control. Subtracting the control trend cancels out the secular time trend, leaving only the genuine causal treatment effect!

The Parallel Trends Assumption: DiD does NOT require the treatment and control groups to have the same baseline outcome level! It only requires that, in the absence of treatment, both groups would have followed parallel trajectories over time. If New Jersey normally grew by 2% per year and Pennsylvania normally grew by 2% per year, any deviation from that parallel path after the policy change is attributed to the policy.

Event Study Designs and Placebo Tests: How can we test whether parallel trends holds? We inspect pre-treatment periods! An event study plots regression coefficients for periods t-3, t-2, t-1 before treatment. If pre-treatment coefficients are statistically indistinguishable from zero, the parallel trends assumption is credible.

In everyday terms

Imagine two fitness trainees, Alice and Bob. Alice is naturally fitter and can run 10 km/h, while Bob can run only 6 km/h. Alice starts taking a new nutrition supplement, and one month later she runs 13 km/h (+3 km/h). Bob does not take the supplement, but because both were training in the same warm summer weather, Bob improves to 7 km/h (+1 km/h). The DiD effect of the supplement is not 13 - 7 = 6 (naive cross-sectional), nor is it 13 - 10 = 3 (naive before-after). It is (13 - 10) - (7 - 6) = 3 - 1 = +2 km/h.

03 The maths

  1. The 2x2 Difference-in-Differences Estimator

    \hat{\tau}_{\text{DiD}} = \left( \bar{Y}_{\text{Treat, Post}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Treat, Pre}} \right) - \left( \bar{Y}_{\text{Control, Post}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Control, Pre}} \right)

    The sample estimator computes the difference in average growth rates between treatment and control cohorts across pre- and post-intervention epochs.

  2. Two-Way Interaction Regression Model

    y_{it} = \beta_0 + \gamma \, \text{Treat}_i + \lambda \, \text{Post}_t + \tau_{\text{DiD}} \left( \text{Treat}_i \times \text{Post}_t \right) + \epsilon_{it}

    Here \gamma captures time-invariant baseline differences between groups, \lambda captures time trends common to all units, and \tau_{\text{DiD}} captures the interaction identifying the true causal treatment effect.

  3. Counterfactual Parallel Trends Condition

    \mathbb{E}\big[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{is}(0) \mid \text{Treat}\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[Y_{it}(0) - Y_{is}(0) \mid \text{Control}\big] \quad \forall t > t_{\text{event}}, \; s < t_{\text{event}}

    The counterfactual expectation states that in the absence of treatment (potential outcome Y(0)), the expected change in outcome for the treated group would have exactly mirrored the observed change in the control group.

04 Key takeaways

  • Difference-in-Differences compares the change over time in a treated group to the change over time in a control group.
  • DiD eliminates time-invariant unobserved confounders and shared macro-temporal trends.
  • The validity of DiD depends on the Parallel Trends Assumption: in the absence of treatment, both groups would have evolved in parallel.
  • The interaction coefficient in a two-way regression model estimates the DiD causal effect directly.

05 In code

python
"""
Difference-in-Differences (DiD) Econometric Simulation
Demonstrates 2x2 DiD calculation, interaction regression,
and pre-trend parallel trajectory verification.
"""

import numpy as np

# Simulate a policy intervention across two regions (Region A = Treated, Region B = Control)
# Over 8 quarters: Quarters 1-4 are Pre-intervention, Quarters 5-8 are Post-intervention
# True Causal Effect of Policy = +15.0 units
np.random.seed(42)
n_per_group_per_qtr = 100
quarters = np.arange(1, 9)

# Baseline regional difference: Region A starts 20 units higher than Region B
base_a = 50.0
base_b = 30.0
trend_per_quarter = 2.5  # Common upward economic trend affecting both regions
true_policy_shock = 15.0 # Introduced at Quarter >= 5 for Region A only

data_records = []
for q in quarters:
    is_post = 1 if q >= 5 else 0
    # Region A (Treated)
    shock = true_policy_shock if is_post else 0.0
    y_a = base_a + (trend_per_quarter * q) + shock + np.random.normal(0, 5, n_per_group_per_qtr)
    for y in y_a:
        data_records.append({"y": y, "treat": 1, "post": is_post, "quarter": q})
        
    # Region B (Control: Never treated)
    y_b = base_b + (trend_per_quarter * q) + np.random.normal(0, 5, n_per_group_per_qtr)
    for y in y_b:
        data_records.append({"y": y, "treat": 0, "post": is_post, "quarter": q})

# Convert to arrays
y_all = np.array([r["y"] for r in data_records])
treat_all = np.array([r["treat"] for r in data_records])
post_all = np.array([r["post"] for r in data_records])
inter_all = treat_all * post_all

# 1. Classical 2x2 Cell Means
y_treat_post = np.mean(y_all[(treat_all == 1) & (post_all == 1)])
y_treat_pre  = np.mean(y_all[(treat_all == 1) & (post_all == 0)])
y_ctrl_post  = np.mean(y_all[(treat_all == 0) & (post_all == 1)])
y_ctrl_pre   = np.mean(y_all[(treat_all == 0) & (post_all == 0)])

diff_treat = y_treat_post - y_treat_pre
diff_ctrl  = y_ctrl_post - y_ctrl_pre
did_estimate_cell = diff_treat - diff_ctrl

print("=== 1. Classical 2x2 Cell Means Calculation ===")
print(f"Treatment Group: Pre = {y_treat_pre:5.2f} -> Post = {y_treat_post:5.2f} | Delta = {diff_treat:+5.2f}")
print(f"Control Group:   Pre = {y_ctrl_pre:5.2f} -> Post = {y_ctrl_post:5.2f} | Delta = {diff_ctrl:+5.2f}")
print(f"Difference-in-Differences Estimate: Delta_Treat - Delta_Ctrl = {did_estimate_cell:+.2f}")
print(f"True Causal Policy Effect:        +{true_policy_shock:.2f}\n")

# 2. Regression Interaction Model: Y = beta0 + gamma*Treat + lambda*Post + tau*(Treat * Post)
X_did = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y_all)), treat_all, post_all, inter_all])
beta_did = np.linalg.inv(X_did.T @ X_did) @ (X_did.T @ y_all)
resids = y_all - X_did @ beta_did
s2 = np.sum(resids**2) / (len(y_all) - 4)
se_did = np.sqrt(np.diagonal(s2 * np.linalg.inv(X_did.T @ X_did)))

print("=== 2. OLS Interaction Regression Model ===")
print(f"Intercept (beta0):              {beta_did[0]:6.2f} (SE: {se_did[0]:.3f})")
print(f"Region A Baseline (gamma):      {beta_did[1]:6.2f} (SE: {se_did[1]:.3f}) [Baseline Level Gap]")
print(f"Post-Period Time Trend (lambda):{beta_did[2]:6.2f} (SE: {se_did[2]:.3f}) [Macro Secular Trend]")
print(f"DiD Treatment Effect (tau):     {beta_did[3]:6.2f} (SE: {se_did[3]:.3f}) [CAUSAL SHOCK]")
print(f"\n95% Confidence Interval on Causal Effect: [{beta_did[3] - 1.96*se_did[3]:.2f}, {beta_did[3] + 1.96*se_did[3]:.2f}]")

Common pitfalls

  • Failing to test parallel pre-trends: If treatment and control groups had different growth trajectories prior to treatment, DiD conflates pre-existing divergent trends with the treatment effect.
  • Serial autocorrelation in standard errors: Ignoring autocorrelation across repeated time periods in panel data leads to severe underestimation of standard errors; cluster-robust standard errors are essential.
  • Staggered rollout bias in Two-Way Fixed Effects: When units adopt treatment at different times, traditional TWFE can assign negative weights to earlier-treated units, requiring modern staggered DiD estimators (Callaway & Sant'Anna).

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the crucial assumption required for Difference-in-Differences to identify a valid causal effect?

Why: DiD allows baseline levels to differ arbitrarily (\gamma). Its validity relies solely on parallel trajectories: that the unobserved counterfactual trend in the treated group equals the observed trend in the control group.

Q2How does Difference-in-Differences remove time-invariant omitted variable bias?

Why: Any factor that is constant over time (such as geographic geography or historical culture) is identical in pre- and post-periods; taking the first difference (Y_post - Y_pre) cancels it out completely.

Q3In the regression model Y = beta0 + beta1*Treat + beta2*Post + beta3*(Treat * Post), which coefficient estimates the DiD causal effect?

Why: The interaction term Treat * Post is 1 only for treated units in the post-treatment period; its coefficient beta3 measures the differential lift above the baseline group gap and macro time trend.
