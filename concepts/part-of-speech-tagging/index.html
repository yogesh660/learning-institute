Part-of-speech tagging

Part-of-speech (POS) tagging is the foundational NLP task of assigning grammatical word classes (such as noun, verb, adjective, or preposition) to each token in a sentence. Because most common words are polysemous and syntactically ambiguous (e.g. "run" as a verb or noun, "flies" as a noun or verb), POS taggers resolve ambiguity by modelling local lexical emissions alongside grammatical transitions using rule-based heuristics, Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), or neural sequence models.

01 The idea

Consider the classic ambiguous sentence: "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana." In the first half, "flies" is an active verb and "like" is a preposition. In the second half, "flies" is a plural noun (the insect Drosophila) and "like" is a verb expressing enjoyment. A naive dictionary lookup fails because human language is saturated with morphosyntactic ambiguity. Part-of-speech tagging resolves this by balancing what a word looks like against the grammatical rhythm of the words around it.

Open vs closed grammatical word classes: Human language consists of two distinct categories of words. Open classes—nouns, lexical verbs, adjectives, and adverbs—carry core semantic content and welcome new additions freely (e.g. "selfie", "googleable"). Closed classes—pronouns, determiners, conjunctions, and prepositions—are structural function words with small, fixed inventories (e.g. "the", "of", "and"). Closed class words rarely change POS, while open class words frequently shift between nouns, verbs, and modifiers depending on context.

The dance between lexical emissions and grammatical transitions: A POS tagger balances two probabilistic forces: emission probability P(word | tag) and transition probability P(tag_i | tag_{i-1}). The emission probability asks: "How likely is the English noun class to emit the specific word 'flies'?" The transition probability asks: "In English grammar, how often does a determiner directly precede another determiner, or a noun precede a verb?" Even if "flies" is most frequently a verb in general usage, if it is preceded by "the fruit", the syntactic transition makes noun far more probable.

Why POS tagging unlocks downstream NLP pipelines: POS tags act as essential grammatical signposts. Lemmatisation algorithms need POS tags to know whether "saw" should reduce to the base verb "see" or the noun "saw". Speech synthesis engines use POS tags to choose correct phonetics (e.g. "produce" stressed on the first syllable as a noun versus the second syllable as a verb). In dependency parsing and information extraction, POS tags prune the search space of grammatical relations by orders of magnitude.

In everyday terms

Imagine you are an orchestra conductor listening to an unfamiliar musical piece. Some notes are identical in pitch (like ambiguous words), but their musical function changes completely based on harmony and sequence: a C-major chord can serve as a triumphant tonic home base or a tense transitional pivot. You do not identify a chord in isolation; you evaluate the notes being played right now (lexical emission) alongside the harmonic chord progression that led up to it and where it resolves next (grammatical transition). POS tagging conducts an orchestra of words, ensuring every token plays its proper grammatical role in the syntactic symphony.

03 The maths

  1. Probabilistic sequence formulation (MAP decoding)

    \hat{T} = \arg\max_{T \in \mathcal{Y}^T} P(T \mid W) = \arg\max_{T} P(W \mid T) P(T)

    Here $W = (w_1, \dots, w_T)$ is the observed token sequence, and $T = (t_1, \dots, t_T)$ is the sequence of grammatical tags. Because $P(W)$ is constant across all candidate tag sequences, decoding maximises the product of prior and emission probabilities.

  2. First-order Hidden Markov Model factorisation

    P(W, T) \approx \prod_{i=1}^T P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}) \cdot P(w_i \mid t_i)

    Under the bigram Markov assumption, tag $t_i$ depends strictly on immediate predecessor $t_{i-1}$, and word $w_i$ depends strictly on current tag $t_i$, decomposing the joint probability into local transition and emission terms.

  3. Viterbi dynamic programming recurrence

    v_t(j) = \max_{k \in \mathcal{Y}} \left[ v_{t-1}(k) \cdot P(t_j \mid t_k) \right] \cdot P(w_t \mid t_j)

    Here $v_t(j)$ represents the highest probability of any valid tag prefix of length $t$ terminating in tag state $j$. Storing backpointers enables exact sequence recovery in $O(T \cdot |\mathcal{Y}|^2)$ polynomial time instead of exponential search.

04 Key takeaways

  • Part-of-speech tagging assigns grammatical categories to words to disambiguate morphosyntactic function in context.
  • Words are heavily polysemous: common words like "run", "flies", and "bank" shift between nouns and verbs based on adjacent syntax.
  • The first-order Hidden Markov Model balances tag transition probabilities P(t_i | t_{i-1}) against lexical emission probabilities P(w_i | t_i).
  • The Viterbi algorithm uses dynamic programming to discover the globally optimal tag sequence in O(T * |Y|^2) polynomial time.
  • Universal Dependencies provides a 17-tag cross-linguistic standard that harmonises morphosyntactic categories across human languages.

05 In code

python
import math
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

class HMMPOSTagger:
    """
    Bigram Hidden Markov Model (HMM) Part-of-Speech Tagger
    with Laplace smoothing and log-space Viterbi decoding.
    """
    def __init__(self):
        self.tags = set()
        self.vocab = set()
        self.transitions = defaultdict(Counter)
        self.emissions = defaultdict(Counter)
        self.tag_counts = Counter()
        self.start_tag = "<START>"
        
    def train(self, tagged_sentences):
        for sentence in tagged_sentences:
            prev_tag = self.start_tag
            self.tag_counts[prev_tag] += 1
            
            for word, tag in sentence:
                word_clean = word.lower()
                self.tags.add(tag)
                self.vocab.add(word_clean)
                
                self.transitions[prev_tag][tag] += 1
                self.emissions[tag][word_clean] += 1
                self.tag_counts[tag] += 1
                prev_tag = tag

    def _log_transition(self, prev_t, curr_t, alpha=0.1):
        count = self.transitions[prev_t][curr_t]
        total = self.tag_counts[prev_t]
        prob = (count + alpha) / (total + alpha * len(self.tags))
        return math.log(prob)

    def _log_emission(self, tag, word, beta=0.01):
        word_clean = word.lower()
        count = self.emissions[tag][word_clean]
        total = self.tag_counts[tag]
        vocab_len = len(self.vocab) if len(self.vocab) > 0 else 1
        prob = (count + beta) / (total + beta * vocab_len)
        return math.log(prob)

    def tag(self, sentence_tokens):
        T = len(sentence_tokens)
        if T == 0:
            return []
            
        tag_list = sorted(list(self.tags))
        viterbi = [{} for _ in range(T)]
        backpointer = [{} for _ in range(T)]
        
        w0 = sentence_tokens[0]
        for tag in tag_list:
            trans_p = self._log_transition(self.start_tag, tag)
            emit_p = self._log_emission(tag, w0)
            viterbi[0][tag] = trans_p + emit_p
            backpointer[0][tag] = None
            
        for t in range(1, T):
            wt = sentence_tokens[t]
            for curr_t in tag_list:
                emit_p = self._log_emission(curr_t, wt)
                
                best_prev_t = None
                best_prob = -float('inf')
                for prev_t in tag_list:
                    p = viterbi[t-1][prev_t] + self._log_transition(prev_t, curr_t) + emit_p
                    if p > best_prob:
                        best_prob = p
                        best_prev_t = prev_t
                        
                viterbi[t][curr_t] = best_prob
                backpointer[t][curr_t] = best_prev_t
                
        best_last_tag = max(tag_list, key=lambda tag: viterbi[T-1][tag])
        
        best_tags = [best_last_tag]
        curr = best_last_tag
        for t in range(T - 1, 0, -1):
            curr = backpointer[t][curr]
            best_tags.append(curr)
            
        best_tags.reverse()
        return list(zip(sentence_tokens, best_tags))

# Sample corpus with ambiguous words: "flies", "can", "bank"
train_data = [
    [("the", "DET"), ("fruit", "NOUN"), ("flies", "NOUN"), ("eat", "VERB"), ("apples", "NOUN")],
    [("time", "NOUN"), ("flies", "VERB"), ("like", "ADP"), ("an", "DET"), ("arrow", "NOUN")],
    [("they", "PRON"), ("can", "AUX"), ("fish", "VERB"), ("in", "ADP"), ("the", "DET"), ("river", "NOUN")],
    [("he", "PRON"), ("opened", "VERB"), ("a", "DET"), ("can", "NOUN"), ("of", "ADP"), ("tuna", "NOUN")],
    [("the", "DET"), ("bank", "NOUN"), ("approved", "VERB"), ("the", "DET"), ("loan", "NOUN")],
    [("birds", "NOUN"), ("can", "AUX"), ("bank", "VERB"), ("steeply", "ADV"), ("in", "ADP"), ("flight", "NOUN")]
]

tagger = HMMPOSTagger()
tagger.train(train_data)

test_sentences = [
    ["time", "flies"],
    ["fruit", "flies"],
    ["can", "they", "bank"]
]

print("HMM POS Tagging Results:")
for sent in test_sentences:
    prediction = tagger.tag(sent)
    res_str = " ".join([f"{w}/{t}" for w, t in prediction])
    print(f"  Input: '{' '.join(sent)}' -> {res_str}")

Common pitfalls

  • Greedy local decoding: Picking the highest-probability tag at each token independently ignores downstream grammatical constraints, generating ungrammatical sequences like determiners followed directly by finite verbs.
  • Floating-point probability underflow: Multiplying dozens of small probabilities between 0 and 1 causes numeric underflow to zero. Always compute Viterbi in log-space: log(a * b) = log(a) + log(b).
  • Zero emission probabilities on out-of-vocabulary tokens: Encountering an unseen word without morphological smoothing collapses the entire sequence probability to zero. Always implement fallback subword smoothing.
  • Relying solely on overall accuracy: Because trivial function words ("the", "is") inflate overall token accuracy to >92%, always evaluate ambiguous word accuracy and out-of-vocabulary accuracy separately.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is the Viterbi algorithm used instead of exhaustively enumerating every possible part-of-speech sequence?

Why: For a tagset of size |Y| and sentence length T, there are |Y|^T distinct paths. For 40 tags and 20 tokens, 40^20 is astronomical. Viterbi collapses identical prefix subproblems into T * |Y|^2 operations.

Q2In the sentence "The sailor can can the tuna", how should the two occurrences of "can" be tagged morphosyntactically?

Why: In English grammar, modal auxiliaries like "can" precede a base verb. Here, "can" serves first as an auxiliary expressing ability, followed immediately by the lexical verb "can" meaning to preserve or package into tins.

Q3What makes discriminative Conditional Random Fields (CRFs) superior to generative Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) for POS tagging?

Why: HMMs assume emissions are conditionally independent given the tag. Incorporating rich feature conjunctions violates HMM assumptions. CRFs model P(T | W) directly, supporting arbitrary overlapping feature functions.
