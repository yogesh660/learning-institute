Joint, marginal and conditional distributions

Multivariate probability distributions formalise the simultaneous behaviour of multiple random variables. The joint distribution P(X=x, Y=y) captures all co-occurrence probabilities. Marginalisation collapses dimensions by integrating or summing out nuisance variables, while conditioning isolates the distribution of one variable given fixed observations of another via Bayes' rule P(X | Y) = P(X, Y) / P(Y). Understanding the decomposition of joint spaces into marginals and conditionals is fundamental to probabilistic graphical models, Markov decision processes, and multivariate density estimation.

01 The idea

When working with tabular datasets, image channels, or sensor networks, variables rarely operate in isolation. How does knowing an applicant's credit score alter the probability distribution of their loan default risk, and how can we recover the standalone distribution of default risk across the entire population? Joint distributions capture complete multivariate reality, while marginalisation and conditioning allow us to slice and project high-dimensional uncertainty down to actionable inferences.

The 2D Table of Reality: Imagine a 2D contingency grid where columns represent education levels and rows represent income brackets. The joint distribution assigns a probability to every individual cell (x, y), such that all cell probabilities sum to 1.

Marginalisation (Collapsing Dimensions): If you only want to know the distribution of income regardless of education, you sum horizontally across all columns. You are projecting the 2D grid onto the vertical margin—hence the term "marginal distribution". Mathematically, marginalisation integrates out the unwanted variable: P(X) = sum_y P(X, y).

Conditioning (Slicing the Distribution): If you learn a specific candidate holds a PhD, you discard every column except the "PhD" column. You take that single column slice, but because its cells do not sum to 1, you normalise by dividing by the marginal probability of having a PhD. That renormalised slice is the conditional distribution P(Income | Education = PhD).

Independence and Covariance: Two variables are statistically independent if and only if knowing one yields zero information about the other: P(X, Y) = P(X)P(Y), which implies P(Y | X) = P(Y). When variables are dependent, conditioning reshapes the distribution, shifting both its conditional expectation and conditional variance.

In everyday terms

Think of an aerial heat map of a crowded festival park where altitude represents the joint probability of finding a person at GPS coordinate (x, y). The marginal distribution of the east-west axis X is the silhouette of the crowd projected onto a northern wall: you look from the side and sum up everyone at that longitude regardless of latitude. The conditional distribution given latitude Y=y0 is like putting up a physical laser tape along latitude y0 and examining only the crowd profile walking along that exact east-west path, normalised so total presence sums to 100%.

03 The maths

  1. Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Probability Densities

    f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \, dy, \qquad f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_X(x)} \quad \text{for } f_X(x) > 0

    For continuous random variables, the marginal density f_X(x) integrates the joint density f_{X,Y}(x, y) across all possible values of y. The conditional density f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) represents the cross-sectional slice of the joint density at x, normalised so that \int f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \, dy = 1.

  2. Law of Total Expectation and Total Variance (Eve's Law)

    \mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\Big], \qquad \text{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\text{Var}(Y \mid X)\Big] + \text{Var}\Big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\Big)

    Eve's Law decomposes the total variance of Y into two orthogonal components: the expected value of the conditional variance (unexplained within-group variance or noise) plus the variance of the conditional expectation (explained between-group variance). This identity forms the foundational mathematics of ANOVA and decision tree split criteria.

  3. Bivariate Gaussian Conditional Distribution

    Y \mid (X = x) \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mu_Y + \rho \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X), \; \sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2) \right)

    When X and Y follow a joint bivariate normal distribution with correlation \rho, the conditional distribution of Y given X=x remains strictly Gaussian. Its conditional mean shifts linearly with x, and its conditional variance shrinks by a factor of (1 - \rho^2), demonstrating how conditioning reduces uncertainty.

04 Key takeaways

  • The joint distribution specifies the simultaneous probability of all variable configurations across the multidimensional state space.
  • Marginalisation collapses dimensions by integrating or summing out unobserved nuisance variables.
  • Conditioning takes a slice along an observed variable and renormalises it by dividing by that variable's marginal probability.
  • Eve's Law of Total Variance decomposes total variance into expected conditional variance (within-group noise) plus variance of conditional expectation (between-group signal).

05 In code

python
"""
Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Distributions
Demonstrates discrete joint probability tables, marginal collapse,
conditional slicing, and verification of Eve's Law of Total Variance.
"""

import numpy as np

# 1. Define a 2D discrete joint distribution table P(Income, Education)
# Rows: Income Levels (Low, Medium, High)
# Columns: Education Levels (HighSchool, Bachelors, Postgraduate)
joint_table = np.array([
    [0.18, 0.08, 0.02],  # Low Income
    [0.12, 0.25, 0.13],  # Medium Income
    [0.02, 0.07, 0.13]   # High Income
])

income_levels = ["Low", "Medium", "High"]
income_values = np.array([25000, 60000, 120000])  # Representative numeric values in GBP
education_levels = ["HighSchool", "Bachelors", "Postgraduate"]

print("=== 1. Joint Probability Table P(Income, Education) ===")
print("Total Joint Sum (must equal 1.0):", round(np.sum(joint_table), 4))
for i, inc in enumerate(income_levels):
    row_str = "  " + f"{inc:6s} | " + " ".join(f"{joint_table[i, j]:6.2f}" for j in range(3))
    print(row_str)

# 2. Marginal Distributions
p_education = np.sum(joint_table, axis=0)  # Sum down rows (marginal education)
p_income = np.sum(joint_table, axis=1)     # Sum across columns (marginal income)

print("\n=== 2. Marginal Distributions ===")
for j, edu in enumerate(education_levels):
    print(f"  P(Education = {edu:12s}) = {p_education[j]:.3f}")

print("")
for i, inc in enumerate(income_levels):
    print(f"  P(Income = {inc:6s}) = {p_income[i]:.3f}")

# 3. Conditional Distributions P(Income | Education)
# For each column j, divide cell by p_education[j]
cond_income_given_edu = joint_table / p_education[np.newaxis, :]

print("\n=== 3. Conditional Distributions P(Income | Education) ===")
for j, edu in enumerate(education_levels):
    print(f"P(Income | Education = {edu}):")
    for i, inc in enumerate(income_levels):
        print(f"  P({inc:6s} | {edu}) = {cond_income_given_edu[i, j]:.3f}")

# 4. Conditional Expectations and Eve's Law Verification
cond_means = np.sum(income_values[:, np.newaxis] * cond_income_given_edu, axis=0)
cond_vars = np.sum(((income_values[:, np.newaxis] - cond_means)**2) * cond_income_given_edu, axis=0)

overall_mean = np.sum(income_values * p_income)
overall_var = np.sum(((income_values - overall_mean)**2) * p_income)

expected_cond_var = np.sum(cond_vars * p_education)     # E[Var(Y|X)]
var_of_cond_mean = np.sum(((cond_means - overall_mean)**2) * p_education)  # Var(E[Y|X])

print("\n=== 4. Verification of Eve's Law of Total Variance ===")
print(f"Overall Marginal Mean Income: £{overall_mean:,.2f}")
for j, edu in enumerate(education_levels):
    print(f"  E[Income | {edu:12s}] = £{cond_means[j]:,.2f} | Std = £{np.sqrt(cond_vars[j]):,.2f}")

print(f"\nTotal Population Variance:          {overall_var:,.0f}")
print(f"E[Var(Income | Education)] (Within): {expected_cond_var:,.0f}")
print(f"Var(E[Income | Education]) (Between):{var_of_cond_mean:,.0f}")
print(f"Within + Between Sum:               {expected_cond_var + var_of_cond_mean:,.0f}")
print("Eve's Law Holds Exactly:", np.isclose(overall_var, expected_cond_var + var_of_cond_mean))

Common pitfalls

  • Confusing P(X | Y) with P(Y | X): Conditional probabilities are directional and asymmetric; inverting them requires Bayes' theorem.
  • Forgetting to renormalise conditional slices: A raw row or column of a joint table sums to the marginal probability, not 1; dividing by the marginal is mandatory.
  • Assuming zero covariance implies independence: Covariance measures only linear relationships; non-linear dependencies can have zero covariance while remaining strongly dependent.

06 Check yourself

Q1How is a marginal distribution P(X) obtained from a discrete joint distribution P(X, Y)?

Why: Marginalisation integrates or sums over all states of the unobserved variable, collapsing the joint distribution onto the marginal axis.

Q2According to Eve's Law of Total Variance, what two components comprise the total variance Var(Y)?

Why: Eve's Law states Var(Y) = E[Var(Y|X)] + Var(E[Y|X]), decomposing overall variance into unexplained within-group dispersion and explained between-group variation.

Q3Under a bivariate normal distribution, how does the conditional variance Var(Y | X=x) compare to the marginal variance Var(Y)?

Why: For bivariate Gaussians, Var(Y | X=x) = sigma_Y^2 * (1 - rho^2). Unless rho=0, observing X strictly reduces uncertainty about Y, independent of the value of x.
