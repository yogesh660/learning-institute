Hallucination and grounding

Hallucination in Large Language Models refers to the generation of fluent, syntactically convincing text that is factually false, unfaithful to provided source material, or entirely fabricated. Rooted in the disconnect between autoregressive next-token probability maximisation and objective truth, hallucinations pose critical safety and reliability hazards in legal, medical, and technical domains. Grounding techniques—such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), Chain-of-Verification (CoVe), and consistency sampling—mitigate ungrounded fabrications.

01 The idea

In 2023, two lawyers submitted a legal brief to a federal judge in New York citing over half a dozen precedent cases complete with detailed quotes, docket numbers, and judicial opinions. The judge discovered that none of the cases existed: they had been completely fabricated by a generative AI assistant. The prose was immaculate, the legal terminology was flawless, but the facts were entirely imaginary. Why do Large Language Models hallucinate, and how do we anchor them to verifiable reality?

Likelihood versus Truth: The root cause of hallucination: An autoregressive language model is trained to minimise cross-entropy loss: predicting which token is most statistically likely to follow preceding tokens across web text. It has no built-in database of verified facts, no concept of physical reality, and no internal truth sensor. If a hallucinated fact sounds grammatically plausible and statistically aligns with common phrasing patterns, the model will output it with supreme confidence.

Factuality vs Faithfulness hallucinations: - Factuality Hallucination: The model generates claims that contradict real-world objective facts (e.g. claiming Neil Armstrong walked on Mars in 1975). - Faithfulness (Ungrounded) Hallucination: In summarisation or RAG setups where a reference document is provided, the model generates statements that contradict the source or introduces extraneous claims unsupported by the text.

The Mechanisms of Grounding: Grounding anchors language generation to external, verifiable sources of truth: 1. RAG Grounding: Forcing the model to condition its answers strictly on retrieved passages provided in the prompt context. 2. Chain-of-Verification (CoVe): A four-step self-correction pipeline: generate baseline response, draft independent factual verification questions, answer questions factually without seeing the baseline, and rewrite the final response reconciling discrepancies. 3. SelfCheckGPT: Measuring self-consistency across stochastic generations: if a fact is firmly grounded in the model's knowledge, multiple sampled generations will agree; if it is hallucinated, alternative samples will contradict each other.

In everyday terms

Think of a brilliant, charismatic storyteller at a dinner party who has read thousands of books but suffers from slight memory gaps. If you ask them about the history of Antarctic exploration, they tell a gripping, cinematic story about Captain Robert Scott. But when their memory reaches a minor factual blank—like the name of a specific supply ship—they do not pause to say "I do not know". Because their primary instinct is to maintain a seamless, entertaining narrative, their brain effortlessly invents a believable ship name on the fly without breaking conversational stride. Grounding is like putting an encyclopedic history book in front of the storyteller and making them point to the exact printed sentence before speaking.

03 The maths

  1. SelfCheckGPT sampling consistency score

    \mathcal{S}_{\text{hallucination}}(s_i) = 1 - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \mathbb{I}\left( R_k \models s_i \right)

    Here $s_i$ is a sentence from the primary generation, $R_1, \dots, R_N$ are independently sampled stochastic response variants, and $\models$ denotes semantic entailment evaluated by a natural language inference model. High scores indicate factual inconsistency across samples.

  2. Attribution fidelity ratio

    \operatorname{Fidelity}(Y, \mathcal{C}) = \frac{\sum_{s \in Y} \mathbb{I}(s \text{ is fully entailed by } \mathcal{C}_{c(s)})}{|Y|}

    Here $Y$ is the set of generated sentences, $\mathcal{C}_{c(s)}$ is the specific context chunk cited by sentence $s$. Full fidelity requires that every asserted claim is strictly entailed by its cited source, penalising ungrounded extrapolation.

  3. Calibration error across factual confidence

    \operatorname{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \operatorname{acc}(B_m) - \operatorname{conf}(B_m) \right|

    Expected Calibration Error measures the gap between the model's linguistic confidence $\operatorname{conf}(B_m)$ and its actual factual accuracy $\operatorname{acc}(B_m)$ across probability bins $B_m$. Poorly calibrated models exhibit high confidence on false assertions.

04 Key takeaways

  • Hallucination is the generation of fluent, syntactically coherent text that is factually false or unfaithful to source context.
  • Hallucinations stem from the fundamental divergence between probabilistic next-token prediction and verified real-world truth.
  • Factuality hallucinations contradict objective world facts; faithfulness hallucinations introduce ungrounded claims in contextual tasks.
  • Grounding techniques anchor model generations to retrieved context documents using strict prompt constraints and citation checks.
  • Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) and SelfCheckGPT use multi-turn verification and sample consistency to detect and correct fabrications.

05 In code

python
class GroundingValidator:
    """
    Simulation of grounding validation and factual consistency
    checking between source context and generated claims.
    """
    @staticmethod
    def check_lexical_grounding(context, claim_sentence):
        """
        Evaluate token grounding overlap ratio between source context
        and generated claim sentence.
        """
        import re
        ctx_words = set(re.findall(r'\b[a-zA-Z]+\b', context.lower()))
        claim_words = re.findall(r'\b[a-zA-Z]+\b', claim_sentence.lower())
        
        # Stop words to ignore in grounding ratio
        stop_words = {"the", "a", "an", "is", "was", "in", "on", "at", "to", "and", "of"}
        content_words = [w for w in claim_words if w not in stop_words]
        
        if not content_words:
            return 1.0, []
            
        grounded_count = sum(1 for w in content_words if w in ctx_words)
        ungrounded = [w for w in content_words if w not in ctx_words]
        ratio = grounded_count / len(content_words)
        return ratio, ungrounded

# Grounding reference document
context_doc = (
    "Apollo 11 was the American spaceflight that first landed humans on the Moon. "
    "Commander Neil Armstrong and Lunar Module Pilot Buzz Aldrin landed the Apollo Lunar Module Eagle "
    "on July 20, 1969. Armstrong became the first person to step onto the lunar surface six hours later."
)

# Test generated claims
claims = [
    "Neil Armstrong stepped onto the lunar surface in July 1969.",
    "Buzz Aldrin landed the Apollo Lunar Module Eagle on the Moon.",
    "Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin explored Martian craters in 1975."  # Hallucinated!
]

print("Grounding Reference Context:")
print(" ", context_doc)
print("\nEvaluating Generated Claims:")

validator = GroundingValidator()
for i, claim in enumerate(claims, 1):
    ratio, ungrounded_tokens = validator.check_lexical_grounding(context_doc, claim)
    is_grounded = ratio >= 0.85
    status = "GROUNDED" if is_grounded else "HALLUCINATION DETECTED"
    
    print(f"\nClaim {i}: '{claim}'")
    print(f"  Status: {status} (Grounding Score: {ratio * 100:.1f}%)")
    if ungrounded_tokens:
        print(f"  Ungrounded entities/tokens: {ungrounded_tokens}")

Common pitfalls

  • Assuming high linguistic confidence implies factual accuracy: Large language models use authoritative, confident tone regardless of whether they are stating historical truth or complete fantasy. Never treat confident phrasing as proof of truth.
  • Sycophancy and user bias confirmation: When a user asks a leading question containing a false premise (e.g. 'Why did Thomas Jefferson invent the telephone?'), models often confirm the false premise rather than correcting the user.
  • Contextual hallucination in long documents (Lost in the Middle): When relevant grounding facts are buried deep in the middle of long multi-thousand-token prompts, models often overlook them and default to parametric hallucination.
  • Fabricated citations: Models prompted to provide references frequently hallucinate fictitious academic papers with believable authors and fabricated DOI links. Always verify citations using external search tools.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the foundational theoretical reason why autoregressive Large Language Models produce hallucinations?

Why: Language models predict tokens based on statistical likelihood in training data. A hallucinated fact that uses common grammatical phrasing and high-probability idioms has high likelihood under the cross-entropy objective, despite being completely false.

Q2How does the Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) framework reduce hallucinated claims in generated responses?

Why: Dhuliawala et al. designed CoVe to break generation into four steps: draft generation, verification question planning, independent fact-checking, and final revision. Answering verification questions independently prevents the model from defending its own initial mistakes.

Q3What does a high score in the SelfCheckGPT consistency metric indicate regarding a generated sentence?

Why: SelfCheckGPT operates on the principle of self-consistency: when a model generates true knowledge, alternative stochastic samples agree. When a model hallucinates, alternative random generations produce conflicting, divergent facts, signaling hallucination.
