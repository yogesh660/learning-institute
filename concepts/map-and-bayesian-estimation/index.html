MAP and Bayesian estimation

Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) and full Bayesian estimation extend classical point estimation by incorporating prior probability distributions over model parameters. While Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) seeks parameters that maximise data likelihood alone, MAP identifies the single mode of the posterior distribution, functioning as a Bayesian bridge to penalised regularisation (such as L2 ridge and L1 lasso). Full Bayesian estimation rejects single point estimates entirely, retaining the complete posterior probability distribution p(\theta | D) to compute posterior predictive distributions with principled parameter uncertainty.

01 The idea

What happens when you flip a coin 3 times and get 3 heads? Maximum Likelihood Estimation insists the coin has a 100% chance of landing heads on every flip. Intuitively, we know coins are usually fair; our prior belief anchors our judgment against extreme conclusions from small samples. How do we formalise prior domain knowledge into statistical estimators, and what is the crucial distinction between choosing a single "best" parameter (MAP) versus maintaining a full distribution over all possible parameters (Bayesian estimation)?

MLE vs MAP vs Full Bayes: - Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): Seeks argmax_theta p(D | theta). It considers only the observed dataset. On small or sparse samples, it severely overfits, placing zero probability on unobserved events. - Maximum A Posteriori (MAP): Seeks argmax_theta p(theta | D) = argmax_theta [p(D | theta) p(theta)]. It combines data evidence with a prior belief distribution, yielding a single optimal point estimate (the posterior mode). - Full Bayesian Estimation: Rejects single point estimates entirely. It maintains the entire posterior distribution p(theta | D). When predicting a new outcome y*, it integrates over all possible values of theta, weighted by their posterior credibility.

The Secret Identity of Regularisation: In machine learning, L2 weight decay (Ridge regression) and L1 penalty (Lasso regression) are often presented as heuristic loss penalties. In reality, MAP estimation reveals their true origin: an L2 penalty is mathematically identical to assuming an independent zero-mean Gaussian prior on weights, while an L1 penalty is identical to assuming a Laplacian prior. Regularisation is literally MAP estimation in disguise!

Epistemic vs Aleatoric Uncertainty: Point estimators (both MLE and MAP) cannot express epistemic uncertainty (uncertainty stemming from lack of training data). Full Bayesian estimation explicitly tracks epistemic uncertainty: when data is scarce, the posterior distribution is wide, yielding broad prediction intervals that widen safely in uncharted feature space.

In everyday terms

Imagine a jury deliberating on a criminal trial. An MLE juror considers only the physical evidence presented in the courtroom, ignoring whether the defendant has a pristine record or a history of identical crimes. A MAP juror combines the evidence with their background knowledge of human behaviour to pick the single verdict they feel is most probable. A full Bayesian deliberation does not pick a single juror's hunch; it polls all possible interpretations, weighting every plausible scenario by how well it fits both the evidence and prior reality to arrive at a calibrated distribution of reasonable doubt.

03 The maths

  1. MAP Objective and Log-Posterior Formulation

    \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(x_i \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta) \right\} = \arg\min_\theta \Big\{ \text{NLL}(\theta) - \log p(\theta) \Big\}

    MAP estimation maximises the joint probability of data and parameters. Minimising negative log-likelihood with a negative log-prior penalty demonstrates the direct equivalence between MAP estimation and regularised loss minimisation.

  2. MAP Equivalence to L2 Regularisation (Gaussian Prior)

    \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_0^2) \implies \log p(\theta) = -\frac{\|\theta\|_2^2}{2\sigma_0^2} - C \implies \mathcal{L}_{\text{MAP}}(\theta) = \text{NLL}(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \|\theta\|_2^2, \quad \lambda = \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}

    Assuming an independent zero-mean normal prior over model parameters produces a quadratic penalty identical to Ridge regression (L2 weight decay). Smaller prior variance \sigma_0^2 corresponds to stronger regularisation hyperparameter \lambda.

  3. Posterior Predictive Distribution (Full Bayesian)

    p(y^* \mid \mathcal{D}) = \int_\Theta p(y^* \mid \theta) \, p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \, d\theta, \qquad \text{where } p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) p(\theta)}{\int_\Theta p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta') p(\theta') \, d\theta'}

    Instead of committing to a single point estimate \hat{\theta}, full Bayesian inference integrates across all parameter possibilities. The marginalisation accounts for parameter uncertainty, avoiding overconfident predictions in regions with sparse data.

04 Key takeaways

  • MLE finds the parameter value that maximises data likelihood alone, which overfits severely on small samples.
  • MAP incorporates a prior distribution, finding the posterior mode; it is mathematically equivalent to penalised regularisation (L2 = Gaussian prior, L1 = Laplace prior).
  • Full Bayesian estimation avoids single point estimates, maintaining the full posterior distribution and integrating over parameter uncertainty for predictions.
  • As sample size N approaches infinity, the data likelihood dominates the prior, causing MLE, MAP, and Bayesian posterior mean to converge to the same value.

05 In code

python
"""
Comparison of Maximum Likelihood (MLE), Maximum A Posteriori (MAP),
and Full Bayesian Estimation on a Small Bernoulli Sample.
"""

import numpy as np

# Scenario: We flip a coin N=5 times and observe k=4 heads.
# We want to estimate theta (probability of heads).
N = 5
k = 4  # 4 heads out of 5 flips

# 1. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
# theta_MLE = k / N
theta_mle = k / N

# 2. Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) Estimation with Beta(alpha, beta) Prior
# Suppose our prior belief is centered at a fair coin: Beta(alpha=3, beta=3)
# (equivalent to having seen 2 virtual heads and 2 virtual tails prior to the experiment)
alpha_prior = 3.0
beta_prior = 3.0

# Posterior is Beta(alpha + k, beta + N - k)
alpha_post = alpha_prior + k
beta_post = beta_prior + (N - k)

# Mode of Beta(a, b) for a, b > 1 is (a - 1) / (a + b - 2)
theta_map = (alpha_post - 1) / (alpha_post + beta_post - 2)

# 3. Full Bayesian Estimation (Posterior Mean and Credible Interval)
# Posterior Mean E[theta | D] = a / (a + b)
theta_bayes_mean = alpha_post / (alpha_post + beta_post)

# 4. Posterior Predictive Distribution for next flip being Heads
# P(Y* = 1 | D) = int theta * p(theta | D) d theta = E[theta | D]
p_next_heads_mle = theta_mle
p_next_heads_map = theta_map
p_next_heads_bayes = theta_bayes_mean

print(f"=== Experiment: {k} Heads in {N} Flips ===")
print(f"MLE Estimate:            theta = {theta_mle:.3f} (80% heads)")
print(f"Prior:                   Beta(alpha={alpha_prior}, beta={beta_prior}) -> Prior Mode = 0.500")
print(f"Posterior Distribution:  Beta(alpha={alpha_post:.1f}, beta={beta_post:.1f})")
print(f"MAP Estimate (Mode):     theta = {theta_map:.3f} (shrunk toward prior)")
print(f"Bayes Posterior Mean:    theta = {theta_bayes_mean:.3f}")

# Numerical evaluation of 95% Equal-Tailed Credible Interval
# Discretise theta in [0, 1] to calculate exact CDF
thetas = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 10000)
# Unnormalised Beta PDF: theta^(a-1) * (1-theta)^(b-1)
log_pdf = (alpha_post - 1) * np.log(thetas) + (beta_post - 1) * np.log(1 - thetas)
pdf = np.exp(log_pdf - np.max(log_pdf))
pdf /= np.trapz(pdf, thetas)
cdf = np.cumsum(pdf) * (thetas[1] - thetas[0])

ci_lower = thetas[np.searchsorted(cdf, 0.025)]
ci_upper = thetas[np.searchsorted(cdf, 0.975)]

print(f"\n95% Bayesian Credible Interval: [{ci_lower:.3f}, {ci_upper:.3f}]")
print(f"Predictive Probability for Next Flip Heads:")
print(f"  Under MLE:        {p_next_heads_mle:.3f}")
print(f"  Under Full Bayes: {p_next_heads_bayes:.3f} (accounts for small sample size)")

Common pitfalls

  • Treating the MAP estimate as a full Bayesian solution: MAP is still a point estimate that discards parameter uncertainty and variance.
  • Using improper priors with MAP without verifying posterior propriety: Maximising an unnormalised objective with ill-defined priors can yield divergent solutions.
  • Assuming priors are purely subjective: Priors can represent empirical historical data, physical constraints (e.g. non-negative mass), or regularisation inductors.

06 Check yourself

Q1What machine learning regularisation technique is mathematically equivalent to MAP estimation with a zero-mean Gaussian prior?

Why: The negative log-probability of a Gaussian prior is proportional to the squared Euclidean norm ||theta||_2^2, which corresponds directly to an L2 Ridge penalty.

Q2Why does full Bayesian estimation produce superior predictive uncertainty compared to MAP estimation?

Why: MAP commits to a single parameter vector, ignoring how wide or flat the posterior surface is. Full Bayesian inference marginalises over all plausible parameters, preserving epistemic uncertainty in predictions.

Q3As sample size N grows toward infinity, what happens to the relationship between MLE and MAP estimates?

Why: Data evidence scales with N. For large datasets, the likelihood dominates the prior term in log-space, making MAP and MLE numerically identical in the large-sample limit.
