OLS Inference: t-Tests, F-Test, R² and Adjusted R²

01 The idea

Fitting an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression line is only the first step in linear modelling. To determine whether estimated slopes represent genuine population relationships rather than sampling noise, we must perform statistical inference. Individual coefficient t-tests evaluate specific predictors, the omnibus F-test assesses whether the model explains more variance than a simple average, and adjusted R² prevents the deceptive inflation of goodness-of-fit as redundant variables are added.

Suppose an analyst fits an OLS regression predicting house prices using three features: square footage, number of bedrooms, and the homeowner's lucky lottery number. Least squares outputs estimated slopes: square footage has $\hat{\beta}_1 = 3.350$ (£3,350 per unit), bedrooms has $\hat{\beta}_2 = 12.901$ (£12,900 per bedroom), and lucky number has $\hat{\beta}_3 = -0.031$.

Did the owner's lucky number genuinely influence property price? The estimated slope is $-0.031$, but its standard error is $0.036$. Dividing the coefficient by its standard error gives a $t$-statistic of $t = -0.856$, producing a p-value of $p = 0.394$. Because this p-value is far above the conventional significance threshold $\alpha = 0.05$, we fail to reject the null hypothesis $\beta_3 = 0$ and conclude that lucky number has zero statistical relationship with house prices. Conversely, square footage achieves $t = 25.91$ ($p < 10^{-15}$), demonstrating an overwhelmingly reliable effect.

Next, look at the overall model: the coefficient of determination $R^2$ indicates that the model accounts for $90.7\%$ of total price variance ($R^2 = 0.907$). However, least squares mathematically guarantees that adding any extra variable—even pure random noise—will never decrease $R^2$. Adjusted $R^2$ corrects for this by penalising model complexity: for our model, adjusted $R^2$ is $0.904$. Finally, the omnibus $F$-test evaluates whether all three predictors jointly explain significant variance compared to an intercept-only baseline: an $F$-statistic of $310.4$ ($p < 10^{-15}$) decisively rejects the global null hypothesis.

In everyday terms

The coefficient t-test is like interviewing individual job candidates to verify each person pulls their weight; the omnibus F-test checks whether the team as a whole beats random noise; and adjusted R² penalises the manager for hiring unnecessary staff.

02 Step by step

  1. Fit the OLS regression model $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ via normal equations: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$.
  2. Compute the residual variance estimate $s^2 = \frac{\mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{e}}{n - p - 1}$ and the covariance matrix $\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = s^2 (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}$.
  3. Extract standard errors $\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j) = s\sqrt{[(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}$ and compute $t$-statistics $t_j = \hat{\beta}_j / \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)$ with $n - p - 1$ degrees of freedom.
  4. Calculate the unadjusted coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1 - SS_{\text{res}} / SS_{\text{tot}}$.
  5. Calculate adjusted $R^2 = 1 - \left(1 - R^2\right)\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}$ to penalise over-parameterisation.
  6. Compute the omnibus $F$-statistic $F = \frac{(SS_{\text{tot}} - SS_{\text{res}}) / p}{SS_{\text{res}} / (n - p - 1)}$ to assess the collective significance of all predictors.

03 The maths

  1. Coefficient t-Statistic and Standard Errors

    $$t_j = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j - 0}{\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j}{s \sqrt{[(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}}} \sim t(n - p - 1)$$

    Here $\hat{\beta}_j$ is the estimated slope, $s = \sqrt{SS_{\text{res}} / (n - p - 1)}$ is the residual standard error, and $[(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}]_{jj}$ is the $j$-th diagonal element of the inverted Gram matrix. Under the null hypothesis $H_0: \beta_j = 0$, $t_j$ follows Student's $t$-distribution with $n - p - 1$ degrees of freedom ($n$ observations, $p$ predictors, 1 intercept).

  2. R-Squared and Adjusted R-Squared

    $$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{res}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}}, \qquad R_{\text{adj}}^2 = 1 - \left(1 - R^2\right)\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}$$

    Here $SS_{\text{res}} = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ is the residual sum of squares and $SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2$ is the total sum of squares. Unadjusted $R^2$ measures the proportion of variance explained by the model, but monotonically increases whenever a new feature is added. Adjusted $R^2$ divides sums of squares by their degrees of freedom, increasing only if the added feature improves model fit by more than expected by chance ($t^2 > 1$).

  3. Omnibus F-Statistic

    $$F = \frac{(SS_{\text{tot}} - SS_{\text{res}}) / p}{SS_{\text{res}} / (n - p - 1)} = \frac{R^2 / p}{(1 - R^2) / (n - p - 1)} \sim F(p, \, n - p - 1)$$

    The omnibus $F$-test evaluates the joint null hypothesis $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_p = 0$ against the alternative that at least one coefficient is non-zero. It tests whether the regression model as a whole explains significantly more variance than a flat horizontal line through the sample mean.

04 Key takeaways

  • Individual $t$-tests assess the marginal contribution of each predictor given that all other predictors are already included in the model.
  • $R^2$ never decreases when adding variables; adjusted $R^2$ penalises degrees of freedom to protect against spurious model complexity.
  • The omnibus $F$-test guards against false discovery by evaluating whether all explanatory variables jointly explain significant outcome variance.
  • In the presence of multicollinearity, the omnibus $F$-test can be highly significant even if no individual $t$-test achieves significance.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100
p = 3

# Predict house prices using sqft (x1), bedrooms (x2), and lucky number (x3)
x1 = rng.uniform(10.0, 40.0, size=n)
x2 = rng.integers(1, 6, size=n)
x3 = rng.uniform(1.0, 100.0, size=n)  # Pure noise variable
y = 50.0 + 3.5 * x1 + 12.0 * x2 + rng.normal(0.0, 10.0, size=n)

# OLS normal equations: beta = (X^T X)^(-1) X^T y
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2, x3])
beta = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y
residuals = y - X @ beta

# Inference: standard errors, t-statistics, p-values
s2 = np.sum(residuals**2) / (n - p - 1)
se_beta = np.sqrt(np.diag(s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)))
t_stats = beta / se_beta
p_vals = 2.0 * (1.0 - stats.t.cdf(np.abs(t_stats), df=n - p - 1))

# Goodness-of-fit: R2, Adjusted R2, F-statistic
ss_tot = np.sum((y - np.mean(y))**2)
ss_res = np.sum(residuals**2)
r2 = 1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot
r2_adj = 1.0 - (ss_res / (n - p - 1)) / (ss_tot / (n - 1))
f_stat = ((ss_tot - ss_res) / p) / (ss_res / (n - p - 1))

print(f"R2: {r2:.3f}, Adjusted R2: {r2_adj:.3f}, F-stat: {f_stat:.1f}")
print(f"SqFt:   beta={beta[1]:.3f}, SE={se_beta[1]:.3f}, t={t_stats[1]:.2f}, p={p_vals[1]:.4e}")
print(f"Lucky:  beta={beta[3]:.3f}, SE={se_beta[3]:.3f}, t={t_stats[3]:.2f}, p={p_vals[3]:.3f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Mistaking high $R^2$ for causal validity: an overfitted model with 50 irrelevant polynomial terms can achieve $R^2 > 0.98$ while failing completely on new data.
  • Interpreting a non-significant $t$-test as proof that a feature has no relationship with $Y$; collinearity with another feature can inflate standard errors.
  • Comparing unadjusted $R^2$ values across models with different numbers of predictors.
  • Trusting standard OLS p-values when errors are heteroscedastic or serially correlated, which violates Gauss–Markov assumptions.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does adding a completely random noise feature to a linear regression model always increase (or leave unchanged) the unadjusted $R^2$?

Why: Adding any column to $\mathbf{X}$ gives least squares an extra dimension to fit the training points, so $SS_{\text{res}}$ cannot increase and $R^2 = 1 - SS_{\text{res}} / SS_{\text{tot}}$ never decreases.

Q2What does a non-significant coefficient t-test ($p = 0.45$) for predictor $X_1$ strictly indicate in a multiple regression model?

Why: Regression $t$-tests measure marginal significance conditioned on all other included variables; $X_1$ might have strong univariate correlation with $Y$, but be redundant in the presence of other features.

Q3Under what condition does adding a new explanatory variable cause the adjusted $R^2$ to increase?

Why: Mathematically, adjusted $R^2$ increases if and only if the absolute $t$-statistic of the added variable exceeds 1.0 (or its partial $F$-statistic exceeds 1.0).

Q4In an OLS regression with 100 observations and 3 predictors (plus intercept), how many degrees of freedom does the residual variance estimator possess?

Why: Residual degrees of freedom are $n - p - 1 = 100 - 3 - 1 = 96$, where $p = 3$ is the number of predictors and 1 accounts for the intercept.
