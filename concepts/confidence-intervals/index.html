01 The idea

A point estimate alone provides an incomplete picture of an unknown population parameter. Confidence intervals construct a calibrated range around the estimate, guaranteeing that a chosen percentage of such intervals across repeated sampling will trap the true parameter value.

Imagine an archer shooting arrows at a bullseye hidden in thick fog. You cannot see where the bullseye is, but you can see where each arrow lands. If you only look at one arrow, you have a point estimate of where the target might be. But if you draw a circle around that arrow with a radius based on the archer's known spread, you create an interval. If the radius is calibrated for 95% confidence, then across 100 shots in the fog, about 95 of your circles will capture the hidden bullseye.

Notice what this means: the bullseye is fixed in one spot; it does not move. It is your circles that shift with each new shot. This is the heart of the frequentist confidence interval. You cannot say "there is a 95% probability the true target lies inside this specific circle"—either it does or it does not (0 or 1). What you can say is that the procedure used to draw the circle captures the target 95% of the time under repeated sampling.

In machine learning and statistics, we build these intervals by taking our point estimate (like a regression coefficient or validation accuracy) and adding and subtracting a margin of error: $\text{Estimate} \pm (\text{Critical Value} \times \text{Standard Error})$. If the population variance is known, we use the standard normal $z$-distribution; when estimating variance from a small sample, we switch to Student's $t$-distribution with heavier tails to account for extra uncertainty.

In everyday terms

A confidence interval is like tossing a ring around a fixed peg: the peg does not move, but 95 out of 100 tossed rings will successfully encircle it.

02 Step by step

  1. Specify the desired confidence level $1 - \alpha$ (most commonly 95%, where $\alpha = 0.05$).
  2. Compute the point estimate (e.g. sample mean $\bar{x}$) from the empirical sample.
  3. Compute the estimated standard error of the estimator: $\operatorname{SE} = s / \sqrt{n}$.
  4. Select the appropriate critical value: $z_{1 - \alpha/2}$ for large samples or known $\sigma$; $t_{1 - \alpha/2, n-1}$ for unknown $\sigma$ on small samples.
  5. Calculate the margin of error: $m = \text{Critical Value} \times \operatorname{SE}$.
  6. Construct the interval boundaries: $[\bar{x} - m, \, \bar{x} + m]$.

03 The maths

  1. Gaussian and Student's t Confidence Intervals

    $$\text{Known } \sigma: \quad \bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \text{Unknown } \sigma: \quad \bar{x} \pm t_{1-\alpha/2, \, n-1} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

    When $\sigma$ is unknown and estimated by $s$, the ratio follows Student's $t$-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. The $t$-distribution has heavier tails than the standard normal distribution, widening the interval to reflect uncertainty in estimating the standard deviation. As $n \to \infty$, $t_{n-1} \to \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

  2. Margin of Error and Sample Size Determination

    $$m = z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \implies n = \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma}{m}\right)^2$$

    The margin of error $m$ is half the interval width. To achieve a specified target precision $m$ with $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence, the required sample size scales inversely with the square of the margin of error.

  3. Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion

    $$\hat{p} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}$$

    The Wald interval for a Bernoulli proportion approximates the binomial distribution with a normal distribution. When $n\hat{p} < 10$ or $n(1-\hat{p}) < 10$, the Wilson score interval or Agresti–Coull adjustment is preferred to prevent coverage breakdown near 0 and 1.

04 Key takeaways

  • Confidence is a property of the sampling procedure over infinite hypothetical replications, not a Bayesian posterior probability on a single interval.
  • Student's $t$-distribution automatically compensates for small sample sizes by expanding critical values.
  • Narrower intervals indicate higher precision, achieved by increasing sample size $n$ or accepting a lower confidence level.
  • If a 95% confidence interval for a treatment effect includes 0, the effect is not statistically significant at the $\alpha = 0.05$ level.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
true_mu, true_sigma = 75.0, 10.0
n = 20
alpha = 0.05

# Simulate 1,000 independent 95% confidence intervals
trials = 1000
captured = 0

for _ in range(trials):
    sample = rng.normal(loc=true_mu, scale=true_sigma, size=n)
    mean = np.mean(sample)
    se = stats.sem(sample)
    # Student's t critical value for df = n - 1
    t_crit = stats.t.ppf(1.0 - alpha / 2.0, df=n - 1)
    ci_lower = mean - t_crit * se
    ci_upper = mean + t_crit * se
    if ci_lower <= true_mu <= ci_upper:
        captured += 1

print(f"Sample Size n={n}, Degrees of Freedom={n - 1}")
print(f"t Critical Value: {t_crit:.3f} (vs normal z = 1.960)")
print(f"Empirical Coverage: {captured / trials * 100:.1f}% (Nominal: 95.0%)")

Common pitfalls

  • Interpreting a 95% CI as "there is a 95% probability the true parameter lies in this specific calculated interval" (that is a Bayesian credible interval).
  • Using the standard normal $z$-distribution on small samples ($n < 30$) when the population standard deviation is unknown, leading to undercoverage.
  • Overlapping confidence intervals: two groups whose 95% CIs slightly overlap can still be statistically significantly different.
  • Ignoring the independence assumption; correlated or clustered data produces artificially narrow confidence intervals that fail to cover at the nominal rate.

06 Check yourself

Q1Which statement correctly reflects the frequentist meaning of a 95% confidence interval $[12.4, 18.2]$ for a population mean $\mu$?

Why: In frequentist statistics, the population parameter $\mu$ is a fixed constant, not a random variable. The 95% confidence level describes the long-run success rate of the interval-generating procedure.

Q2Why is Student's $t$-distribution used instead of the standard normal $z$-distribution when constructing confidence intervals for small samples?

Why: Estimating the unknown population variance introduces extra randomness. Student's $t$-distribution has heavier tails, producing wider intervals that preserve the correct nominal coverage.

Q3If a 95% confidence interval for the difference between two model accuracies $(\text{Acc}_A - \text{Acc}_B)$ is $[-0.015, +0.062]$, what can you conclude at the $\alpha = 0.05$ significance level?

Why: Because the 95% confidence interval spans across zero (from negative to positive), the null hypothesis of equal performance ($\text{Acc}_A - \text{Acc}_B = 0$) cannot be rejected at $\alpha = 0.05$.

Q4How does increasing the confidence level from 90% to 99% affect the width of a confidence interval (all else being equal)?

Why: A higher confidence level requires a larger critical value (e.g. $z = 2.576$ for 99% vs $z = 1.645$ for 90%), expanding the margin of error and widening the interval to guarantee higher coverage.

Q5A machine learning engineer measures model latency across $n = 100$ independent query runs, finding sample mean latency $\bar{x} = 45.0\text{ ms}$ and sample standard deviation $s = 15.0\text{ ms}$. Using standard normal approximation with $z_{0.975} = 1.96$, what is the margin of error for a 95% confidence interval for the mean latency?

Why: The standard error is $\operatorname{SE} = s / \sqrt{n} = 15.0 / \sqrt{100} = 1.50\text{ ms}$. The margin of error is $m = z_{0.975} \times \operatorname{SE} = 1.96 \times 1.50 = 2.94\text{ ms}$. The full width of the interval is $2m = 5.88\text{ ms}$.

Q6When constructing a 95% confidence interval for the mean of a normally distributed feature from a sample of $n = 18$ observations with unknown population variance $\sigma^2$, which critical value must be used?

Why: When the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is unknown and estimated by the sample standard deviation $s$, the standardised mean follows Student's $t$-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. For $n = 18$, $\nu = 18 - 1 = 17$.

Q7When estimating a 95% confidence interval for a conversion rate where only 3 conversions occurred out of 200 visitors ($\hat{p} = 0.015$), why is the standard Wald interval $[\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}]$ problematic?

Why: The Wald interval relies on a normal approximation to the binomial distribution. When $n\hat{p} < 10$ (here $200 \times 0.015 = 3$), the distribution is highly skewed; the Wald interval gives poor actual coverage (often well below nominal 95%) and can yield negative lower limits. Wilson score or Agresti–Coull intervals are preferred.

Q8Consider the following Python snippet using SciPy to construct a 95% confidence interval for a sample mean: data = np.array([12, 15, 14, 18, 16]) mean = np.mean(data) se = stats.sem(data) ci = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(data) - 1, loc=mean, scale=se) What does stats.sem(data) compute in this snippet?

Why: stats.sem computes the standard error of the mean, $\hat{\sigma} / \sqrt{n} = s / \sqrt{n}$. The stats.t.interval function multiplies this standard error by the two-tailed critical value and centres it around loc.

Q9In the standard notation of confidence intervals, if the desired confidence level is $99\%$, what is the significance level $\alpha$?

Why: The confidence level is defined as $1 - \alpha$. For a $99\%$ confidence interval ($0.99$), the significance level is $\alpha = 1 - 0.99 = 0.01$.

Q10An analytics team wants to estimate the mean response time of a web service to within a margin of error $m = 2.0\text{ ms}$ with $95\%$ confidence ($z_{0.975} = 1.96$). From past measurements, the standard deviation is known to be $\sigma = 20.0\text{ ms}$. What is the minimum sample size $n$ required?

Why: Using the sample size formula $n = (z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma / m)^2 = (1.96 \times 20.0 / 2.0)^2 = (19.6)^2 = 384.16$. Rounding up to the next integer guarantees the desired precision: $n = 385$.

Q11Suppose two independent models, Model 1 and Model 2, have estimated mean latencies whose 95% confidence intervals overlap slightly: Model 1 has $[10.0, 14.0]$ and Model 2 has $[13.0, 17.0]$. A colleague claims that because their individual 95% confidence intervals overlap, the difference between the two models cannot be statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$. Why is this claim incorrect?

Why: The margin of error for the difference $(m_{\text{diff}} = z \sqrt{\operatorname{SE}_1^2 + \operatorname{SE}_2^2})$ is strictly smaller than the sum of the individual margins of error $(m_1 + m_2 = z(\operatorname{SE}_1 + \operatorname{SE}_2))$ because $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} < a + b$. Two 95% confidence intervals can overlap by up to roughly 29% of their width while their difference remains statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$.

Q12In an A/B test with $n = 100$ visitors, 50 users convert, giving sample proportion $\hat{p} = 0.50$. Using the Wald method with $z_{0.975} = 1.96$, what is the 95% confidence interval for the population conversion rate?

Why: The estimated standard error is $\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) / n} = \sqrt{0.50 \times 0.50 / 100} = \sqrt{0.0025} = 0.050$. The margin of error is $m = 1.96 \times 0.050 = 0.098$. The interval is $0.500 \pm 0.098 = [0.402, 0.598]$.
