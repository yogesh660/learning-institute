Chunking and embeddings for retrieval

Chunking and embedding represent the foundational ingestion stage of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architectures. Because raw documents are too extensive to embed as monolithic vectors, text must be partitioned into granular passages that balance semantic coherence against retrieval specificity. From fixed-length token windows with sliding overlaps to recursive syntactic splitting, semantic clustering, and hierarchical parent-document indexing, chunking strategies directly determine retrieval recall and downstream LLM generation quality.

01 The idea

If you feed an entire 200-page corporate financial report into an embedding model as a single vector, the resulting embedding averages together revenue numbers, legal disclaimers, executive biographies, and supply chain updates into an uninformative semantic soup. Conversely, if you chop the report into single-sentence fragments, a search for "Q3 revenue" finds a sentence saying "It increased by 14%" without knowing what "It" refers to. The art and science of chunking solves this Goldilocks dilemma: partitioning text into chunks that are small enough for precise retrieval, yet rich enough to be self-contained.

The Granularity vs Context Trade-off: - Small Chunks (50 to 150 tokens): High retrieval precision. The embedding vector reflects a specific, tightly focused idea. However, small chunks lack surrounding context, resulting in pronoun ambiguity and missing qualifiers. - Large Chunks (500 to 1,000 tokens): High contextual completeness. The passage contains necessary background and surrounding facts. However, the vector embedding averages multiple topics, reducing cosine similarity with targeted search queries.

The Evolution of Chunking Strategies: 1. Fixed-Size Token Chunking: Splitting text every N tokens with an overlap of M tokens (e.g. 512 tokens with 64 overlap). Fast and simple, but frequently slices mid-sentence or breaks tabular data. 2. Recursive Character / Syntactic Chunking: Recursively splitting on natural boundaries: first paragraph breaks (\n\n), then sentence breaks (\n, full stops), then word boundaries. Preserves grammatical paragraphs. 3. Semantic Chunking: Computing cosine similarity between consecutive sentence embeddings and creating a split only when semantic similarity drops below a percentile threshold. 4. Hierarchical / Parent-Document Retrieval: Decoupling the chunk you search from the chunk you read: index small, focused child chunks (100 tokens) for search, but return their larger parent section (1,000 tokens) to the LLM generator.

Embedding Dimension and Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL): Modern embedding models (like OpenAI text-embedding-3 or voyage-ai) produce dense vectors of 1,536 or 3,072 dimensions. Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) trains models such that the first 256 or 512 dimensions contain the vast majority of semantic information, allowing developers to truncate embeddings by 75% to save memory and vector database costs with negligible accuracy loss.

In everyday terms

Think of packing a shipping container with household goods. If you pack an entire house into one gigantic wooden crate, no forklift can lift it, and finding your car keys requires unpacking the entire crate (document-level embedding). If you put every single pencil, fork, and bolt into its own individual tiny envelope (sentence-level chunking), you have two million envelopes, lose the context of which room they belong to, and drown in shipping labels. Effective chunking packs items into neatly labelled, themed boxes (kitchen utensils, bedroom books) with packing slips (metadata) so you can retrieve exactly what you need in seconds.

03 The maths

  1. Sliding window token chunking with stride

    C_k = [w_{k \cdot (L - O)}, \, w_{k \cdot (L - O) + 1}, \, \dots, \, w_{k \cdot (L - O) + L - 1}]

    Here $L$ is chunk window length in tokens, $O$ is overlap length ($O < L$), and the stride is $S = L - O$. Overlap ensures that entities and facts positioned at chunk boundaries are represented continuously in at least one window.

  2. Semantic chunking split threshold condition

    \operatorname{Split}(i) \iff \cos\left( E(s_i), E(s_{i+1}) \right) < \mu_{\text{sim}} - k \cdot \sigma_{\text{sim}}

    A semantic boundary is triggered between consecutive sentences $s_i$ and $s_{i+1}$ when their embedding cosine similarity drops significantly below the running mean $\mu_{\text{sim}}$ by $k$ standard deviations $\sigma_{\text{sim}}$, detecting topical transitions.

  3. Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) loss

    \mathcal{L}_{\text{MRL}} = \sum_{m \in \{d_1, d_2, \dots, D\}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{contrastive}}\left( \mathbf{z}_{1:m}, \mathbf{z}^+_{1:m} \right)

    MRL optimises contrastive loss across nested vector prefixes $d_1 < d_2 < \dots < D$. The top dimensions capture high-level semantic clusters, allowing vector truncation to $d_1 = 256$ dimensions with minimal loss in retrieval accuracy.

04 Key takeaways

  • Chunking balances retrieval precision (small chunks) against contextual completeness (large chunks).
  • Recursive character splitting respects natural linguistic boundaries: paragraphs, sentences, and clauses.
  • Sliding window overlap (typically 10-20% of chunk size) prevents facts positioned at boundaries from being cleaved in half.
  • Hierarchical parent-document retrieval indexes granular child chunks for search while supplying full parent passages to the LLM.
  • Prepending document titles and section breadcrumbs to chunks resolves ambiguous pronouns and boosts retrieval recall.

05 In code

python
class RecursiveTextSplitter:
    """
    Recursive syntactic text splitter that respects paragraph and sentence
    boundaries while enforcing maximum chunk size and sliding overlap.
    """
    def __init__(self, chunk_size=100, chunk_overlap=20):
        self.chunk_size = chunk_size
        self.chunk_overlap = chunk_overlap
        self.separators = ["\n\n", "\n", ". ", " "]

    def split_text(self, text):
        return self._split(text, self.separators)

    def _split(self, text, separators):
        final_chunks = []
        if not separators:
            return [text] if text else []
            
        sep = separators[0]
        splits = text.split(sep)
        
        current_chunk = ""
        for s in splits:
            s_clean = s.strip()
            if not s_clean:
                continue
                
            candidate = f"{current_chunk}{sep}{s_clean}" if current_chunk else s_clean
            if len(candidate) <= self.chunk_size:
                current_chunk = candidate
            else:
                if current_chunk:
                    final_chunks.append(current_chunk)
                    # Create overlap from end of current chunk
                    overlap_start = max(0, len(current_chunk) - self.chunk_overlap)
                    overlap_text = current_chunk[overlap_start:]
                    current_chunk = f"{overlap_text} {s_clean}".strip()
                else:
                    # Current piece itself exceeds chunk size, recurse on next separator
                    deeper = self._split(s_clean, separators[1:])
                    final_chunks.extend(deeper)
                    current_chunk = ""
                    
        if current_chunk:
            final_chunks.append(current_chunk)
        return final_chunks

# Sample document demonstrating paragraph, sentence, and section boundaries
raw_document = (
    "Overview of Neural Architecture Search.\n\n"
    "Neural Architecture Search (NAS) automates the design of artificial neural networks. "
    "Rather than hand-crafting layer topologies, algorithms explore vast discrete search spaces. "
    "Common exploration techniques include reinforcement learning, evolutionary algorithms, and gradient-based continuous relaxation.\n\n"
    "Computational Costs and Efficiency.\n\n"
    "Early NAS methods required thousands of GPU hours on CIFAR-10. "
    "Modern one-shot NAS architectures share weights across sub-networks, reducing training time to a single day on modest hardware."
)

splitter = RecursiveTextSplitter(chunk_size=160, chunk_overlap=30)
chunks = splitter.split_text(raw_document)

print(f"Original Document Length: {len(raw_document)} characters")
print(f"Generated {len(chunks)} Chunks (Target size: 160 chars, Overlap: 30 chars):\n")
for i, c in enumerate(chunks, 1):
    print(f"--- Chunk {i} ({len(c)} chars) ---")
    print(f"'{c}'\n")

Common pitfalls

  • Splitting blindly by character count: Slicing text every 500 characters breaks words in half and severs sentences midway, creating broken token embeddings. Always split by tokens or natural linguistic boundaries.
  • Zero overlap in sliding windows: With zero overlap, a key answer spanning the boundary between chunk A and chunk B is split across two vectors, causing both chunks to score poorly. Always maintain 10-20% overlap.
  • Shredding tables and structured data: Naively chunking markdown or HTML tables into linear text turns structured rows and columns into incomprehensible gibberish. Use dedicated table parsers or serialize rows as key-value pairs.
  • Neglecting chunk metadata: When a retrieved chunk reads 'The fee is £50' without document metadata, the LLM cannot know whether the fee applies to parking, checking accounts, or visa processing. Always attach document titles and headers.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Hierarchical Parent-Document retrieval offer superior performance over single-scale chunking in RAG pipelines?

Why: Small chunks have high vector specificity for finding exact matches, but lack context. Large chunks contain complete context, but blur vector similarity. Hierarchical retrieval indexes small child chunks for search and passes the full parent context to the generator.

Q2What failure mode occurs when sliding window chunking is performed with zero overlap (overlap = 0)?

Why: If a critical answer spans words 510 to 515 and the chunk splits at word 512 with zero overlap, half the concept is in chunk 1 and half in chunk 2. Neither chunk contains the complete fact. Maintaining 10-20% overlap ensures the fact appears intact in at least one chunk.

Q3How does Semantic Chunking decide where to partition a document?

Why: Semantic chunking calculates the vector similarity between adjacent sentences. When the topic transitions, the cosine similarity between consecutive sentences drops sharply, providing an adaptive, content-aware boundary marker.
