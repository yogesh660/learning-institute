01 The idea

In subscription businesses and customer platforms, retaining existing customers is dramatically cheaper than acquiring new ones. However, framing churn simply as binary classification (churned vs not churned) discards the critical temporal dimension: when will the customer churn, and what is their survival probability over time? Survival analysis accounts for right-censoring to model the complete tenure curve and hazard dynamics.

In customer databases, active customers have not yet churned; their true eventual churn date lies in the future. This is classic right-censoring: we know their tenure is at least $T$ days, but we cannot observe their final lifespan.

Standard binary classification models trained on a fixed time window (e.g. "did the user churn in month 3?") suffer from survivorship bias, window length sensitivity, and inability to predict expected remaining customer lifetime.

Survival analysis models two core mathematical functions: the Survival function $S(t) = P(T > t)$ (the probability that a customer remains active past time $t$) and the Hazard function $\lambda(t)$ (the instantaneous churn risk rate at time $t$ given survival up to $t$).

The Kaplan-Meier estimator non-parametrically estimates the baseline survival curve across cohorts. The Cox Proportional Hazards semi-parametric model then evaluates how customer covariates (login frequency, support tickets, monthly spend) multiply the baseline hazard rate, allowing proactive retention interventions.

In everyday terms

Imagine clinical researchers studying patient recovery from a rare illness. When the clinical trial concludes after two years, some patients have fully recovered, while others are still undergoing treatment. The researchers cannot classify surviving patients as "never recovering" or discard them as missing data. Survival analysis mathematically incorporates the fact that ongoing patients survived at least two years without assuming they will never recover.

02 Step by step

  1. Define churn event and observation window: Establish rigorous operational churn definitions (e.g. cancellation confirmation or 60 days of zero activity).
  2. Construct survival dataset: For each customer, record elapsed tenure $T_i$ and event indicator $E_i \in \{0, 1\}$ ($1$ if churn was observed, $0$ if right-censored / still active).
  3. Fit non-parametric Kaplan-Meier curves: Estimate baseline retention trajectories across customer acquisition channels or subscription tiers.
  4. Fit Cox Proportional Hazards model: Estimate covariate hazard ratios $e^{\beta_j}$ to quantify which behavioural features multiply or diminish churn risk.
  5. Validate proportional hazards assumption: Test Schoenfeld residuals to ensure covariate effects remain proportional across customer tenure.
  6. Deploy proactive retention triggers: Score active customers on cumulative churn hazard over the upcoming 30 days to trigger targeted customer success interventions.

03 The maths

  1. Survival Function and Cumulative Hazard

    S(t) = P(T > t) = \exp\left( -\int_0^t \lambda(u) \, du \right) = \exp\left( -\Lambda(t) \right)

    The survival probability $S(t)$ decays as the cumulative hazard function $\Lambda(t)$ accumulates over tenure $t$.

  2. Kaplan-Meier Non-Parametric Estimator

    \hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left( 1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i} \right)

    Estimates survival curve step-by-step at event times $t_i$, where $d_i$ is the number of churn events and $n_i$ is the number of customers at risk immediately prior to $t_i$.

  3. Cox Proportional Hazards Formulation

    \lambda(t \mid \mathbf{x}) = \lambda_0(t) \cdot \exp\left( \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \mathbf{x} \right)

    Decomposes churn risk into an arbitrary baseline hazard $\lambda_0(t)$ multiplied by an exponential linear combination of customer covariates $\mathbf{x}$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Survival analysis properly handles right-censored active customers without introducing survivorship bias.
  • The survival function $S(t)$ gives the probability of a customer remaining past tenure $t$.
  • The Cox model estimates hazard ratios: a feature with $\exp(\beta) = 1.40$ increases churn risk by 40% at any point in time.
  • Enables proactive, time-aware interventions by predicting when churn probability spikes.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def kaplan_meier_estimator(durations: np.ndarray, events: np.ndarray):
    """Compute non-parametric Kaplan-Meier survival curve."""
    # Sort chronologically by duration
    order = np.argsort(durations)
    d_sorted = durations[order]
    e_sorted = events[order]
    
    unique_times = np.unique(d_sorted[e_sorted == 1])
    n_at_risk = len(durations)
    survival_prob = 1.0
    km_curve = [(0.0, 1.0)]
    
    for t in unique_times:
        # Count at risk and events at time t
        at_risk = np.sum(d_sorted >= t)
        deaths = np.sum((d_sorted == t) & (e_sorted == 1))
        survival_prob *= (1.0 - deaths / at_risk)
        km_curve.append((t, survival_prob))
        
    return km_curve

# Sample customer tenure data: (tenure_months, churned: 1=churn, 0=active/censored)
durations = np.array([3, 5, 6, 8, 12, 12, 15, 18, 24, 24, 30, 36])
events = np.array([1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0])  # Several active customers

km = kaplan_meier_estimator(durations, events)
print("Customer Survival Curve (Kaplan-Meier):")
for t, s in km:
    print(f"Month {t:2.0f} -> Survival Probability: {s * 100:.1f}%")
# Observe probability accounts for right-censored users who have not churned yet

Common pitfalls

  • Discarding active customers: Throwing away active customers because their eventual outcome is unobserved biases the model heavily towards short-tenure churners.
  • Treating active customers as non-churners: Coding active customers as $y=0$ in binary classification assumes they will stay forever, underestimating long-term churn.
  • Violating proportional hazards: If a feature's effect changes drastically over time (e.g. onboarding discounts having large impact in month 1 but zero in month 12), standard Cox models must use time-varying covariates.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is "right-censoring" in customer churn modelling?

Why: Right-censoring occurs because active customers have not churned yet; we know their survival time is at least their current tenure, but not when they will eventually leave.

Q2In a Cox Proportional Hazards model, what does a hazard ratio of $\exp(\beta_j) = 1.35$ for a feature indicate?

Why: The hazard ratio multiplies the baseline hazard $\lambda_0(t)$. A ratio of 1.35 corresponds to a 35% relative increase in instantaneous attrition risk.

Q3Why does standard binary classification (predicting churn yes/no over the next 90 days) fall short compared to survival analysis?

Why: Binary classification loses temporal granularity and cannot estimate expected remaining customer lifespan or dynamic hazard curves over tenure.
