Permutation tests

Permutation tests (also called randomisation or exact re-randomisation tests) are non-parametric hypothesis tests that calculate exact, distribution-free p-values by exhaustively or randomly shuffling group labels under the null hypothesis. Unlike classical t-tests and ANOVA that rely on asymptotic central limit theorems and strict normality assumptions, permutation tests require only that observations are exchangeable under the null hypothesis H_0. They can evaluate arbitrary test statistics—including medians, trimmed means, ratios, and custom business metrics—with exact finite-sample Type I error guarantees.

01 The idea

What should you do when evaluating an A/B test with 25 users per variant, where the metric is heavily skewed with extreme outliers and violates every assumption of the Student's t-test? Classical parametric formulas fall apart on small, non-normal samples. Permutation tests provide the gold standard solution: by shuffling the "Variant A" and "Variant B" labels thousands of times, they construct an exact empirical null distribution directly from the observed data without making any parametric distributional assumptions.

The Core Logic of Exchangeability: Under the null hypothesis H_0, the treatment has no effect on the outcome. Therefore, the group labels ("Control" vs "Treatment") assigned to each participant are completely arbitrary! If treatment had no impact, an observed revenue of £150 in group A was equally likely to have occurred in group B.

Building the Empirical Null by Shuffling: Pool all observations from groups A and B together. Randomly shuffle the labels and recompute your test statistic (e.g. difference in means x_bar_A - x_bar_B). Repeat this shuffling process 10,000 times. This builds an exact empirical reference distribution showing what differences arise purely from random chance.

Calculating the Exact p-Value: The two-sided p-value is simply the proportion of shuffled permutations where the test statistic is at least as extreme as the observed test statistic from the real experiment. No z-tables, t-tables, or degrees of freedom required!

Total Metric Flexibility: In classical tests, you are restricted to statistics with known analytical sampling distributions (like the mean). In a permutation test, you can test differences in medians, 90th percentiles, variance ratios, or custom profit formulas with identical mathematical validity.

In everyday terms

Imagine dealing a deck of cards into two piles, Red and Blue, and noticing the Red pile has a higher total face value. Did the dealer cheat, or was it pure chance? To test this, you don't need a mathematical theorem about Gaussian curves. You simply gather the cards, shuffle them thoroughly, deal them into two piles again, and check the difference. If you repeat this shuffle 10,000 times and an advantage as large as the original deal appears only 12 times, the p-value is 12 / 10,000 = 0.0012, proving the original deal was extraordinarily unlikely to occur by random chance.

03 The maths

  1. Exact Permutation p-Value Formulation

    p_{\text{exact}} = \frac{1}{\binom{N}{n_A}} \sum_{\pi \in \Pi} \mathbb{I}\Big( \big|T(\pi(\mathcal{D}))\big| \ge |T_{\text{obs}}| \Big)

    For sample sizes n_A and n_B with total N = n_A + n_B, there are \binom{N}{n_A} possible ways to partition the observations into two groups. When N is small, all permutations \Pi can be evaluated exhaustively to yield an exact finite-sample p-value.

  2. Monte Carlo Permutation p-Value with Continuity Correction

    \hat{p} = \frac{1 + \sum_{b=1}^B \mathbb{I}\Big( |T^{(b)}| \ge |T_{\text{obs}}| \Big)}{B + 1}

    When the total number of permutations is astronomically large, B random permutations are sampled uniformly with replacement. Including the original observed dataset as the (B+1)-th permutation ensures \hat{p} is strictly conservative and guarantees exact Type I error control at level \alpha.

  3. Exchangeability Condition under Null Hypothesis

    P(X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_N = x_N) = P(X_{\pi(1)} = x_1, \dots, X_{\pi(N)} = x_N) \quad \forall \pi \in \mathcal{S}_N

    The fundamental assumption underpinning permutation testing is exchangeability: under H_0, the joint probability distribution of the pooled sample is invariant under all permutations \pi in the symmetric group \mathcal{S}_N. Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations satisfy this condition automatically.

04 Key takeaways

  • Permutation tests generate exact, distribution-free p-values by shuffling group labels under the null hypothesis of exchangeability.
  • Unlike parametric t-tests or ANOVA, permutation tests require no assumptions of normality, homoscedasticity, or large sample sizes.
  • They allow testing arbitrary test statistics—including medians, percentiles, trimmed means, and custom revenue ratios.
  • The Monte Carlo formulation with continuity correction (1 + extreme) / (B + 1) guarantees strict finite-sample Type I error control.

05 In code

python
"""
Two-Sample Permutation Test vs Student's t-test
Demonstrating exact distribution-free hypothesis testing on skewed,
heavy-tailed A/B test data with extreme outliers.
"""

import numpy as np

# Simulate skewed A/B test data (e.g. e-commerce purchase amounts in GBP)
# Control: Log-normal(mu=2.5, sigma=1.0)
# Treatment: Log-normal(mu=2.9, sigma=1.2) with extreme outliers
np.random.seed(42)
n_control = 25
n_treatment = 25

control_data = np.random.lognormal(mean=2.5, sigma=1.0, size=n_control)
treatment_data = np.random.lognormal(mean=2.9, sigma=1.2, size=n_treatment)
# Introduce two extreme high-value spender outliers in treatment
treatment_data[0] *= 4.0
treatment_data[1] *= 5.0

print(f"=== Sample Summary (n_control={n_control}, n_treatment={n_treatment}) ===")
print(f"Control:   Mean = £{np.mean(control_data):.2f} | Median = £{np.median(control_data):.2f}")
print(f"Treatment: Mean = £{np.mean(treatment_data):.2f} | Median = £{np.median(treatment_data):.2f}")

def run_permutation_test(group_a: np.ndarray, group_b: np.ndarray, num_permutations: int = 10000, statistic_type: str = "mean"):
    """
    Execute two-sided Monte Carlo permutation test for mean or median difference.
    """
    n_a = len(group_a)
    n_b = len(group_b)
    pooled = np.concatenate([group_a, group_b])
    total_n = len(pooled)

    # 1. Observed test statistic
    if statistic_type == "mean":
        t_obs = np.mean(group_b) - np.mean(group_a)
    elif statistic_type == "median":
        t_obs = np.median(group_b) - np.median(group_a)
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unsupported statistic type")

    # 2. Monte Carlo shuffling under H0
    permuted_diffs = np.empty(num_permutations)
    for b in range(num_permutations):
        shuffled = np.random.permutation(pooled)
        new_a = shuffled[:n_a]
        new_b = shuffled[n_a:]
        if statistic_type == "mean":
            permuted_diffs[b] = np.mean(new_b) - np.mean(new_a)
        else:
            permuted_diffs[b] = np.median(new_b) - np.median(new_a)

    # 3. Two-sided p-value with continuity correction
    count_extreme = np.sum(np.abs(permuted_diffs) >= np.abs(t_obs))
    p_value = (1 + count_extreme) / (num_permutations + 1)

    return t_obs, p_value, permuted_diffs

# 1. Permutation Test on Difference in Means
t_obs_mean, p_perm_mean, _ = run_permutation_test(control_data, treatment_data, num_permutations=10000, statistic_type="mean")

# 2. Permutation Test on Difference in Medians (Robust to outliers!)
t_obs_med, p_perm_med, _ = run_permutation_test(control_data, treatment_data, num_permutations=10000, statistic_type="median")

# 3. Classical Student's t-test for comparison
from scipy import stats
t_stat, p_ttest = stats.ttest_ind(treatment_data, control_data, equal_var=False)

print("\n=== Hypothesis Testing Results ===")
print(f"Student's Welch t-test:           Difference = £{np.mean(treatment_data) - np.mean(control_data):.2f} | p-value = {p_ttest:.4f}")
print(f"Permutation Test (Means):         Obs Diff   = £{t_obs_mean:.2f} | p-value = {p_perm_mean:.4f}")
print(f"Permutation Test (Medians):       Obs Diff   = £{t_obs_med:.2f} | p-value = {p_perm_med:.4f}")

alpha = 0.05
print("\nConclusion at alpha = 0.05:")
print(f"  Permutation (Means):   {'Statistically Significant' if p_perm_mean < alpha else 'Fail to Reject H0'}")
print(f"  Permutation (Medians): {'Statistically Significant' if p_perm_med < alpha else 'Fail to Reject H0'}")

Common pitfalls

  • Assuming permutation tests allow heteroscedasticity under general hypotheses: Under H_0, the entire outcome distribution must be invariant across groups, not just the mean.
  • Forgetting the +1 continuity correction: Omitting the +1 can produce a p-value of exactly 0.0 when no permuted sample exceeds T_obs, which is theoretically invalid under finite B.
  • High computational cost on large datasets: Reshuffling millions of rows 10,000 times is computationally prohibitive; in large samples, asymptotic tests or fast approximations should be used.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary mathematical assumption required for a two-sample permutation test to be valid?

Why: The permutation test requires exchangeability: under H_0, swapping labels between observations does not alter their joint probability distribution.

Q2Why is the continuity correction (1 + extreme) / (B + 1) used when calculating Monte Carlo permutation p-values?

Why: The observed test statistic is itself one valid permutation of the pooled data under H_0. Counting it (+1 in numerator and denominator) guarantees that E[p-value] under H_0 is uniform and non-zero.

Q3What major advantage do permutation tests offer over classical Student's t-tests in A/B testing?

Why: Permutation tests construct the empirical null distribution by direct simulation, freeing analysts to test robust statistics (like medians or 95th percentiles) that have no closed-form analytical distribution.
