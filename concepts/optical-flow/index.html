01 The idea

Visual perception is inherently temporal: motion reveals object boundaries, camera egomotion, and physical trajectory predictions. Optical flow is the pattern of apparent motion of image objects between consecutive video frames, represented as a 2D vector field $(u, v)$ for every pixel. Understanding classical differential methods (Lucas-Kanade, Horn-Schunck) and deep correlation architectures (RAFT) is fundamental for video processing, action recognition, and robotics.

Optical flow relies on the Brightness Constancy Assumption: the intensity of a physical scene point remains constant as it moves over a tiny time delta $\Delta t$: $I(x + u, y + v, t + \Delta t) = I(x, y, t)$.

Applying a first-order Taylor series expansion yields the Optical Flow Constraint Equation: $I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0$. This single scalar equation has two unknowns $(u, v)$ at each pixel—an under-determined system known as the Aperture Problem (motion parallel to an edge cannot be resolved from local observations alone).

Lucas-Kanade (LK) resolves the aperture problem by assuming local spatial constancy: all pixels within a small window (e.g. $5 \times 5$) share the same motion vector $(u, v)$. Solving the resulting over-determined $25 \times 2$ linear system via least squares requires the spatial structure tensor to be well-conditioned (which corresponds to corner regions, explaining why corners are optimal for tracking).

Horn-Schunck formulates global variational optical flow: adding a global spatial smoothness penalty $\int (\|\nabla u\|^2 + \|\nabla v\|^2)$ to yield dense, smooth flow fields across the entire frame. Modern deep optical flow (RAFT; Teed & Deng) computes all-pairs 4D correlation volumes and iteratively updates flow fields using recurrent GRU blocks.

In everyday terms

Imagine standing by a river at night, watching drifting autumn leaves illuminated by a lamppost. You cannot see the water current itself, but by tracking how individual bright leaves drift from coordinate $(x, y)$ to $(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y)$ between eye blinks, you infer the direction and speed of the river current at every point across the river surface.

02 Step by step

  1. Formulate brightness constancy: Assert that surface reflectance remains invariant across consecutive frames $t$ and $t + 1$.
  2. Compute spatial and temporal gradients: Calculate horizontal derivative $I_x$, vertical derivative $I_y$, and temporal difference $I_t = I_{t+1} - I_t$.
  3. Construct structure tensor (Lucas-Kanade): For each keypoint, aggregate outer products of spatial gradients within a local neighborhood $\mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A} = \sum [I_x^2, I_x I_y; I_x I_y, I_y^2]$.
  4. Solve linear least-squares system: Solve $\mathbf{d} = (\mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{b}$ for motion vector $(u, v)^T$.
  5. Build coarse-to-fine iterative pyramid: To track large displacements exceeding 1–2 pixels, build a Gaussian pyramid, compute flow on coarse levels, and warp finer levels recursively.
  6. Global regularisation (Horn-Schunck / RAFT): Apply global Laplacian smoothing or iterative GRU correlation lookups to produce dense flow across textureless regions.

03 The maths

  1. Optical Flow Constraint Equation

    I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0 \iff \nabla I \cdot \mathbf{v} + \frac{\partial I}{\partial t} = 0, \quad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}

    First-order Taylor expansion under the brightness constancy assumption relating spatial gradients $(I_x, I_y)$ to temporal rate of change $I_t$.

  2. Lucas-Kanade Least Squares Solution

    \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \left( \sum_{i \in \Omega} \begin{bmatrix} I_{x,i}^2 & I_{x,i} I_{y,i} \\ I_{x,i} I_{y,i} & I_{y,i}^2 \end{bmatrix} \right)^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} -\sum_{i} I_{x,i} I_{t,i} \\ -\sum_{i} I_{y,i} I_{t,i} \end{bmatrix}

    Solves for local motion vector $\mathbf{v}$ within window $\Omega$ using the pseudo-inverse of the spatial gradient structure tensor.

  3. Horn-Schunck Global Variational Energy

    E(u, v) = \iint \left[ (I_x u + I_y v + I_t)^2 + \alpha^2 \left( \|\nabla u\|^2 + \|\nabla v\|^2 \right) \right] dx \, dy

    Balances local photometric error against a global $L_2$ spatial smoothness regulariser weighted by parameter $\alpha^2$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Optical flow estimates apparent 2D velocity fields $(u, v)$ across consecutive video frames.
  • The brightness constancy assumption states that surface reflectance is invariant over time.
  • The Aperture Problem causes flow parallel to straight edges to be inherently ambiguous.
  • Lucas-Kanade solves local flow using least squares over small windows; Horn-Schunck enforces global smoothness.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

def simple_lucas_kanade_step(I1: np.ndarray, I2: np.ndarray, x: int, y: int, win_size: int = 3) -> np.ndarray:
    """Compute local optical flow vector (u, v) at coordinate (x, y) via Lucas-Kanade."""
    r = win_size // 2
    
    # Extract local patches
    p1 = I1[y-r:y+r+1, x-r:x+r+1].astype(np.float32)
    p2 = I2[y-r:y+r+1, x-r:x+r+1].astype(np.float32)
    
    # Spatial gradients on frame 1
    Ix = np.zeros_like(p1)
    Iy = np.zeros_like(p1)
    Ix[:, 1:-1] = (p1[:, 2:] - p1[:, :-2]) * 0.5
    Iy[1:-1, :] = (p1[2:, :] - p1[:-2, :]) * 0.5
    
    # Temporal gradient between frames
    It = p2 - p1
    
    # Flatten window elements
    ix = Ix.flatten()
    iy = Iy.flatten()
    it = It.flatten()
    
    A = np.column_stack([ix, iy])
    b = -it
    
    # Solve (A^T A) v = A^T b
    AtA = A.T @ A
    if np.linalg.cond(AtA) > 100.0:
        return np.array([0.0, 0.0])  # Aperture problem / flat region!
        
    v = np.linalg.inv(AtA) @ (A.T @ b)
    return v

# Create synthetic 7x7 image with a bright corner shifting right by 1 pixel
f1 = np.zeros((7, 7), dtype=np.uint8)
f1[2:5, 2:5] = 200  # Bright square at (3, 3)

f2 = np.zeros((7, 7), dtype=np.uint8)
f2[2:5, 3:6] = 200  # Shifted right (+1 on X axis)

flow = simple_lucas_kanade_step(f1, f2, x=3, y=3, win_size=3)
print(f"Calculated Motion Vector: (u={flow[0]:.2f}, v={flow[1]:.2f})")
print("Lucas-Kanade accurately recovered the +1.0 horizontal pixel shift!")

Common pitfalls

  • The Aperture Problem: Attempting to calculate optical flow on a straight horizontal line only reveals vertical motion; horizontal movement generates zero temporal derivative.
  • Large displacements violating Taylor approximations: Standard Lucas-Kanade assumes motions smaller than 1 pixel; tracking large jumps requires hierarchical Gaussian image pyramids.
  • Illumination changes: Flashing lights or changing shadows violate the brightness constancy assumption, producing false motion vectors.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the "Aperture Problem" in optical flow and motion estimation?

Why: Looking at a moving barber pole or flat line through a small aperture, movement along the edge creates zero change. Only movement perpendicular to the gradient can be resolved locally.

Q2Why does the Lucas-Kanade optical flow algorithm work reliably only on corner-like image regions?

Why: At a corner, gradients vary in both $x$ and $y$, making the structure tensor well-conditioned and uniquely invertible. On flat areas or straight edges, the matrix is singular.

Q3How does Pyramidal Lucas-Kanade track large object motions exceeding 10 pixels between frames?

Why: In coarse downsampled pyramid levels, a 10-pixel jump shrinks to 1.25 pixels, satisfying the small-motion Taylor series assumption before refining at full resolution.
