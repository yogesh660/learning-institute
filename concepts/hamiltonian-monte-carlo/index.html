Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) overcome the random-walk diffusion limits of classical Metropolis-Hastings by transforming Bayesian parameter sampling into physical Hamiltonian dynamics. By augmenting target parameters (position) with auxiliary momentum vectors, HMC simulates frictionless motion across the negative log-posterior potential energy surface using symplectic numerical integrators (leapfrog algorithm). This physical trajectory simulation enables large, distant state transitions with acceptance rates approaching 100%, scaling Bayesian inference to models with thousands of correlated parameters.

01 The idea

Random Walk Metropolis-Hastings is like taking drunken random steps in the dark: in a 100-dimensional space, almost every random direction steps into low-probability regions, causing acceptance rates to plummet unless step sizes are reduced to microscopic increments. In high dimensions, random walks take millions of iterations to explore the posterior. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo replaces random diffusion with physical mechanics: imagine rolling a frictionless hockey puck down the gravitational contours of your posterior landscape. Why does simulating classical mechanics solve high-dimensional Bayesian inference, and how does the leapfrog algorithm preserve energy?

The Curse of Dimensionality in Random Walks: In high dimensions, probability mass does not concentrate at the mode (where volume is zero) nor in the tails (where density is zero). It concentrates in a narrow hyperspherical shell called the "typical set". A random walk step almost always steps off this narrow ridge, getting rejected. To make distant jumps that stay within the typical set, we need vectors that guide proposals along the contours of the probability landscape.

The Hamiltonian Physics Analogy: - Position (\mathbf{q}): The model parameters we want to sample (\boldsymbol{\theta}). - Potential Energy (U(\mathbf{q})): The negative log-posterior density -\ln p(\mathbf{q} \mid \mathcal{D}). High probability corresponds to deep valleys. - Momentum (\mathbf{p}): Auxiliary random velocities sampled from a Gaussian distribution \mathbf{p} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{M}). - Kinetic Energy (K(\mathbf{p})): \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{p}^\top \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{p}. - Total Hamiltonian Energy: H(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) = U(\mathbf{q}) + K(\mathbf{p}).

Frictionless Gliding: By Hamiltonian conservation of energy, as the particle rolls downhill into high probability valleys, potential energy converts into kinetic energy (it speeds up). As it climbs uphill into low probability tails, kinetic energy converts into potential energy (it slows down). The total energy H remains strictly constant! Because total energy is conserved, the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability \exp(-\Delta H) is identically 1.0!

The Leapfrog Integrator: Computers cannot simulate continuous differential equations perfectly. Standard Euler integration accumulates energy drift, causing the puck to fly into space. The leapfrog algorithm is "symplectic"—it alternates half-step momentum updates with full-step position updates, preserving phase space volume (Liouville's theorem) and maintaining near-perfect energy conservation over long trajectories.

In everyday terms

Imagine a skateboarder in a giant U-shaped concrete skate bowl. The bottom of the bowl is the highest posterior probability region. If the skateboarder were a random-walk Metropolis sampler, they would stagger in random directions, falling flat on their face 95% of the time. HMC pushes the skateboarder with a random burst of speed (momentum): the skateboarder effortlessly carves high-speed arcs along the walls of the bowl, gliding across the entire geometry without ever losing total energy, landing at a completely different, distant spot on the bowl with 100% stability.

03 The maths

  1. Hamiltonian System Differential Equations

    \frac{d\mathbf{q}}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{p}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{q}} = -\nabla_\mathbf{q} U(\mathbf{q}) = \nabla_\mathbf{q} \ln p(\mathbf{q} \mid \mathcal{D})

    Hamilton's equations describe the time evolution of position \mathbf{q} and momentum \mathbf{p}. The total energy H(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}) = U(\mathbf{q}) + K(\mathbf{p}) is conserved along continuous trajectories: dH/dt = 0.

  2. Symplectic Leapfrog Integrator

    \mathbf{p}\left(t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\right) = \mathbf{p}(t) - \frac{\epsilon}{2} \nabla U(\mathbf{q}(t)), \quad \mathbf{q}(t + \epsilon) = \mathbf{q}(t) + \epsilon \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{p}\left(t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\right), \quad \mathbf{p}(t + \epsilon) = \mathbf{p}\left(t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\right) - \frac{\epsilon}{2} \nabla U(\mathbf{q}(t + \epsilon))

    The leapfrog algorithm updates momentum at half-integer time steps and position at integer time steps. It is time-reversible and volume-preserving (symplectic), ensuring that energy errors do not drift secularly over time.

  3. Metropolis Acceptance Probability under Numerical Discretisation

    \alpha = \min\left(1, \; \exp\big( -H(\mathbf{q}^*, \mathbf{p}^*) + H(\mathbf{q}^{(t-1)}, \mathbf{p}^{(0)}) \big) \right) = \min\left(1, \; \exp\left( -\Delta H \right)\right)

    Even though continuous dynamics preserve energy exactly (\Delta H = 0), finite step size \epsilon introduces minor discretisation error. The final Metropolis filter guarantees exact convergence to the stationary distribution.

04 Key takeaways

  • Hamiltonian Monte Carlo simulates classical Hamiltonian physical dynamics to propose distant, high-probability state transitions.
  • Auxiliary momentum variables allow the sampler to glide along the contours of the typical set, avoiding random-walk diffusion.
  • The leapfrog numerical integrator is symplectic and time-reversible, preserving phase-space volume and conserving total energy.
  • Because energy is approximately conserved (\Delta H \approx 0), HMC achieves high acceptance rates (> 80%) even in high-dimensional spaces.

05 In code

python
"""
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) Sampler Implementation
Demonstrating leapfrog symplectic integration, energy conservation,
and high acceptance sampling on a correlated 2D Gaussian.
"""

import numpy as np

# Target Potential Energy U(q) = -log p(q)
# Correlated Gaussian: q ~ N(0, Sigma), Sigma = [[1.0, 0.8], [0.8, 1.0]]
cov = np.array([[1.0, 0.8], [0.8, 1.0]])
cov_inv = np.linalg.inv(cov)

def potential_energy(q):
    """U(q) = 0.5 * q^T Sigma^-1 q"""
    return 0.5 * (q @ cov_inv @ q)

def grad_potential_energy(q):
    """nabla U(q) = Sigma^-1 q"""
    return cov_inv @ q

def kinetic_energy(p):
    """K(p) = 0.5 * p^T p (assuming identity mass matrix M = I)"""
    return 0.5 * np.sum(p ** 2)

def leapfrog(q, p, epsilon, num_steps):
    """Symplectic Leapfrog Integrator."""
    q_curr = np.copy(q)
    p_curr = np.copy(p)
    
    # Initial half-step for momentum
    p_curr -= 0.5 * epsilon * grad_potential_energy(q_curr)
    
    # Alternate full position and momentum steps
    for step in range(num_steps):
        q_curr += epsilon * p_curr
        if step < num_steps - 1:
            p_curr -= epsilon * grad_potential_energy(q_curr)
            
    # Final half-step for momentum
    p_curr -= 0.5 * epsilon * grad_potential_energy(q_curr)
    
    # Negate momentum to make transition reversible
    return q_curr, -p_curr

def run_hmc(num_samples=3000, epsilon=0.15, leapfrog_steps=20):
    samples = np.empty((num_samples, 2))
    curr_q = np.array([4.0, -4.0]) # Starting point
    accepted = 0
    delta_h_list = []

    for i in range(num_samples):
        # 1. Sample auxiliary momentum p ~ N(0, I)
        curr_p = np.random.normal(0, 1, size=2)
        
        # 2. Compute initial Hamiltonian H = U(q) + K(p)
        h_init = potential_energy(curr_q) + kinetic_energy(curr_p)
        
        # 3. Simulate Leapfrog Trajectory
        prop_q, prop_p = leapfrog(curr_q, curr_p, epsilon, leapfrog_steps)
        
        # 4. Compute proposed Hamiltonian
        h_prop = potential_energy(prop_q) + kinetic_energy(prop_p)
        delta_h = h_prop - h_init
        delta_h_list.append(delta_h)
        
        # 5. Metropolis Acceptance
        if np.log(np.random.uniform(0, 1)) < -delta_h:
            curr_q = prop_q
            accepted += 1
            
        samples[i] = curr_q

    acc_rate = accepted / num_samples
    return samples, acc_rate, delta_h_list

# Run HMC simulation
np.random.seed(42)
samples, acc_rate, delta_h_list = run_hmc(num_samples=4000, epsilon=0.18, leapfrog_steps=15)
burn_in = 500
clean_samples = samples[burn_in:]

print("=== Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Simulation ===")
print(f"Total Steps: 4000 | Burn-in: {burn_in} | Clean Samples: {len(clean_samples)}")
print(f"HMC Acceptance Rate: {acc_rate:.1%} (High acceptance despite large leapfrog jumps!)")
print(f"Mean Energy Error |Delta H|: {np.mean(np.abs(delta_h_list)):.4f} (Energy is near-conserved!)")

print(f"\nEstimated Posterior Mean: [{np.mean(clean_samples[:, 0]):.3f}, {np.mean(clean_samples[:, 1]):.3f}] (True: [0.00, 0.00])")
print(f"Estimated Correlation:    {np.corrcoef(clean_samples[:, 0], clean_samples[:, 1])[0, 1]:.3f} (True: 0.800)")

Common pitfalls

  • Tuning leapfrog step size epsilon and trajectory length L: Too large an epsilon causes catastrophic numerical divergence; too small causes slow exploration (solved by the NUTS algorithm).
  • Pathological geometry and funnels: In hierarchical models with varying curvature (Neal's funnel), HMC encounters divergent transitions; non-centred parameterisations are necessary.
  • Requiring differentiable posteriors: HMC requires the gradient of the log-posterior \nabla_\mathbf{q} \ln p(\mathbf{q} \mid \mathcal{D}); it cannot be applied directly to discrete parameters without marginalisation.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Hamiltonian Monte Carlo scale vastly better to high dimensions than Random Walk Metropolis?

Why: Random-walk proposals must shrink as dimension grows to keep a sensible acceptance rate, so the cost per independent sample rises roughly linearly with the dimension d (against about d^{1/4} for HMC). HMC translates gradient vector information into momentum, traversing the typical set efficiently.

Q2What property of the leapfrog integrator makes it essential for HMC?

Why: Non-symplectic integrators (like Euler) accumulate energy drift, causing the Hamiltonian to explode. Symplectic integrators oscillate around the true energy surface, preserving phase space volume.

Q3What algorithm automatically tunes the trajectory length L in modern Bayesian probabilistic programming languages (such as Stan and PyMC)?

Why: NUTS builds recursive binary trees of leapfrog steps, automatically terminating the trajectory as soon as the particle begins to turn back on itself (No-U-Turn), eliminating the need for manual tuning of L.
