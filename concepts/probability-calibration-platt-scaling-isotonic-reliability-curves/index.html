Probability Calibration: Platt Scaling, Isotonic Regression and Reliability Curves

Probability calibration aligns raw classifier confidence scores with true empirical event rates, diagnosed using reliability curves and corrected via Platt scaling or isotonic regression.

01 The idea

A classification model can achieve high accuracy and strong AUC while outputting completely uncalibrated confidence scores: when it outputs 0.90 probability, the positive outcome may occur only 50% of the time. In clinical risk estimation, insurance underwriting, and automated fraud routing, numerical probabilities must be reliable. Reliability diagrams diagnose miscalibration, while Platt scaling and Isotonic regression map distorted margins into calibrated posterior probabilities.

Imagine a meteorology app that announces an 80% chance of rain. If you review all 100 days across the year where the app posted an 80% forecast, did it actually rain on approximately 80 of those days? If it only rained on 45 of those days, the forecast is severely overconfident. Conversely, if it rained on 98 of those days, the forecast is underconfident. A classifier is well-calibrated if a predicted probability of $p$ directly corresponds to an empirical positive frequency of $p$.

Different model architectures exhibit characteristic calibration flaws: - **Support Vector Machines (SVMs)** output uncalibrated signed distances to the separating hyperplane; standard margins have no probabilistic meaning. - **Naive Bayes** pushes probabilities toward 0.0 and 1.0 because multiplying correlated feature probabilities compounds evidence, producing a distinct sigmoidal distortion in reliability curves. - **Deep Neural Networks** with modern regularisation (dropout, weight decay, cross-entropy over-parameterisation) are systematically overconfident: they achieve high accuracy while assigning 99% confidence to borderline instances (Guo et al., 2017). - **Random Forests** struggle near 0 and 1 because tree leaf averaging pushes ensemble predictions inward toward the historical base rate.

To calibrate a model, we fit a monotonic post-processing function $g(s)$ on held-out validation data. **Platt scaling** fits a two-parameter logistic sigmoid $1 / (1 + e^{A s + B})$, making it ideal for sigmoidal distortions (such as SVM margins) or modest validation sets ($n < 1,000$). **Isotonic regression** fits a non-parametric, piece-wise constant non-decreasing step function using the Pool Adjacent Violators Algorithm (PAVA). Isotonic regression can correct arbitrary monotonic distortions without parametric assumptions, but requires larger validation datasets to prevent step-fitting overfitting.

In everyday terms

Calibration is like zeroing and aligning a bathroom scale: the scale might accurately tell you that you weigh more than your friend (ranking discrimination), but until it is calibrated against certified weights, you cannot trust the actual number on the display.

02 Step by step

  1. Generate uncalibrated decision scores or raw probabilities $s_i$ on a dedicated held-out validation set (never calibrate on training data).
  2. Construct a **Reliability Diagram (Calibration Curve)**: bin predictions into $M$ equal intervals (e.g. $[0, 0.1), [0.1, 0.2), \dots$) and plot mean predicted confidence against observed fraction of positives.
  3. Compute the **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)** and **Brier Score** to quantify baseline calibration error.
  4. Select a calibrator: choose **Platt Scaling** if the miscalibration is sigmoidal or validation data is modest ($n < 1,000$), or **Isotonic Regression** if the distortion is non-sigmoidal and validation data is plentiful.
  5. Fit the post-processing calibration mapping $g(s)$ on validation scores using maximum likelihood or least squares.
  6. Apply the fitted calibrator to test predictions, verifying that ECE and Brier score improve while ROC-AUC ranking discrimination remains preserved.

03 The maths

  1. Definition of perfect calibration

    $$\mathbb{P}\big(Y = 1 \mid \hat{P} = p\big) = p \quad \forall p \in [0, 1]$$

    $\hat{P}$ is the model's predicted probability, and $Y \in \\{0, 1\\}$ is the true binary label. Perfect calibration guarantees that among all instances assigned probability $p$, exactly a fraction $p$ are positive outcomes.

  2. Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

    $$\operatorname{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{n} \Big| \operatorname{acc}(B_m) - \operatorname{conf}(B_m) \Big|$$

    Predictions are grouped into $M$ bins $B_m$. $|B_m|/n$ is the bin sample weight, $\operatorname{acc}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i \in B_m} y_i$ is empirical bin accuracy, and $\operatorname{conf}(B_m) = \frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i \in B_m} \hat{p}_i$ is mean predicted confidence. ECE computes the weighted absolute divergence between confidence and reality.

  3. Platt scaling and Isotonic regression

    $$\hat{P}_{\text{Platt}} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(A s + B)}, \quad \min_{g} \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - g(s_i)\big)^2 \\;\\; \text{s.t.} \\;\\; g(s_i) \le g(s_j) \text{ for } s_i \le s_j$$

    Platt scaling optimises parameters $A$ and $B$ via negative log-likelihood on validation margins $s_i$. Isotonic regression fits a non-parametric monotonic step function $g(s)$ via the Pool Adjacent Violators Algorithm (PAVA), preserving rank order while minimising mean squared error.

04 Key takeaways

  • Discrimination vs Calibration: ROC-AUC evaluates ranking discrimination, whereas calibration evaluates whether probability magnitudes match empirical event frequencies.
  • Monotonicity preservation: monotonic calibrators (Platt scaling and Isotonic regression) do not alter prediction rank order, leaving ROC-AUC completely unchanged.
  • The Brier Score ($\frac{1}{n}\sum (y_i - \hat{p}_i)^2$) decomposes mathematically into uncertainty, reliability (calibration error), and resolution (discrimination).
  • Always calibrate on held-out data: fitting calibration transformations on training sets perpetuates model overconfidence.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 300
z = np.random.normal(0, 1.2, n)
p_true = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))
y = (np.random.uniform(0, 1, n) < p_true).astype(int)

# Uncalibrated raw decision margins
raw_scores = 2.5 * z + 0.8

def compute_ece(probs, labels, M=5):
    bins = np.linspace(0, 1, M + 1)
    ece = 0.0
    for m in range(M):
        mask = (probs >= bins[m]) & (probs < bins[m+1] if m < M-1 else probs <= bins[m+1])
        if np.sum(mask) == 0: continue
        bin_acc = np.mean(labels[mask])
        bin_conf = np.mean(probs[mask])
        ece += (np.sum(mask) / len(labels)) * abs(bin_acc - bin_conf)
    return ece

# Raw min-max scaled scores
p_raw = (raw_scores - raw_scores.min()) / (raw_scores.max() - raw_scores.min())
ece_raw = compute_ece(p_raw, y)
brier_raw = np.mean((p_raw - y)**2)

# Platt scaling: fit A, B via gradient descent on validation log-loss
A, B = -1.0, 0.0
lr = 0.05
for _ in range(500):
    logits = A * raw_scores + B
    p_platt = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(logits, -15, 15)))
    A -= lr * np.mean((p_platt - y) * raw_scores)
    B -= lr * np.mean(p_platt - y)

p_cal = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(A * raw_scores + B, -15, 15)))
ece_cal = compute_ece(p_cal, y)
brier_cal = np.mean((p_cal - y)**2)

print("Fitted Platt parameters: A =", round(float(A), 3), "B =", round(float(B), 3))
print("Uncalibrated ECE:", round(float(ece_raw), 3), "Brier Score:", round(float(brier_raw), 3))
print("Platt Calibrated ECE:", round(float(ece_cal), 3), "Brier Score:", round(float(brier_cal), 3))

Common pitfalls

  • Calibrating on the training set: training predictions are naturally overconfident; calibrating on them freezes this overconfidence into production.
  • Overfitting with Isotonic Regression on small samples ($n < 500$): unconstrained step functions fit sample noise, producing flat, discontinuous probability steps.
  • Assuming high ROC-AUC implies good calibration: modern deep neural networks with 95% AUC frequently exhibit severe calibration drift.
  • Class prevalence shifts: calibration parameters $A$ and $B$ are sensitive to the base rate of the calibration set; if test prevalence shifts, probabilities become biased.

06 Check yourself

Q1When is Platt scaling generally preferred over Isotonic regression for probability calibration?

Why: Platt scaling fits only two parameters ($A$ and $B$), avoiding the step-function overfitting that Isotonic regression suffers from on small validation samples.

Q2What does it mean for a probabilistic classifier to be "well-calibrated"?

Why: Calibration evaluates whether predicted probability values match true observed event rates: an 80% prediction should be correct roughly 80% of the time.

Q3What effect does monotonic calibration (such as Platt scaling or Isotonic regression) have on a classifier's ROC-AUC score?

Why: ROC-AUC depends purely on the relative rank order of predicted scores. Monotonic transformations preserve rank ordering, leaving the ROC curve and its AUC invariant.

Q4Why are raw decision margins from Support Vector Machines (SVMs) uncalibrated?

Why: Standard SVMs optimise geometric margins, not likelihoods. The output $w^T x + b$ represents distance to the hyperplane, which carries no direct probabilistic meaning without post-hoc scaling.
