Odds and Log-Odds (the Logit)

Probability, odds and log-odds are three views of the same number. Log-odds is the scale on which logistic regression is linear.

01 The idea

A probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1 and log-odds of $\ln 4 \approx 1.39$. Log-odds stretch the 0–1 range of probability onto the whole real line, which is what makes a linear model of probabilities possible.

Probabilities are cramped near 0 and 1: going from 0.98 to 0.99 is a big change in certainty but looks tiny. Odds $p/(1-p)$ spread the upper end out to infinity. Taking the log makes the scale symmetric: $p = 0.5$ maps to 0, $p = 0.9$ to $+2.2$, $p = 0.1$ to $-2.2$.

On the log-odds scale, independent pieces of evidence add. That is why Logistic regression models log-odds as a weighted sum and why its coefficients read as "each unit adds $w$ to the log-odds, multiplying the odds by $e^w$".

In everyday terms

A betting slip: "4 to 1 on" is odds; log-odds is the same bet on a scale where evidence for and against simply adds up.

02 Step by step

  1. Probability: $p \in [0, 1]$.
  2. Odds: $p / (1 - p) \in [0, \infty)$.
  3. Log-odds (logit): $\log\frac{p}{1-p} \in (-\infty, \infty)$.
  4. Invert with the sigmoid: $p = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$.

04 The maths

  1. The logit and its inverse

    $$z = \operatorname{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1 - p}, \qquad p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

    Each is the inverse of the other.

  2. Odds ratio

    $$\frac{\text{odds}(x_j + 1)}{\text{odds}(x_j)} = e^{w_j}$$

    In logistic regression, a coefficient of 0.69 doubles the odds ($e^{0.69} \approx 2$).

05 Key takeaways

  • Log-odds is symmetric around 0 (p = 0.5).
  • Evidence adds on the log-odds scale.
  • Odds ratios, not probability differences, are constant per unit in logistic regression.

06 In code

python
import numpy as np
p = np.array([0.1, 0.5, 0.8, 0.99])
odds = p / (1 - p)
logit = np.log(odds)
back = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))
print(np.c_[p, odds.round(2), logit.round(2), back])

Common pitfalls

  • Reading a logistic coefficient as a change in probability. It is a change in log-odds; the probability change depends on where you start.

07 Check yourself

Q1What are the log-odds of $p = 0.5$?

Why: Odds are 1, and $\log 1 = 0$.

Q2A log-odds increase of $\ln 3$ means the odds:

Why: Adding $\ln 3$ to log-odds multiplies odds by 3.

Q3What is the range of the logit (log-odds) function $\operatorname{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right)$ for probability $p \in (0, 1)$?

Why: As $p \to 0$, $p/(1-p) \to 0$ and $\ln(p/(1-p)) \to -\infty$. As $p \to 1$, $p/(1-p) \to \infty$ and $\ln(p/(1-p)) \to +\infty$. Mapping $(0, 1)$ to $(-\infty, \infty)$ allows linear models to output unbounded real scores without violating probability bounds.

Q4If an event has a probability of $p = 0.80$, what are the odds of the event occurring?

Why: $\text{Odds} = \frac{p}{1 - p} = \frac{0.80}{1 - 0.80} = \frac{0.80}{0.20} = 4.0$. For every 1 time the event fails to occur, it occurs 4 times.

Q5How do the log-odds of complementary probabilities $p$ and $1 - p$ relate to each other?

Why: $\operatorname{logit}(1 - p) = \ln\left(\frac{1 - p}{1 - (1 - p)}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{1 - p}{p}\right) = -\ln\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right) = -\operatorname{logit}(p)$.

Q6If the odds of a horse winning a race are given as $3$ (that is, 3 to 1 in favour), what is the probability that the horse wins?

Why: To convert odds to probability: $p = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}} = \frac{3}{1 + 3} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.750$ (or 75%). A common mistake is dividing by the odds ($1/3$) rather than odds $+ 1$.

Q7In a logistic regression model, feature $x_1$ has a coefficient of $w_1 = -0.693$ (where $e^{-0.693} \approx 0.50$). For each 1-unit increase in $x_1$ (holding other features constant), what happens to the odds of the positive outcome?

Why: In logistic regression, coefficients represent changes in log-odds. Adding $-0.693$ to the log-odds multiplies the odds by $e^{-0.693} \approx 0.50$, effectively halving the odds.

Q8Why does a constant 1-unit increase in a feature $x_j$ NOT produce a constant percentage-point increase in probability in logistic regression?

Why: Logistic regression is linear in log-odds, but probability is passed through the sigmoid. Adding $+1$ to log-odds moves $p$ from $0.50$ to $0.73$ ($+23$ points), but moving from $p = 0.98$ to $p = 0.99$ requires a much larger log-odds jump.

Q9How does Bayes' theorem naturally simplify when expressed on the log-odds scale?

Why: Bayes' rule in odds form is $\frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(\neg A \mid B)} = \frac{P(A)}{P(\neg A)} \times \frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid \neg A)}$. Taking logarithms turns multiplication into addition, showing why linear models add evidence terms directly in log-odds space.

Q10A patient has a baseline predicted probability of disease of $p = 0.20$ (baseline odds $= 0.20 / 0.80 = 0.25$). A clinical test has a positive likelihood ratio of $8.0$. Using the odds formulation of Bayes' rule, what are the updated posterior odds and the updated posterior probability of disease?

Why: $\text{Posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times \text{likelihood ratio} = 0.25 \times 8.0 = 2.0$ (or 2 to 1 in favour). Converting back to probability: $p = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}} = \frac{2.0}{1 + 2.0} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.6667 = 66.7\%$.

Q11For a logistic regression model with intercept $w_0 = -1.0$ and single coefficient $w_1 = 0.50$, what is the predicted probability $p$ for an observation with input $x = 2.0$?

Why: Log-odds score $z = w_0 + w_1 x = -1.0 + 0.50(2.0) = -1.0 + 1.0 = 0.0$. Applying the sigmoid function: $p = \sigma(0) = \frac{1}{1 + e^0} = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.50$.

Q12A logistic regression model has two features with weights $w_1 = \ln(2) \approx 0.6931$ and $w_2 = \ln(3) \approx 1.0986$, and intercept $w_0 = 0$. If an instance has $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 1$, what are the exact odds and the exact probability $P(y=1)$?

Why: Log-odds $z = w_0 + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 = 0 + \ln(2) + \ln(3) = \ln(2 \times 3) = \ln(6)$. Exponentiating to get odds: $\text{odds} = e^{\ln(6)} = 6.0$. Converting to probability: $p = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}} = \frac{6}{1 + 6} = \frac{6}{7} \approx 0.8571 = 85.7\%$.
