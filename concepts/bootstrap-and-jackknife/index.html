Bootstrap and Jackknife

Non-parametric resampling for uncertainty estimation: sample with replacement to bootstrap any statistic, or leave one out with the jackknife.

01 The idea

When analytical formulas for standard errors do not exist—such as for medians, complex ratios, or pipeline metrics—resampling methods provide a universal computational solution. The bootstrap treats the empirical sample as a proxy population and resamples with replacement, while the jackknife systematically leaves out one observation at a time.

Imagine you calculate the median household wealth in a small sample of 50 families. Calculating the standard error of a mean is trivial ($s / \sqrt{n}$), but what is the formula for the standard error of a sample median? Under arbitrary or skewed distributions, there is no simple textbook formula. Historically, statisticians were forced to make rigid parametric assumptions or give up on interval estimates entirely.

In 1979, Bradley Efron proposed the bootstrap: "pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps". If your sample of 50 families is the best representation of the population you have, treat it as the population. Put all 50 values into an imaginary urn, draw 50 families with replacement (so some appear multiple times and others not at all), and compute the median. Repeat this computational drawing 2,000 times. The spread of those 2,000 recomputed medians gives you an accurate standard error, and their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give you an empirical 95% confidence interval.

The jackknife is an older, deterministic cousin pioneered by Maurice Quenouille and John Tukey. Instead of random sampling with replacement, the jackknife leaves out observation 1 and recalculates the statistic on the remaining $n - 1$ points, then leaves out observation 2, and so on. This produces $n$ leave-one-out replicates, offering a fast, non-random diagnostic for estimator bias and variance.

In everyday terms

Bootstrapping is like photocopying your sample, cutting it into slips of paper, and drawing new random datasets from the hat to see how sensitive your conclusion is.

02 Step by step

  1. Given an original dataset $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$, compute the point estimate of interest $\hat{\theta} = T(\mathbf{x})$.
  2. For each of $B$ bootstrap iterations (typically $B \in [1000, 5000]$), draw a sample $\mathbf{x}^{*b}$ of size $n$ with replacement from $\mathbf{x}$.
  3. Compute the replicate estimate $\hat{\theta}^{*b} = T(\mathbf{x}^{*b})$ on each resampled dataset.
  4. Estimate the bootstrap standard error as the sample standard deviation of the $B$ replicates: $\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}_B = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1}\sum (\hat{\theta}^{*b} - \bar{\theta}^*)^2}$.
  5. Construct confidence intervals: use the percentile method ($[\hat{\theta}^*_{\alpha/2}, \, \hat{\theta}^*_{1-\alpha/2}]$) or bias-corrected accelerated ($\text{BC}_a$) intervals.
  6. For the jackknife, compute $n$ leave-one-out replicates $\hat{\theta}_{(-i)} = T(\mathbf{x} \setminus \{x_i\})$ to estimate bias $\widehat{\text{Bias}}_{\text{jack}} = (n-1)(\bar{\theta}_{(\cdot)} - \hat{\theta})$.

03 The maths

  1. Bootstrap Standard Error and Percentile Interval

    $$\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}_{\text{boot}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B - 1} \sum_{b=1}^B \left(\hat{\theta}^{*b} - \bar{\theta}^*\right)^2}, \qquad \text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[ q_{\alpha/2}\left(\{\hat{\theta}^{*b}\}\right), \; q_{1-\alpha/2}\left(\{\hat{\theta}^{*b}\}\right) \right]$$

    The empirical bootstrap standard error is the standard deviation across all $B$ bootstrap estimates $\hat{\theta}^{*b}$. The percentile confidence interval takes the empirical quantiles of the bootstrap distribution directly, requiring zero normality assumptions.

  2. The 63.2% Rule of Sampling with Replacement

    $$P(\text{observation } i \text{ selected}) = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321$$

    When drawing $n$ items with replacement from a set of size $n$, each observation has probability $1/n$ of being picked on any single draw. In the limit, each bootstrap sample contains roughly 63.2% unique original observations, leaving approximately 36.8% out-of-bag (OOB).

  3. Jackknife Variance and Bias Estimators

    $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\text{jack}}(\hat{\theta}) = \frac{n - 1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\hat{\theta}_{(-i)} - \bar{\theta}_{(\cdot)}\right)^2, \qquad \widehat{\text{Bias}}_{\text{jack}} = (n - 1)(\bar{\theta}_{(\cdot)} - \hat{\theta})$$

    The jackknife leaves out each observation $i$ sequentially to compute $\hat{\theta}_{(-i)}$. The scale factor $\frac{n-1}{n}$ corrects for the fact that the leave-one-out subsamples are heavily overlapping and far more similar to each other than independent samples would be.

04 Key takeaways

  • Bootstrapping requires no parametric distribution assumptions; it works for medians, ratios, eigenvalues, and complex ML metric pipelines.
  • Roughly 63.2% of the distinct original observations appear in each bootstrap sample, leaving 36.8% out-of-bag (the foundation of Random Forest OOB error).
  • The standard jackknife fails on non-smooth statistics (such as the sample median); the delete-$d$ jackknife or bootstrap must be used instead.
  • Percentile bootstrap intervals can be biased for skewed distributions; the $\text{BC}_a$ (bias-corrected and accelerated) method adjusts for skewness and acceleration.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
# Skewed sample of 50 observations (e.g. server latencies in ms)
data = rng.lognormal(mean=3.0, sigma=0.8, size=50)

# Non-parametric statistic: sample median
sample_median = np.median(data)

# Bootstrap estimation (B = 2,000 resamples)
B = 2000
boot_medians = np.zeros(B)
n = len(data)

for b in range(B):
    resample = rng.choice(data, size=n, replace=True)
    boot_medians[b] = np.median(resample)

boot_se = np.std(boot_medians, ddof=1)
ci_lower = np.percentile(boot_medians, 2.5)
ci_upper = np.percentile(boot_medians, 97.5)

print(f"Sample Median: {sample_median:.2f}")
print(f"Bootstrap SE: {boot_se:.3f}")
print(f"95% Percentile CI: [{ci_lower:.2f}, {ci_upper:.2f}]")

Common pitfalls

  • Bootstrapping time series data with simple i.i.d. resampling, which destroys serial autocorrelation; block bootstrap must be used instead.
  • Using the standard leave-one-out jackknife for the median; removing one point changes the median in discrete jumps, leading to inconsistent variance estimates.
  • Believing the bootstrap creates new information; if the original sample is small or unrepresentative, the bootstrap simply replicates that bias.
  • Using too few bootstrap replications; while $B = 200$ suffices for standard errors, confidence interval tails require $B \ge 1,000$ to $2,000$.

06 Check yourself

Q1What approximate percentage of unique original training instances are included in any single standard bootstrap sample of size $n$?

Why: Because sampling is done with replacement, each point has probability $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321$ (or 63.2%) of appearing at least once.

Q2For which of the following statistics does the classical leave-one-out jackknife fail to provide a consistent variance estimate?

Why: The jackknife requires the estimator to be smooth (differentiable). The sample median is non-smooth; deleting a single point causes discontinuous jumps in rank.

Q3Why is naive i.i.d. bootstrapping inappropriate for time-series forecasting models?

Why: Independent resampling scrambles the sequence of observations, destroying serial dependence. Time-series data requires block bootstrapping to preserve local autocorrelation.

Q4What is the primary advantage of the bootstrap over classical asymptotic normal confidence intervals?

Why: The bootstrap is fully non-parametric and computational: it estimates standard errors and quantiles directly from empirical resampling without requiring closed-form calculus derivations or Gaussian assumptions.

Q5In standard non-parametric bootstrapping, how are resamples of size $n$ drawn from the original dataset of $n$ observations?

Why: The core mechanism of Efron's non-parametric bootstrap is drawing samples of size $n$ with replacement from the empirical dataset, treating the empirical sample as a proxy for the unknown population.

Q6How does the standard leave-one-out jackknife differ in execution from the standard bootstrap?

Why: For a sample of size $n$, the leave-one-out jackknife systematically omits observation $i$ for each $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, producing exactly $n$ deterministic subsets without random sampling.

Q7When applying the bootstrap to estimate confidence interval percentiles (such as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for a 95% CI), approximately how many bootstrap replications $B$ are recommended as a minimum?

Why: While estimating standard errors requires only $B \approx 100\text{ to }200$ replicates, estimating extreme tail quantiles (2.5% and 97.5%) reliably requires significantly more iterations, typically $B \ge 1,000\text{ to }2,000$ to reduce Monte Carlo simulation noise.

Q8After drawing $B = 1,000$ bootstrap replicates of a model's test accuracy, you sort the 1,000 replicate accuracies in ascending order. Under the empirical percentile method, which sorted ranks define the two-sided 95% confidence interval?

Why: For a two-sided 95% confidence interval, $\alpha = 0.05$. The tail cutoff quantiles are $\alpha/2 = 0.025$ and $1 - \alpha/2 = 0.975$. Out of 1,000 sorted values, $0.025 \times 1,000 = 25$ and $0.975 \times 1,000 = 975$.

Q9When bootstrapping serially correlated time-series data, why is the block bootstrap used instead of individual point resampling?

Why: Independent resampling scrambles the chronological order of observations, destroying serial dependence. Block bootstrapping samples contiguous blocks of length $L$, preserving the autocorrelation structure within each block.

Q10Suppose a tiny dataset consists of $n = 3$ observations: $\{A, B, C\}$. If a bootstrap sample of size 3 is drawn uniformly with replacement, what is the exact probability that observation $A$ is omitted entirely (i.e. is out-of-bag)?

Why: On any single draw, the probability of selecting an observation other than $A$ is $(1 - 1/3) = 2/3$. Across 3 independent draws with replacement, the probability that $A$ is never chosen is $(2/3)^3 = 8/27 \approx 0.2963$. (As $n \to \infty$, $(1 - 1/n)^n \to 1/e \approx 0.368$).

Q11A researcher has $n = 4$ observations: $[2, 4, 6, 8]$. The overall sample mean is $\hat{\theta} = 5.0$. The four leave-one-out means are: $\hat{\theta}_{(-1)} = 6.0$, $\hat{\theta}_{(-2)} = 5.333$, $\hat{\theta}_{(-3)} = 4.667$, $\hat{\theta}_{(-4)} = 4.0$. What is the average of these leave-one-out means $\bar{\theta}_{(\cdot)}$, and what does the jackknife bias estimate $\widehat{\text{Bias}} = (n - 1)(\bar{\theta}_{(\cdot)} - \hat{\theta})$ evaluate to?

Why: The average of the leave-one-out means is $\bar{\theta}_{(\cdot)} = (6.0 + 16/3 + 14/3 + 4.0) / 4 = 20.0 / 4 = 5.0$. The jackknife bias estimate is $(4 - 1)(5.0 - 5.0) = 3 \times 0.0 = 0.0$, correctly confirming that the sample mean is an unbiased estimator.

Q12Why is the bias-corrected and accelerated ($\text{BC}_a$) bootstrap interval preferred over the simple percentile interval for skewed estimators?

Why: The simple percentile interval assumes transformation symmetry. When the sampling distribution of $\hat{\theta}$ is skewed or its variance varies with the true parameter (non-zero acceleration), simple percentile intervals suffer from substantial coverage error. $\text{BC}_a$ adjusts the percentile cutoff indices for both median bias $z_0$ and acceleration $a$.
