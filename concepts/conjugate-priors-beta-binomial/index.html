01 The idea

In Bayesian statistics, updating beliefs via Bayes' theorem often involves intractable integrals that require complex Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations. Conjugate priors provide an elegant algebraic shortcut: when the prior and likelihood belong to a conjugate family, the posterior distribution belongs to the exact same family as the prior. The Beta–Binomial model serves as the canonical example of conjugacy, updating binary conversion beliefs by simply adding observed successes and failures to prior hyperparameters.

Imagine launching a brand-new website feature where you want to estimate the conversion rate $\theta$. Before collecting data, you have prior experience from dozens of similar features: conversions typically hover around 20%, but rarely drop below 5% or exceed 45%.

In Bayesian terms, we represent this initial uncertainty with a Beta distribution: $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$. The hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can be intuitively interpreted as "pseudo-observations": $\alpha$ prior successes and $\beta$ prior failures. Setting $\alpha = 4$ and $\beta = 16$ encodes a prior belief centred at $\frac{4}{4 + 16} = 20\%$, backed by the equivalent strength of 20 prior visitors.

Now, run a live experiment: 100 new visitors arrive, and 35 convert (35 successes, 65 failures). Because the Beta distribution is conjugate to the Binomial likelihood, computing the posterior does not require calculus or numerical integration. You simply add live counts directly to your prior counts: $\alpha_{\text{post}} = 4 + 35 = 39$ and $\beta_{\text{post}} = 16 + 65 = 81$. The updated posterior is $\text{Beta}(39, 81)$, with posterior mean $\frac{39}{39 + 81} = 32.5\%$ and 95% credible interval $[24.4\%, 41.1\%]$. The prior gently regularises the noisy raw sample rate of 35% toward baseline beliefs.

In everyday terms

Updating a Beta prior with Binomial data is like adding tallies to a score sheet: you start with a few pencil marks representing previous games, and each new game simply adds tally marks in the win and loss columns without having to redesign the sheet.

02 Step by step

  1. Specify the parameter of interest: a success probability $\theta \in [0, 1]$ governing binary Bernoulli or Binomial trials.
  2. Select prior hyperparameters $\alpha_0$ and $\beta_0$ to encode baseline domain knowledge (or set $\alpha_0 = \beta_0 = 1$ for a flat uniform prior).
  3. Observe experimental data: record the number of successes $k$ out of $n$ independent trials (with $n - k$ failures).
  4. Compute posterior hyperparameters in closed form: $\alpha_1 = \alpha_0 + k$ and $\beta_1 = \beta_0 + n - k$.
  5. Calculate summary properties of the posterior $\text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \beta_1)$: posterior mean $\frac{\alpha_1}{\alpha_1 + \beta_1}$ and credible intervals via Beta quantiles.
  6. Use the posterior distribution as the prior for subsequent incoming batches of data in a streaming, sequential learning pipeline.

03 The maths

  1. Beta Prior and Binomial Likelihood

    $$P(\theta) = \frac{\theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}}{\text{B}(\alpha, \beta)}, \qquad P(k \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{k} \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k}$$

    Here $\theta$ is the success probability, $\text{B}(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}$ is the Beta normalisation constant, and $\alpha, \beta > 0$ are shape hyperparameters. The Binomial likelihood gives the probability of observing $k$ successes in $n$ independent trials given rate $\theta$.

  2. Posterior Derivation via Conjugacy

    $$P(\theta \mid k) \propto P(k \mid \theta) P(\theta) \propto \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k} \cdot \theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1} = \theta^{(\alpha + k) - 1} (1 - \theta)^{(\beta + n - k) - 1}$$

    Multiplying likelihood and prior preserves the exact algebraic functional form: the exponents sum directly. The posterior distribution is identically $\text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \, \beta + n - k)$, proving that the Beta family is conjugate to the Binomial likelihood.

  3. Posterior Mean as a Weighted Average

    $$E[\theta \mid k] = \frac{\alpha + k}{\alpha + \beta + n} = \left(\frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha + \beta + n}\right) \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} + \left(\frac{n}{\alpha + \beta + n}\right) \frac{k}{n}$$

    The Bayesian posterior mean is a transparent convex combination of the prior mean $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$ and the empirical sample mean $\frac{k}{n}$. The relative weight assigned to each component is proportional to effective sample size: $(\alpha + \beta)$ for the prior versus $n$ for the data.

04 Key takeaways

  • Conjugacy occurs when the posterior distribution belongs to the exact same functional probability family as the prior distribution.
  • The Beta distribution is conjugate to the Binomial and Bernoulli likelihoods, reducing Bayesian updating to basic integer addition.
  • The posterior mean is an exact weighted average between prior belief and sample evidence, providing natural shrinkage regularisation.
  • Sequential updating is seamless: today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior as streaming batches of data arrive.

05 In code

python
import scipy.stats as stats

# Prior: Beta(4, 16) -> centered at 20% conversion
alpha_0, beta_0 = 4, 16
prior_mean = alpha_0 / (alpha_0 + beta_0)

# Observed data: 35 conversions out of 100 visitors
n_visitors = 100
k_conversions = 35

# Conjugate update: add successes and failures directly
alpha_1 = alpha_0 + k_conversions
beta_1 = beta_0 + (n_visitors - k_conversions)

post_mean = alpha_1 / (alpha_1 + beta_1)
post_mode = (alpha_1 - 1.0) / (alpha_1 + beta_1 - 2.0)
ci_lower = stats.beta.ppf(0.025, alpha_1, beta_1)
ci_upper = stats.beta.ppf(0.975, alpha_1, beta_1)

print(f"Prior:     Beta({alpha_0}, {beta_0}), Mean: {prior_mean:.1%}")
print(f"Observed:  {k_conversions}/{n_visitors} ({k_conversions/n_visitors:.1%})")
print(f"Posterior: Beta({alpha_1}, {beta_1}), Mean: {post_mean:.1%}, Mode: {post_mode:.1%}")
print(f"95% Credible Interval: [{ci_lower:.3f}, {ci_upper:.3f}]")

Common pitfalls

  • Setting hyperparameter counts $\alpha, \beta$ too large, which rigidly anchors the model and prevents it from learning from moderate sample sizes.
  • Confusing the posterior mode (MAP estimate, $\frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2}$) with the posterior mean ($\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$).
  • Using Beta–Binomial conjugacy on time-varying conversion rates where underlying probabilities drift over time.
  • Assuming all likelihoods possess conjugate priors; complex neural networks and non-linear models lack conjugate priors and require MCMC or variational approximations.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why are conjugate priors computationally advantageous in Bayesian statistics?

Why: Conjugacy ensures the posterior belongs to the same functional family as the prior, reducing Bayesian updates to simple parameter addition without numerical integration.

Q2If you begin with a uniform prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ and observe 7 heads and 3 tails in coin flips, what is the posterior distribution?

Why: Adding observed counts to prior hyperparameters gives $\alpha' = 1 + 7 = 8$ and $\beta' = 1 + 3 = 4$, resulting in $\text{Beta}(8, 4)$.

Q3In the Beta–Binomial model, what happens to the posterior distribution as the sample size $n \to \infty$?

Why: As $n$ grows large, the weight $\frac{n}{\alpha + \beta + n}$ approaches 1.0, causing the posterior to concentrate around the sample mean with vanishing variance.

Q4What intuitive interpretation do the hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ carry in a Beta prior?

Why: The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ act as virtual or pseudo-observations: $\alpha$ reflects prior successes and $\beta$ prior failures, with sum $\alpha + \beta$ representing prior sample strength.
