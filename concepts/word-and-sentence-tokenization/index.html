Word and sentence tokenization

Tokenization partitions continuous streams of natural language characters into discrete linguistic units: sentences and words. While splitting on whitespace serves as a naive first approximation for English, authentic prose is rife with boundary ambiguities: sentence segmenters must distinguish between abbreviation markers ("Dr.", "Inc.", "U.S.") and true sentence-terminal full stops, while word tokenisers must split clitics and contractions ("don't" $\to$ "do", "n't"), isolate punctuation, and preserve hyphenated technical terms. Standard rule-based finite-state tokenisers (such as the Penn Treebank standard) and unsupervised sentence boundary detectors (such as Kiss and Strunk's Punkt algorithm) provide the structured foundation for syntactic parsing and feature extraction.

01 The idea

To a computer, a book or webpage is an unbroken sequence of bytes. Before an algorithm can count frequencies, assign parts of speech, or parse syntactic dependencies, it must decide where one thought ends and the next begins, and where individual words separate. Yet human writing resists simplistic delimiter rules: is "St." a saint or a street? Does "Ph.D." terminate a sentence if it ends a paragraph? Is "rock'n'roll" one word or three? Resolving these edge cases cleanly is the objective of word and sentence tokenisation.

The illusion of whitespace separation: In English and Latin-script languages, spaces between words tempt developers to write simple text.split(' '). However, naive whitespace splitting fails on multiple fronts: it glues punctuation onto words ("apple." instead of "apple" and "."), lumps contracted auxiliary verbs into unrecognisable compounds ("we'll" instead of "we" and "will"), and fractures multi-word idioms. In non-segmented writing systems like Chinese, Japanese, or Thai, words are written continuously without spaces, rendering whitespace splitting completely useless.

Sentence Boundary Disambiguation (SBD): Splitting text into sentences (Sentence Boundary Detection or sentence segmentation) appears trivial—look for periods, question marks, and exclamation points. However, the period character '.' is overloaded: it serves as a sentence terminator, a decimal point in numbers (3.14), an abbreviation marker ("e.g.", "Prof."), an ellipsis (...), an email/URL element, and an acronym separator ("U.S.A."). If a sentence segmenter splits after "Dr.", subsequent syntactic parsers fail because they receive broken grammatical sentence fragments.

The Punkt unsupervised boundary discovery paradigm: Tiberius Kiss and Jan Strunk (2006) proved that sentence boundary disambiguation can be solved with high accuracy without manual rules or labelled training corpora. The Punkt algorithm examines unannotated text using statistical collocations: it identifies abbreviations by calculating whether a word followed by a period occurs frequently without the period, evaluates whether the following word is heavily capitalized, and tests whether the two-word sequence exhibits high mutual information. Punkt achieves over 98% accuracy across multilingual corpora without needing hand-engineered language grammars.

Penn Treebank standard for word tokenisation: The Penn Treebank (PTB) tokenisation convention established the standard for linguistic parsing. Rather than discarding punctuation or treating words as monolithic strings, PTB separates clitics and contractions into distinct syntactic morphemes: "don't" becomes "do" and "n't" (so "n't" can be tagged as an adverbial negation), "they'll" becomes "they" and "'ll", and possessive "'s" separates from its noun ("cat's" $\to$ "cat", "'s"). Standardising contractions into distinct grammatical tokens enables uniform downstream syntactic analysis.

In everyday terms

Think of reading a continuous ticker-tape transcript of spoken dialogue. Without spaces, words run together like "wevegottogotothestation". Punctuation marks are like hand gestures made by the speaker: a period is sometimes a firm drop of the hand indicating a finished thought (end of sentence), but other times it is merely an abbreviated flick indicating a job title ("Dr. Smith"). A naive tokeniser is like a paper cutter that blindly slices the ticker tape every time it spots a dot: it accidentally cuts Dr. Smith in half, separating the doctor from their surname and ruining the medical file. A linguistically aware tokeniser inspects the surrounding context: it recognizes that "Dr." is an abbreviation prefix, preserves the integrity of the name, and only drops the slicing blade when a true full stop signals a fresh thought.

03 The maths

  1. Log-likelihood ratio for abbreviation detection (Punkt)

    \Lambda = 2 \left( \log L(H_1) - \log L(H_0) \right) = 2 \sum_{i} k_i \log \frac{p_i}{p}

    The Punkt algorithm evaluates whether a word with a trailing period (such as "Corp.") is an abbreviation ($H_1$) or an end-of-sentence word ($H_0$) by computing the log-likelihood ratio test of independence. Words with large positive $\Lambda$ scores are added to the abbreviation lexicon.

  2. Token-to-Type Ratio (TTR)

    \text{TTR} = \frac{|\mathcal{V}|}{N_{\text{tokens}}} = \frac{\text{Unique Types}}{\text{Total Running Tokens}}

    Here $|\mathcal{V}|$ is the vocabulary size (number of unique types) and $N_{\text{tokens}}$ is total running token count. TTR quantifies lexical diversity in a corpus. Over-tokenising words increases $|\mathcal{V}|$ artificially, while under-tokenising leads to higher out-of-vocabulary rates.

  3. Heaps' Law of vocabulary growth

    |\mathcal{V}| = k \cdot N_{\text{tokens}}^\beta, \quad 0 < \beta < 1

    Heaps' Law models how vocabulary size $|\mathcal{V}|$ grows asymptotically with corpus size $N_{\text{tokens}}$. Parameters $k \approx 10$ to $100$ and $\beta \approx 0.4$ to $0.6$ depend directly on how aggressively contractions, hyphens, and compound words are split during tokenisation.

04 Key takeaways

  • Tokenisation converts raw continuous text into discrete sentence and word elements.
  • Naive whitespace splitting fails on punctuation, clitics, contractions, and unsegmented languages like Chinese.
  • Sentence Boundary Detection must disambiguate periods used for abbreviations, decimals, and true sentence terminations.
  • The Penn Treebank standard separates contractions ("don't" $\to$ "do", "n't") into distinct syntactic units.
  • Unsupervised algorithms like Punkt discover language-specific abbreviations without manual grammar engineering.

05 In code

python
import re

# Rule-based word and sentence tokenisation pipeline (Penn Treebank standard)
sample_text = "Dr. Smith arrived at 3.15 p.m. He said, 'We couldn't visit the U.S. capital!' Did you see him?"

# 1. Rule-based Sentence Boundary Detection
# Known honorific / title abbreviations to protect
abbreviations = ["Dr", "Mr", "Mrs", "Ms", "Prof", "Inc", "Ltd", "Jr", "Sr", "U.S", "p.m", "a.m"]

def sentence_tokenize(text):
    # Protect abbreviation periods by replacing them with temporary placeholder
    protected_text = text
    for abbr in abbreviations:
        # Match whole word abbreviation followed by dot
        pattern = r'\b' + re.escape(abbr) + r'\.'
        protected_text = re.sub(pattern, abbr + "@DOT@", protected_text)
    
    # Split on sentence terminals (. ! ?) followed by whitespace or end of string
    raw_sentences = re.split(r'(?<=[.!?])\s+', protected_text)
    
    # Restore protected periods
    sentences = [s.replace("@DOT@", ".") for s in raw_sentences if s.strip()]
    return sentences

sentences = sentence_tokenize(sample_text)
print(f"Detected {len(sentences)} sentences:")
for i, s in enumerate(sentences):
    print(f"  Sentence {i+1}: \"{s}\"")

# 2. Penn Treebank style Word Tokenisation
def word_tokenize_ptb(sentence):
    text = sentence
    
    # Isolate leading and trailing quotes, brackets, and punctuation
    text = re.sub(r'([,;:\(\)\[\]\{\}\?!])', r' \1 ', text)
    
    # Isolate periods that are true terminals (not in decimals or abbreviations)
    text = re.sub(r'(?<!\d)\.(?!\d)', r' . ', text)
    
    # Handle negative contractions: split "n't"
    text = re.sub(r"(?i)\b([a-z]+)n't\b", r"\1 n't", text)
    # Special cases: "can't" -> "ca n't", "won't" -> "wo n't"
    text = re.sub(r"(?i)\bca n't\b", "ca n't", text)
    text = re.sub(r"(?i)\bwo n't\b", "wo n't", text)
    
    # Handle other clitics: 'll, 're, 've, 'm, 'd, 's
    text = re.sub(r"'(ll|re|ve|m|d|s)\b", r" '\1", text)
    
    # Split on whitespace
    tokens = [tok for tok in text.split() if tok]
    return tokens

print(f"\nWord tokens for Sentence 2 (\"We couldn't visit the U.S. capital!\"):")
tokens_s2 = word_tokenize_ptb(sentences[1])
print(tokens_s2)
print(f"Notice 'couldn\\'t' split into: ['could', \"n't\"]")
print(f"Notice punctuation isolated:     ['!']")

Common pitfalls

  • Discarding all punctuation during word tokenisation: Stripping punctuation destroys sentence boundaries, removes numerical decimals, and strips grammatical markers; punctuation should be isolated as distinct tokens rather than deleted.
  • Assuming periods always indicate sentence boundaries: Splitting on every period creates fragmented sentences around abbreviations ("Dr. Jane Smith moved to the U.S. in 2020.").
  • Treating contractions as monolithic single words: Treating "don't" as an indivisible token prevents models from recognizing the negation operator "n't" and the auxiliary verb "do" independently.
  • Language-specific tokeniser assumptions: Applying an English whitespace tokeniser to unsegmented scripts (such as Chinese or Japanese) outputs the entire paragraph as a single giant token; specialized segmenters (like Jieba or MeCab) are required.
  • Inconsistent hyphen handling: Inconsistently handling hyphens ("state-of-the-art" vs "state of the art") splits single conceptual adjectives into fragments, causing vocabulary fragmentation unless normalised.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does naive whitespace splitting (text.split(' ')) fail when preparing real-world English text for NLP pipelines?

Why: Whitespace splitting glues punctuation onto tokens, creating duplicate vocabulary entries ("dog", "dog,", "dog."). It also fails to decompose grammatical contractions like "can't" into root and negation components.

Q2What makes Sentence Boundary Detection (SBD) challenging in natural language text?

Why: Because the full stop is heavily overloaded as an abbreviation marker, decimal separator, and acronym element, simple rule-based splitting after every period causes severe fragmentation of proper names and numerical quantities.

Q3According to the Penn Treebank standard, how should the contraction "couldn't" be tokenised?

Why: The Penn Treebank standard separates clitics and contractions into distinct tokens: "could" (modal auxiliary verb) and "n't" (adverbial negation), allowing downstream taggers to assign distinct parts of speech to each.

Q4How does the Punkt algorithm detect abbreviations in an unannotated text corpus without human-engineered grammar rules?

Why: Punkt evaluates whether a word followed by a period occurs with high probability inside sentences, whether subsequent words are capitalized, and whether the pair forms an abbreviation collocation, identifying abbreviations in an unsupervised manner.
