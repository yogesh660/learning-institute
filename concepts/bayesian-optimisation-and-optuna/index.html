01 The idea

Random search and grid search treat every trial independently, discarding valuable information about the objective landscape uncovered during previous runs. Bayesian optimisation models the objective function as an expensive black-box probabilistic distribution, balancing exploration of uncertain regions with exploitation of known high-performing areas using acquisition functions like Expected Improvement. Modern frameworks like Optuna replace Gaussian processes with Tree-structured Parzen Estimators (TPE) and automated pruning, enabling scalable tuning across complex mixed-type search spaces.

Imagine prospecting for oil across a vast desert by drilling exploratory test wells. Drilling a single well costs £100,000 and takes two weeks. You would never choose your drilling sites using a rigid grid or random lottery. After drilling your first few sites, you analyse the geological data to form a mental map of the subsurface: where oil is likely concentrated (high expected yield) and where the terrain remains completely unknown (high uncertainty).

If you only drill right next to your best existing well, you might squeeze out minor gains while missing a massive oil basin fifty miles away (over-exploitation). If you only drill in completely random unexplored corners, you waste millions confirming dry dust (over-exploration). The rational strategy balances both: drill where the probability of finding a larger deposit than your current best is highest.

Bayesian optimisation formalises this balance using two components: a probabilistic surrogate model (such as a Gaussian process) that predicts both the expected performance and its uncertainty at every unvisited coordinate, and an acquisition function (such as Expected Improvement) that scores each point's potential. Optuna brings this into modern production through the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE). Instead of modelling $p(y \mid \theta)$ via expensive matrix inversions, TPE models the parameter distributions of good trials $p(\theta \mid y < y^*)$ and bad trials $p(\theta \mid y \ge y^*)$ separately using kernel density estimates, selecting candidate points that maximise their ratio while aggressively pruning stalled trials mid-training.

In everyday terms

A grandmaster chess player evaluating candidate moves: calculating deep variants only for moves with high tactical promise while occasionally exploring unconventional lines that offer surprising upside.

02 Step by step

  1. Initialise the search space: evaluate a small budget of seed points (typically 5 to 10 trials) using quasi-random Sobol or Latin hypercube sampling.
  2. Fit the surrogate model: train a Gaussian Process or Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) on all evaluated configurations and their observed validation losses.
  3. Formulate the acquisition function: calculate Expected Improvement (EI) or Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) across candidate hyperparameter configurations.
  4. Optimise the acquisition function: identify the hyperparameter coordinate $\theta^*$ that maximises the acquisition score.
  5. Evaluate the objective: train the machine learning model using $\theta^*$ on validation data to record its true objective value $f(\theta^*)$.
  6. Enable automated pruning: abort trials early (via Optuna's MedianPruner or Hyperband) if intermediate training curves fall below median historical progress.
  7. Update the surrogate dataset and iterate until the computational trial budget is exhausted.

03 The maths

  1. Gaussian Process surrogate posterior

    $$f(\theta) \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(\mu(\theta), \, k(\theta, \theta')\big), \qquad \mu_n(\theta) = k_*^T (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} y, \quad \sigma_n^2(\theta) = k(\theta, \theta) - k_*^T (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} k_*$$

    where $K$ is the kernel Gram matrix evaluated at observed trials and $k_*$ is the covariance vector between candidate $\theta$ and past observations. The posterior mean $\mu_n(\theta)$ models exploitation, while the posterior variance $\sigma_n^2(\theta)$ quantifies epistemic uncertainty for exploration.

  2. Expected Improvement (EI) acquisition function

    $$\operatorname{EI}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[\max(0, y_{\text{best}} - f(\theta))\big] = (y_{\text{best}} - \mu_n(\theta)) \, \Phi(z) + \sigma_n(\theta) \, \phi(z), \quad z = \frac{y_{\text{best}} - \mu_n(\theta)}{\sigma_n(\theta)}$$

    where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $\phi$ is the standard normal density function. The first term prioritises exploitation (large improvement over current best $y_{\text{best}}$), while the second term rewards exploration (high uncertainty $\sigma_n(\theta)$).

  3. Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)

    $$\frac{p(\theta \mid y < y^*)}{p(\theta \mid y \ge y^*)} = \frac{\ell(\theta)}{g(\theta)} \propto \operatorname{EI}(\theta)$$

    By setting a quantile threshold $\gamma$ (typically 10% or 15%), TPE partitions historical trials into good performers $\ell(\theta)$ and bad performers $g(\theta)$. Maximising the likelihood ratio $\ell(\theta) / g(\theta)$ samples points that are highly probable under the good distribution and improbable under the bad distribution, eliminating $O(n^3)$ GP matrix inversion.

04 Key takeaways

  • Replaces uninformed sampling with sequential probabilistic modelling of the hyperparameter response surface.
  • Expected Improvement balances the trade-off between exploiting promising regions and exploring uncertain areas.
  • Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) replaces Gaussian processes with non-parametric kernel density ratios, scaling to high trial counts and categorical spaces.
  • Automated pruning terminates unpromising trials midway through training epochs, saving up to 80% of total compute.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

# True black-box function with local minimum
def f(x):
    return np.sin(3 * x) + 0.5 * (x - 1) ** 2

# Initial observations
X_obs = np.array([0.2, 1.8, 2.8])
y_obs = f(X_obs)

# RBF kernel for Gaussian Process surrogate
def rbf_cov(x1, x2, l=0.5, s=1.0):
    return s**2 * np.exp(-0.5 * (x1[:, None] - x2[None, :])**2 / l**2)

# Run 4 Bayesian Optimisation steps via Expected Improvement (EI)
cands = np.linspace(0, 3, 100)
for step in range(4):
    K = rbf_cov(X_obs, X_obs) + 1e-6 * np.eye(len(X_obs))
    K_s = rbf_cov(cands, X_obs)
    mu = K_s @ np.linalg.solve(K, y_obs)
    sigma = np.sqrt(np.maximum(1e-6, 1.0 - np.sum(K_s * np.linalg.solve(K, K_s.T).T, axis=1)))
    
    # Expected Improvement (minimisation: improvement over current best y_best)
    y_best = np.min(y_obs)
    z = (y_best - mu) / sigma
    ei = (y_best - mu) * norm.cdf(z) + sigma * norm.pdf(z)
    
    next_x = cands[np.argmax(ei)]
    X_obs = np.append(X_obs, next_x)
    y_obs = np.append(y_obs, f(next_x))

best_idx = np.argmin(y_obs)
print(f"Evaluated points: {X_obs.round(3)}")
print(f"Best observed x*: {X_obs[best_idx]:.3f} | Minimum value: {y_obs[best_idx]:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Cubic scaling of Gaussian processes: Inverting the GP covariance matrix scales as $O(n^3)$ with the number of trials $n$. Standard GP Bayesian optimisation slows to a crawl beyond 150 to 200 trials (which is why Optuna uses TPE or Random Forests).
  • Inappropriate kernel length scales: If the GP kernel length-scale is too short, the surrogate assumes the objective is wildly jagged, treating nearby points as completely independent and collapsing into random search.
  • Cold-start vulnerability: If initial seed points all happen to fall in uninformative regions, the acquisition function can get trapped reinforcing local minima before exploring broader basins.
  • Ignoring trial execution overhead: In fast-training models (e.g. linear models or small decision trees), fitting a complex surrogate model can take longer than simply running 500 parallel random trials.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary role of the acquisition function in Bayesian optimisation?

Why: The acquisition function (such as Expected Improvement or Upper Confidence Bound) evaluates the surrogate model's posterior mean and uncertainty across unvisited points, guiding the search to coordinates with the highest potential return.

Q2How does Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) differ fundamentally from a standard Gaussian Process surrogate?

Why: Instead of modelling $p(y \mid \theta)$ with a computationally heavy Gaussian Process ($O(n^3)$ complexity), TPE splits historical evaluations into top performers $\ell(\theta)$ and remaining performers $g(\theta)$ using a quantile cutoff $\gamma$, maximising $\ell(\theta)/g(\theta) \propto \operatorname{EI}(\theta)$.

Q3In the Expected Improvement formula $\operatorname{EI}(\theta) = (y_{\text{best}} - \mu) \Phi(z) + \sigma \phi(z)$, what behaviour does the second term $\sigma \phi(z)$ represent?

Why: The term $\sigma \phi(z)$ is proportional to the posterior standard deviation $\sigma$. When evaluating an unexplored region of the search space, $\sigma$ is large, driving up Expected Improvement even if the predicted mean $\mu$ is unremarkable.

Q4What is the purpose of Optuna's automated "pruning" mechanism (such as MedianPruner)?

Why: Pruning checks intermediate validation scores (e.g. after each epoch) against past completed trials. If a trial performs worse than the historical median at that epoch, Optuna halts the trial immediately, saving compute.
