01 The idea

High-dimensional feature spaces suffer from multicollinearity, redundant measurements, and computational overhead. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identifies an orthogonal sequence of linear projections that sequentially maximise variance, transforming correlated features into mutually uncorrelated principal components and compressing data with minimal reconstruction error.

Imagine holding a complex three-dimensional object—such as a teapot or a sculpture—in front of a blank wall under a single spotlight. If you align the light from a random angle, the cast shadow collapses into an unrecognisable blob where handles and spouts overlap. But as you rotate the object to its broadest, most characteristic orientation, the 2D shadow captures virtually all the defining contours of the 3D shape. PCA is the mathematical equivalent of finding that optimal orientation: it rotates high-dimensional coordinate axes until the projected shadow of the data exhibits the widest possible spread.

The fundamental premise of PCA is that directions exhibiting large variance convey the most informative differences between data points, whereas directions with negligible variance correspond to redundant noise or flat dimensions. When two sensors measure closely coupled phenomena (such as indoor temperature in Celsius and Fahrenheit), the data forms an elongated ellipse in 2D space. Instead of keeping both redundant features, PCA aligns the first principal axis along the major axis of the ellipse (capturing the shared thermal trend) and the second principal axis along the minor axis (capturing minor sensor noise).

Crucially, each consecutive principal component is constrained to be strictly orthogonal (perpendicular) to all preceding components. This guarantees that the newly created coordinates are completely decorrelated—their sample covariance is exactly zero. By selecting the top $k$ components that account for 90% or 95% of total variance, analysts dramatically compress datasets, eliminate multicollinearity for downstream linear models, and enable two- and three-dimensional exploratory scatter plots without discarding the primary patterns of variability.

In everyday terms

Rotating a 3D architectural model under a camera until you capture the single elevation angle that reveals the maximum building perimeter and room layout in a single 2D photograph.

02 Step by step

  1. Centre the dataset by subtracting feature means: $X_c = X - \mathbf{1}\mu^T$, ensuring the origin lies at the geometric centroid. Optionally standardise features to unit variance if variables have unequal measurement scales.
  2. Compute the sample covariance matrix $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X_c^T X_c$, or apply Singular Value Decomposition directly to the centred matrix $X_c = U S V^T$ to avoid numerical squaring of condition numbers.
  3. Extract the right singular vectors $V$ (or eigenvectors of $\Sigma$) and their corresponding singular values $S$ (where eigenvalues $\lambda_j = S_j^2 / (n - 1)$).
  4. Sort eigenvalues and singular vectors in descending order of explained variance $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$.
  5. Select the top $k$ principal axes $V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ based on cumulative explained variance ratio criteria (e.g. 95% threshold or scree plot elbow).
  6. Project the centred data onto the selected subspace to obtain lower-dimensional coordinates $Z = X_c V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$.
  7. Quantify information retention via reconstruction error $\|X_c - Z V_k^T\|_F^2$, which equals the sum of discarded eigenvalues $\sum_{j=k+1}^d \lambda_j$.

03 The maths

  1. Covariance matrix and principal axis objective

    $$\max_{w} \operatorname{Var}(X_c w) = \max_{w} \frac{1}{n-1} w^T X_c^T X_c w \quad \text{subject to} \quad w^T w = 1$$

    Formulating the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^T \Sigma w - \lambda(w^T w - 1)$ and differentiating yields $\Sigma w = \lambda w$. The principal direction $w$ is the eigenvector of covariance matrix $\Sigma$ corresponding to the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = \operatorname{Var}(X_c w_1)$. Subsequent orthogonal components correspond to eigenvectors ordered by descending eigenvalues.

  2. SVD connection and computational efficiency

    $$X_c = U S V^T, \qquad \Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X_c^T X_c = \frac{1}{n-1} V S^2 V^T$$

    $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are orthogonal matrices containing left and right singular vectors, and $S$ contains singular values $s_j$. Computing SVD directly on $X_c$ requires $O(n d \min(n, d))$ operations and preserves double-precision floating-point accuracy without forming the condition-squared product $X_c^T X_c$.

  3. Explained variance ratio and optimal reconstruction

    $$\operatorname{EVR}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j} = \frac{s_k^2}{\sum_{j=1}^d s_j^2}, \qquad \min_{\operatorname{rank}(\hat{X})=k} \|X_c - \hat{X}\|_F^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^d \lambda_j$$

    By the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem, the truncated projection $\hat{X} = Z V_k^T = X_c V_k V_k^T$ provides the optimal rank-$k$ approximation of centred matrix $X_c$ under both Frobenius and spectral matrix norms.

04 Key takeaways

  • Maximises projected variance while simultaneously minimising mean squared orthogonal reconstruction error.
  • Transforms correlated features into mutually orthogonal, linearly uncorrelated components.
  • Direct SVD of centred data $X_c = U S V^T$ is numerically superior to explicit eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$.
  • Requires standardisation whenever input features carry differing units or non-comparable variance scales.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Generate synthetic 3D data with correlated features
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_samples = 150
z_true = rng.normal(0, 2, size=(n_samples, 1))
noise = rng.normal(0, 0.3, size=(n_samples, 3))
X = np.hstack([z_true * 2.0, z_true * -1.5, z_true * 0.5]) + noise

# 1. Centre the data (zero mean)
mu = np.mean(X, axis=0)
X_c = X - mu

# 2. Compute SVD: X_c = U * S * Vt
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X_c, full_matrices=False)
eigenvalues = (S ** 2) / (n_samples - 1)
evr = eigenvalues / np.sum(eigenvalues)

# 3. Project onto top k=2 principal components
k = 2
V_k = Vt[:k, :].T
Z = X_c @ V_k

# 4. Reconstruct and calculate reconstruction error
X_reconstructed = Z @ V_k.T + mu
rec_error = np.mean(np.sum((X - X_reconstructed) ** 2, axis=1))

print(f"Explained variance ratio: {evr[:k].round(4)}")
print(f"Total variance explained: {np.sum(evr[:k]) * 100:.2f}%")
print(f"Mean squared reconstruction error: {rec_error:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Scale sensitivity: Features measured in large units (e.g. annual salary in pounds) dominate total variance over smaller features (e.g. age in years). Without standardisation, PCA simply projects onto the largest arbitrary units rather than meaningful patterns.
  • Linearity restriction: PCA can only discover linear planar subspaces. Data arranged along non-linear manifolds (such as a Swiss roll, concentric rings, or helical curves) gets squashed and entangled.
  • Assuming high variance equals relevance: PCA is unsupervised and blind to target labels. The direction of maximum variance might represent uninformative measurement noise or illumination differences rather than the target classification signal.
  • Orthogonality constraint: Enforcing mutual orthogonality can force later principal components to blend distinct physical processes together, making individual components difficult to interpret as pure physical entities.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why must features usually be standardised before applying PCA?

Why: Variance depends on the choice of units. If one feature is measured in millimetres and another in metres, the millimetre feature will display a million-fold greater variance and dominate the top principal component regardless of information content.

Q2If the eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrix for a 4D dataset are $\lambda = [6.0, 2.5, 1.0, 0.5]$, what percentage of total variance is explained by the first two principal components?

Why: Total variance is the sum of all eigenvalues: $6.0 + 2.5 + 1.0 + 0.5 = 10.0$. The sum of the top two is $6.0 + 2.5 = 8.5$. The explained variance ratio is $8.5 / 10.0 = 0.85$ (or 85%).

Q3How does Singular Value Decomposition ($X_c = U S V^T$) relate to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^T X_c$?

Why: Substituting $X_c = U S V^T$ into $\Sigma$ gives $\frac{1}{n-1} V S U^T U S V^T = \frac{1}{n-1} V S^2 V^T$ because $U^T U = I$. This proves $V$ are the orthonormal eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_j = s_j^2 / (n-1)$.

Q4What does the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem guarantee about the PCA rank-$k$ reconstruction $\hat{X} = X_c V_k V_k^T$?

Why: The Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem establishes that the truncated SVD reconstruction minimises the Frobenius distance $\|X_c - \hat{X}\|_F$ among all matrices of rank at most $k$.
