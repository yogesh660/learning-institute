Text classification and sentiment

Text classification maps variable-length natural language documents into discrete categorical classes, serving as the backbone for sentiment analysis, spam detection, topic tagging, and intent routing. Modern text classification encompasses a spectrum of techniques: from fast linear baselines (TF-IDF with Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, or Support Vector Machines) to fine-tuned contextual Transformers using pooled [CLS] representations. Handling linguistic subtleties such as negation, sarcasm, class imbalance, and domain shift is essential for reliable production systems.

01 The idea

Is the customer review "The hotel room was not bad, but I definitely wouldn't call it luxurious" positive, negative, or neutral? To a naive keyword spotter, the word "bad" flags negative, while "luxurious" flags positive. Effective text classification requires looking past isolated surface words to model compositionality, scope of negation, and subjective stance across whole sentences.

The spectrum of text classification: Text classification problems vary widely in cardinality and structure: - Binary Classification: Spam vs Ham, Positive vs Negative sentiment. - Multi-Class Classification: Routing customer support tickets to one of 20 departments (mutually exclusive). - Multi-Label Classification: Tagging an academic paper with multiple research topics (e.g. both 'Computer Science' and 'Quantitative Biology'). - Fine-Grained Sentiment & Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA): Extracting sentiment targets and associated polarities independently (e.g. food: positive, price: negative).

Linear baselines versus deep contextual encoders: A simple TF-IDF representation combined with a linear classifier (Logistic Regression or SVM) provides an extraordinarily competitive baseline: it trains in seconds, evaluates in microseconds, and requires minimal compute. However, bag-of-words linear models fail when word order alters meaning ("not good" vs "good"). Pre-trained Transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa) capture subtle syntactic negation and long-range dependencies, delivering top accuracy when computational budget permits.

The challenge of linguistic negation and sarcasm: Negation words ("not", "never", "hardly", "no") invert the emotional polarity of subsequent terms. In classical pipelines, bigrams or negation tagging (appending a _NEG suffix to words between a negative particle and subsequent punctuation) are required to capture polarity shifts. In ironic or sarcastic prose, literal sentiment words contradict intended meaning, posing severe challenges even for advanced neural architectures.

In everyday terms

Think of sorting mail in a bustling corporate post room. A simple linear classifier is like an automated sorting arm that glances at key return addresses or stamped labels ("INVOICE", "URGENT", "LEGAL") and tosses envelopes into corresponding bins at lightning speed. It works brilliantly for 90% of routine mail. A deep Transformer classifier is like an experienced executive assistant who reads the actual letter, detects subtle diplomatic tone, notices that a polite compliment was delivered with veiled frustration, and routes the document to the Chief Executive with an urgent warning flag.

03 The maths

  1. Multinomial Logistic Regression classification probability

    P(y = c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{w}_c^T \mathbf{x} + b_c)}{\sum_{j=1}^C \exp(\mathbf{w}_j^T \mathbf{x} + b_j)}

    Here $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the document feature vector (TF-IDF or pooled Transformer embedding), $\mathbf{w}_c \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the learned weight vector for category $c$, and $C$ is the total number of classes.

  2. Cross-entropy loss with inverse class weighting

    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{c=1}^C \alpha_c \, y_{i, c} \log \hat{y}_{i, c}, \quad \alpha_c = \frac{N}{C \cdot N_c}

    Here $N_c$ is the number of training samples in class $c$. Weighting factor $\alpha_c$ scales the penalty for errors on rare classes, counteracting severe majority class bias.

  3. Macro-averaged versus Micro-averaged F1 metrics

    \operatorname{Macro-}F_1 = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^C F_{1, c}, \quad \operatorname{Micro-}F_1 = \frac{2 \sum_{c} \operatorname{TP}_c}{2 \sum_c \operatorname{TP}_c + \sum_c \operatorname{FP}_c + \sum_c \operatorname{FN}_c}

    Macro-F1 assigns equal weight to every category regardless of sample frequency, making it the essential metric for imbalanced text classification. Micro-F1 pools global counts, mirroring overall accuracy.

04 Key takeaways

  • Text classification maps variable-length natural language text into discrete categorical labels.
  • TF-IDF paired with linear classifiers (Logistic Regression, SVM) provides a formidable, fast baseline for production systems.
  • Fine-tuned Transformer models (BERT, RoBERTa) capture syntactic negation, idioms, and long-range semantic dependencies.
  • Negation words alter the polarity of subsequent phrases; capturing negation requires bigrams, suffix tagging, or bidirectional attention.
  • Always evaluate imbalanced text classification using Macro-averaged F1 and confusion matrices rather than overall accuracy.

05 In code

python
import math
import re
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

class SimpleTextClassifier:
    """
    Multinomial Naive Bayes text classifier from scratch
    with Laplace smoothing and n-gram feature extraction.
    """
    def __init__(self, alpha=1.0):
        self.alpha = alpha
        self.classes = set()
        self.class_counts = Counter()
        self.word_counts = defaultdict(Counter)
        self.total_words_in_class = Counter()
        self.vocab = set()
        
    def _tokenize(self, text):
        # Extract unigrams and bigrams
        words = [w.lower() for w in re.findall(r'\b[a-zA-Z]+\b', text)]
        bigrams = [f"{words[i]}_{words[i+1]}" for i in range(len(words)-1)]
        return words + bigrams

    def fit(self, texts, labels):
        for text, label in zip(texts, labels):
            self.classes.add(label)
            self.class_counts[label] += 1
            tokens = self._tokenize(text)
            
            for token in tokens:
                self.vocab.add(token)
                self.word_counts[label][token] += 1
                self.total_words_in_class[label] += 1

    def predict(self, text):
        tokens = self._tokenize(text)
        total_docs = sum(self.class_counts.values())
        V = len(self.vocab)
        
        best_class = None
        best_log_prob = -float('inf')
        
        for c in self.classes:
            # Prior log P(c)
            log_prob = math.log(self.class_counts[c] / total_docs)
            
            # Likelihood sum log P(w|c) with Laplace smoothing
            denom = self.total_words_in_class[c] + self.alpha * V
            for token in tokens:
                if token in self.vocab:
                    count = self.word_counts[c][token]
                    prob_w = (count + self.alpha) / denom
                    log_prob += math.log(prob_w)
                    
            if log_prob > best_log_prob:
                best_log_prob = log_prob
                best_class = c
                
        return best_class

# Sample sentiment training corpus
train_reviews = [
    ("This movie was absolutely wonderful and charming", "positive"),
    ("Great acting, brilliant script, and delightful humor", "positive"),
    ("An inspiring masterpiece with unforgettable performances", "positive"),
    ("Terrible waste of time, boring and poorly directed", "negative"),
    ("Awful dialogue, horrible acting, completely predictable", "negative"),
    ("Dull, uninspired, and thoroughly disappointing experience", "negative")
]

texts, labels = zip(*train_reviews)
clf = SimpleTextClassifier(alpha=1.0)
clf.fit(texts, labels)

test_sentences = [
    "A wonderful performance with delightful acting",
    "Completely terrible and horribly boring",
    "Great script but poorly directed"
]

print("Text Classification & Sentiment Predictions:")
for test in test_sentences:
    pred = clf.predict(test)
    print(f"  Review: '{test}' -> Sentiment: {pred.upper()}")

Common pitfalls

  • Evaluating on overall accuracy under severe class imbalance: If 95% of customer emails are spam, a dummy model predicting "spam" achieves 95% accuracy while completely failing on authentic emails. Always report Macro-F1 and per-class recall.
  • Data leakage via global TF-IDF fitting: Fitting TF-IDF vectorisers on the combined train and test set leaks test vocabulary statistics into model features. Always fit vectorisers exclusively on training data.
  • Ignoring domain shift in sentiment models: A sentiment model trained on IMDb film reviews fails on financial news (e.g. "shares plummeted" vs "a terrifying thriller"). Always validate models on representative in-domain text.
  • Stripping negation stop words: Generic stop word removal lists frequently discard words like "not", "no", "never", completely reversing the sentiment polarity of sentences. Always preserve negation particles.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is Macro-averaged F1 preferred over accuracy or Micro-averaged F1 for evaluating imbalanced text classification?

Why: When one category contains 90% of samples, overall accuracy and Micro-F1 mirror performance on the dominant class. Macro-F1 computes unweighted arithmetic mean across all categories, penalising models that ignore minority classes.

Q2Why does naive stop word removal often degrade sentiment analysis model accuracy?

Why: Stripping "not" turns "not bad" into "bad" and "not good" into "good", completely flipping sentiment polarity. Text classification pipelines for sentiment must preserve negation words or use bigrams.

Q3What advantage does fine-tuning a pre-trained Transformer (like BERT) offer over a TF-IDF + Logistic Regression baseline for complex sentiment classification?

Why: TF-IDF treats text as an unordered bag of tokens, making it difficult to distinguish "not bad at all" from "bad not at all". Bidirectional self-attention encodes full syntactic compositionality and negation scope across the entire sentence.
