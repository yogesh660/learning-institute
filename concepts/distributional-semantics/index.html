Distributional semantics

Distributional semantics is the theoretical and computational foundation of modern lexical representation. Rooted in J.R. Firth's famous 1957 maxim—"You shall know a word by the company it keeps"—the distributional hypothesis posits that words occurring in similar linguistic contexts possess similar semantic meanings. By counting word co-occurrences in sliding symmetric windows, applying statistical association metrics like Positive Pointwise Mutual Information (PPMI), and evaluating geometric distance via cosine similarity, distributional models transform discrete linguistic tokens into continuous vector spaces.

01 The idea

If you encounter an invented English word like "roozle" in the sentences "She poured a chilled glass of fresh roozle", "He sliced the ripe roozle into quarters", and "The tart citrus scent of roozle filled the kitchen", you immediately deduce what it is: a citrus fruit. You did not look it up in a dictionary; you inferred its semantic meaning purely from the surrounding context words ("glass", "poured", "sliced", "ripe", "citrus"). Distributional semantics formalises this human intuition into computational linear algebra.

The Distributional Hypothesis and contextual substitutability: Formalised by Zellig Harris and J.R. Firth in the 1950s, the distributional hypothesis states that the degree of semantic similarity between two words is an empirical function of the similarity of their contextual distributions. Words with high contextual overlap (such as "doctor" and "physician", or "cat" and "kitten") can frequently be substituted into the same sentence frames without violating grammatical or semantic validity.

From raw co-occurrence counts to statistical information: Counting raw co-occurrences in a text corpus introduces massive frequency distortion. High-frequency function words like "the", "of", and "is" appear adjacent to almost every word, dominating raw counts without conveying specific semantic information. Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) resolves this by measuring the ratio between the observed joint probability of two words and their independent probability under random chance.

Geometric spaces and cosine similarity: When words are projected as vectors in a high-dimensional vocabulary space, semantic relatedness corresponds to spatial proximity. Rather than measuring Euclidean distance—which is heavily biased by absolute word frequency—distributional models measure the cosine of the angle between vectors. Normalising by Euclidean length ensures that frequent words and rare synonyms align in identical directional rays.

In everyday terms

Think of profiling people based on their social circles. If you want to know who someone is, you do not need to interrogate their inner psyche; you simply observe who they spend time with. If Alice spends her weekends rock-climbing with mountaineers, talking about carabiners and ropes, and Bob spends his weekends at the exact same climbing gym with the exact same people discussing harnesses and chalk, you can confidently conclude that Alice and Bob share a passion for rock climbing, even if Alice and Bob have never personally met. Distributional semantics profiles words by their textual companions: words that hang out with the same linguistic friends share the same underlying meaning.

03 The maths

  1. Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)

    \operatorname{PMI}(w, c) = \log_2 \frac{P(w, c)}{P(w) P(c)} = \log_2 \frac{C(w, c) \cdot N}{\sum_{c'} C(w, c') \cdot \sum_{w'} C(w', c)}

    Here $C(w, c)$ is the co-occurrence count of target $w$ and context $c$, and $N = \sum_{w, c} C(w, c)$ is the total sum of all co-occurrences in the corpus. If $w$ and $c$ are completely independent, $P(w, c) = P(w)P(c)$ and $\operatorname{PMI} = 0$.

  2. Positive Pointwise Mutual Information (PPMI)

    \operatorname{PPMI}(w, c) = \max \left( 0, \, \operatorname{PMI}(w, c) \right)

    Because negative PMI values are dominated by statistical noise on rare words with insufficient observation counts, PPMI clamps all negative values to zero, producing a non-negative sparse representation.

  3. Directional cosine similarity in vector space

    \cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^d u_i v_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d u_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d v_i^2}}

    Cosine similarity measures the angular alignment between two word vectors on the unit hypersphere, decoupling semantic orientation from raw term frequency magnitude.

04 Key takeaways

  • The distributional hypothesis asserts that words occurring in similar textual contexts possess similar semantic meanings.
  • Raw co-occurrence counts are heavily distorted by ubiquitous function words; Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) normalises against random expectation.
  • Positive PMI (PPMI) clips uninformative negative associations to zero, yielding robust non-negative semantic vectors.
  • Cosine similarity measures semantic proximity as angular alignment, preventing document length or word frequency from skewing comparisons.
  • While PPMI matrices capture rich lexical meaning, their extreme sparsity and high dimensionality (|V| x |V|) motivate dense dimensionality reduction techniques.

05 In code

python
import math
import re
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

class DistributionalSemantics:
    """
    Co-occurrence matrix builder and PPMI calculator
    for distributional word vectors and cosine similarity.
    """
    def __init__(self, window_size=2):
        self.window_size = window_size
        self.vocab = set()
        self.cooccur = defaultdict(Counter)
        self.word_counts = Counter()
        self.total_pairs = 0
        self.ppmi_vectors = defaultdict(dict)

    def _tokenize(self, text):
        return [w.lower() for w in re.findall(r'\b[a-zA-Z]+\b', text)]

    def fit(self, corpus):
        # Count co-occurrences in sliding symmetric window
        for sentence in corpus:
            tokens = self._tokenize(sentence)
            for i, target in enumerate(tokens):
                self.vocab.add(target)
                self.word_counts[target] += 1
                
                start = max(0, i - self.window_size)
                end = min(len(tokens), i + self.window_size + 1)
                
                for j in range(start, end):
                    if i != j:
                        ctx = tokens[j]
                        self.cooccur[target][ctx] += 1
                        self.total_pairs += 1
                        
        self._compute_ppmi()

    def _compute_ppmi(self):
        # Marginal context sums
        context_totals = Counter()
        for target, contexts in self.cooccur.items():
            for ctx, count in contexts.items():
                context_totals[ctx] += count

        N = self.total_pairs
        for target, contexts in self.cooccur.items():
            target_total = sum(contexts.values())
            for ctx, count in contexts.items():
                # Joint and independent probabilities
                p_wc = count / N
                p_w = target_total / N
                p_c = context_totals[ctx] / N
                
                pmi = math.log2(p_wc / (p_w * p_c))
                ppmi = max(0.0, pmi)
                if ppmi > 0.0:
                    self.ppmi_vectors[target][ctx] = ppmi

    def cosine_similarity(self, word1, word2):
        v1 = self.ppmi_vectors.get(word1.lower(), {})
        v2 = self.ppmi_vectors.get(word2.lower(), {})
        
        if not v1 or not v2:
            return 0.0
            
        # Sparse dot product
        common_keys = set(v1.keys()) & set(v2.keys())
        dot = sum(v1[k] * v2[k] for k in common_keys)
        
        norm1 = math.sqrt(sum(val ** 2 for val in v1.values()))
        norm2 = math.sqrt(sum(val ** 2 for val in v2.values()))
        
        if norm1 == 0.0 or norm2 == 0.0:
            return 0.0
            
        return dot / (norm1 * norm2)

# Training corpus with distinct semantic domains (drinks vs animals)
corpus = [
    "She poured a chilled glass of fresh orange juice into the cup",
    "He drank a hot mug of black tea and sweet coffee in the morning",
    "Cold juice and warm tea are delicious refreshing drinks",
    "The domestic cat chased the small brown mouse across the garden",
    "A hungry dog barked at the swift cat near the wooden fence",
    "The lively puppy played with the clever dog in the backyard"
]

model = DistributionalSemantics(window_size=2)
model.fit(corpus)

print(f"Vocabulary size: {len(model.vocab)} words")
print(f"Total co-occurrence events: {model.total_pairs}")

pairs = [
    ("juice", "tea"),     # High semantic similarity (drinks)
    ("cat", "dog"),       # High semantic similarity (pets)
    ("juice", "dog"),     # Unrelated (drink vs pet)
    ("coffee", "tea")     # High semantic similarity (beverages)
]

print("\nDistributional Cosine Similarities (PPMI):")
for w1, w2 in pairs:
    sim = model.cosine_similarity(w1, w2)
    print(f"  Similarity('{w1}', '{w2}'): {sim:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Rare-word bias in raw PMI: Very rare words that happen to co-occur once receive artificially gigantic PMI scores because P(w) and P(c) in the denominator are minuscule. Mitigate using smoothed context marginal distributions: P_alpha(c) proportional to P(c)^0.75.
  • Selecting inappropriate context window sizes: Small context windows (w = 1 to 2) capture tight syntactic relations (adjective-noun, verb-object); large context windows (w = 8 to 15) capture broad topical/domain associations. Choose window size deliberately based on task.
  • Conflating antonyms with non-synonyms: Because antonyms like "hot" and "cold" or "large" and "small" share almost identical contexts, distributional models score them with high cosine similarity. Distributional similarity reflects relatedness, not necessarily identical polarity.
  • Extreme memory consumption on raw matrices: A vocabulary of 100,000 words produces a 100,000 x 100,000 matrix with 10 billion cells. Always store co-occurrence counts using sparse matrix formats (such as CSR).

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the foundational premise of the Distributional Hypothesis formulated by J.R. Firth and Zellig Harris?

Why: Firth's principle—"You shall know a word by the company it keeps"—establishes that contextual co-occurrence distributions reflect underlying lexical meaning, allowing models to learn semantic representations from raw text.

Q2Why is Positive Pointwise Mutual Information (PPMI) preferred over raw Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)?

Why: Negative PMI arises when two words co-occur less frequently than expected under independence. On finite corpora, this often reflects lack of data rather than genuine semantic repulsion. Truncating negative values to zero produces a clean, stable representation.

Q3Why does cosine similarity measure semantic relatedness more reliably than Euclidean distance between raw frequency vectors?

Why: A frequent word like "car" might appear 10,000 times while its synonym "automobile" appears 50 times. Their Euclidean distance is huge due to frequency scale differences. Cosine similarity evaluates the angle between vectors, measuring contextual distribution shape rather than absolute volume.
