Global vs Local Models: Gradient Boosting for Forecasting

Comparing individual per-series models against pooled tabular gradient boosters that learn shared cross-series representations across millions of time series.

01 The idea

Classical forecasting fits an independent model (such as ARIMA or ETS) to every individual series. While effective for isolated macroeconomic indicators, this local approach breaks down when managing thousands of retail products with short, noisy histories. Modern global forecasting trains a single machine learning model across all pooled time series simultaneously, cross-learning shared temporal dynamics, calendar events, and cross-sectional features.

Imagine a nationwide retailer managing 100,000 product-store combinations. A traditional local modelling strategy fits 100,000 independent ARIMA or exponential smoothing models every week. For a newly launched product with only three weeks of sales history, a local model fails completely because three data points are insufficient to estimate seasonal orders or trend parameters. If thousands of independent models fail to converge or require manual parameter tuning, maintaining the fleet becomes an operational impossibility.

A global model restructures the forecasting challenge into a large tabular regression problem. All 100,000 time series are stacked into a unified dataset where each row corresponds to a specific product, store, and timestamp. Features include historical lags, rolling statistics, calendar flags (day of week, month, holidays), and static entity attributes (category, store size, price tier). A single gradient boosted decision tree (such as LightGBM or XGBoost) is trained across all pooled records.

The global model cross-learns relationships across series: it discovers that discount promotions increase unit sales by 40% across all snack categories, and that sales dip on rainy Mondays across suburban stores. Because the model learns the structural mapping from features to demand, it can forecast a brand-new product on its very first day of sale by relying on its category, price, and store attributes. While local models remain ideal for isolated, high-frequency series with unique dynamics (like regional electrical grid load), global tree ensembles dominate large-scale retail competitions like the M5.

In everyday terms

A specialist doctor who has treated 10,000 diverse patients (global model) diagnosing a new patient instantly, versus a doctor who has only ever monitored one patient for a decade (local model).

02 Step by step

  1. Unpivot panel data to long tabular structure: stack all series into rows containing series ID, timestamp, and target values.
  2. Engineer temporal and metadata features: construct causal lag features, rolling summary statistics, calendar flags, and static entity properties.
  3. Normalise series to handle scale heterogeneity: scale each series by its historical mean volume or train on log-transformed targets.
  4. Train unified gradient boosting ensemble: fit LightGBM, CatBoost, or XGBoost minimising Huber or MAE loss across all pooled rows.
  5. Benchmark against local baselines: compare the global model against naive persistence and local exponential smoothing baselines on holdout horizons.

03 The maths

  1. Local independent model formulation

    \hat{y}_{i, t+h} = f_i\left(y_{i, 1:t}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_i\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}_i = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \sum_{s=1}^t L\left(y_{i, s}, f_i(y_{i, 1:s-1}; \boldsymbol{\theta})\right)

    Where an independent parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$ is estimated separately for each series $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$. Parameters cannot be shared or transferred across series.

  2. Global pooled model formulation

    \hat{y}_{i, t+h} = F\left(\mathbf{x}_{i, t}, \mathbf{z}_i; \boldsymbol{\Theta}\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Theta} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{s=1}^{T_i} L\left(y_{i, s}, F(\mathbf{x}_{i, s-1}, \mathbf{z}_i; \boldsymbol{\Theta})\right)

    Where $\mathbf{x}_{i, t}$ represents dynamic temporal features (lags, rolling stats), $\mathbf{z}_i$ represents static entity metadata, and parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ is shared across all $N$ series simultaneously.

  3. Normalised scaling for global pooling

    \tilde{y}_{i, t} = \frac{y_{i, t}}{s_i}, \qquad s_i = \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} y_{i, t} + \epsilon

    Dividing each series by its historical scale factor $s_i$ standardises target magnitudes across disparate products, preventing high-volume products from dominating gradient updates.

04 Key takeaways

  • Global models train a single estimator across thousands of pooled series, cross-learning shared patterns across items.
  • Global approaches naturally solve the cold-start problem by forecasting newly introduced products using static metadata attributes.
  • Scale normalisation is essential: without standardisation, high-volume series dictate tree split criteria at the expense of smaller series.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n_series, time_steps = 4, 30
scales = [10.0, 50.0, 100.0, 500.0]
season = np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(time_steps) / 7.0)

all_series = []
for s in scales:
    noise = np.random.normal(0, 0.05, size=time_steps)
    all_series.append(s * (1.0 + 0.3 * season + noise))

train_len, horizon = 24, 6
# 1. Local models: independent linear trend fitted per series
local_maes = []
for i in range(n_series):
    s_tr = all_series[i][:train_len]
    s_te = all_series[i][train_len:]
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(np.arange(train_len), s_tr, 1)
    pred_local = slope * np.arange(train_len, train_len + horizon) + intercept
    local_maes.append(np.mean(np.abs(s_te - pred_local)))

# 2. Global model: pooled regression across series normalised by training mean
X_pool, y_pool = [], []
for i in range(n_series):
    s_tr = all_series[i][:train_len]
    scale_i = np.mean(s_tr)
    norm_tr = s_tr / scale_i
    for t_idx in range(7, train_len):
        X_pool.append([norm_tr[t_idx-1], norm_tr[t_idx-7]])
        y_pool.append(norm_tr[t_idx])

X_pool, y_pool = np.array(X_pool), np.array(y_pool)
w = np.linalg.lstsq(X_pool, y_pool, rcond=None)[0]

global_maes = []
for i in range(n_series):
    s_tr = all_series[i][:train_len]
    s_te = all_series[i][train_len:]
    scale_i = np.mean(s_tr)
    norm_tr = list(s_tr / scale_i)
    preds_norm = []
    for h in range(horizon):
        pred_val = w[0] * norm_tr[-1] + w[1] * norm_tr[-7]
        norm_tr.append(pred_val)
        preds_norm.append(pred_val)
    pred_global = np.array(preds_norm) * scale_i
    global_maes.append(np.mean(np.abs(s_te - pred_global)))

print(f"Local models mean test MAE across series:  {np.mean(local_maes):.2f}")
print(f"Global model mean test MAE across series: {np.mean(global_maes):.2f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Failing to normalise target scales across series: high-volume series dominate tree splits, causing poor predictions on low-volume items.
  • Cross-series leakage in rolling features: computing rolling averages without grouping by series ID, accidentally blending unrelated series together.
  • Overfitting on high-cardinality raw series IDs: treating series ID as an unregularised categorical feature causes trees to memorise individual historical trajectories.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary operational advantage of a global forecasting model over thousands of local ARIMA models in large-scale retail?

Why: Global models pool observations across all products and stores, learning general promotional and seasonal dynamics while dramatically simplifying pipeline maintenance compared to managing 100,000 separate models.

Q2Why is target scale normalisation crucial when pooling disparate time series into a global gradient boosting model?

Why: If one series sells 50,000 units and another sells 20 units, absolute errors on the large series will overwhelm tree loss functions unless targets are normalised or log-transformed.

Q3In which scenario is an independent local forecasting model typically preferred over a global pooled model?

Why: When a single series has abundant history and highly specialised physical dynamics with no related series to learn from, a bespoke local model avoids negative transfer from irrelevant external data.
