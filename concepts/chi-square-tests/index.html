Chi-Square Tests

01 The idea

While t-tests evaluate continuous measurements, many empirical problems involve counts of categorical occurrences. Pearson's chi-square ($\chi^2$) tests assess whether observed categorical frequencies conform to a theoretical distribution (goodness-of-fit) or whether two categorical variables are statistically independent.

Suppose an engineering team tests whether user device type (Desktop vs Mobile) is independent of subscription tier choice (Free, Pro, Enterprise). Over a week, 500 new users register: Desktop users comprise 120 Free, 80 Pro, and 50 Enterprise (250 total); Mobile users comprise 180 Free, 50 Pro, and 20 Enterprise (250 total). Overall, 300 users choose Free (60%), 130 choose Pro (26%), and 70 choose Enterprise (14%).

If device type had zero influence on subscription choice (the null hypothesis of independence), both Desktop and Mobile users should mirror these overall 60%, 26%, and 14% shares. We would expect $250 \times 0.60 = 150.0$ Free Desktop users, $250 \times 0.26 = 65.0$ Pro Desktop users, and $250 \times 0.14 = 35.0$ Enterprise Desktop users. The exact same expected counts would apply to Mobile users.

Comparing observed counts to expected frequencies reveals clear discrepancies: Free Desktop count is 120 (30 fewer than expected), while Enterprise Desktop is 50 (15 more than expected). Pearson's chi-square test tallies the normalised squared discrepancy $(O - E)^2 / E$ across all six table cells. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling out, while dividing by $E$ scales each residual relative to its baseline magnitude. For this table, the sum of residuals yields $\chi^2 = 31.78$ with 2 degrees of freedom, producing a p-value of $1.26 \times 10^{-7}$. Because this is vastly smaller than $\alpha = 0.05$, we reject independence and confirm that subscription choice is strongly linked to device type.

In everyday terms

Pearson's chi-square test is like auditing a casino roulette wheel: you compare how often the ball lands in each pocket against how often it should land if the wheel were perfectly balanced.

02 Step by step

  1. Compile observed data into a two-way contingency table of raw frequency counts across $R$ rows and $C$ columns.
  2. Compute row totals $R_i$, column totals $C_j$, and the grand total sample size $N = \sum O_{ij}$.
  3. Under the null hypothesis of independence, calculate expected cell frequencies: $E_{ij} = (R_i \times C_j) / N$.
  4. Check Cochran's sample size criteria: ensure all $E_{ij} \ge 1$ and at least 80% of cells have $E_{ij} \ge 5$.
  5. Sum Pearson residuals $\sum (O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2 / E_{ij}$ to obtain the test statistic $\chi^2$ with degrees of freedom $\nu = (R - 1)(C - 1)$.
  6. Calculate the p-value from the $\chi^2(\nu)$ distribution and decide whether to reject the null hypothesis of independence.

03 The maths

  1. Pearson's Chi-Square Test Statistic

    $$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^R \sum_{j=1}^C \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} \sim \chi^2(\nu)$$

    Here $O_{ij}$ is the observed integer frequency in row $i$ and column $j$, and $E_{ij}$ is the expected frequency under $H_0$. Under the null hypothesis, as sample size $N \to \infty$, this sum of normalised squared deviations converges asymptotically to a chi-square distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom.

  2. Expected Frequencies and Degrees of Freedom

    $$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i C_j}{N}, \qquad \nu = (R - 1)(C - 1)$$

    Here $R_i$ is the $i$-th row marginal sum, $C_j$ is the $j$-th column marginal sum, and $N$ is total sample size. In an $R \times C$ contingency table, fixing row and column totals leaves $(R - 1)(C - 1)$ cells free to vary independently, determining the degrees of freedom $\nu$. For a 1D goodness-of-fit test with $K$ categories, $\nu = K - 1$.

  3. Goodness-of-Fit Test Statistic

    $$\chi^2 = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{(O_k - n p_k)^2}{n p_k} \sim \chi^2(K - 1 - m)$$

    In goodness-of-fit testing, observed counts $O_k$ across $K$ categories are compared to theoretical expected counts $n p_k$, where $p_k$ is the hypothesised probability of category $k$. If $m$ parameters of the theoretical distribution are estimated from the sample data (such as fitting a Poisson rate $\lambda$), the degrees of freedom are reduced to $\nu = K - 1 - m$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Chi-square tests require raw integer counts; never run chi-square calculations on percentages, fractions, or normalised rates.
  • Degrees of freedom in contingency tables depend exclusively on table dimensions $(R - 1)(C - 1)$, entirely independent of sample size $N$.
  • Cochran's guideline requires expected cell counts to equal or exceed 5 in at least 80% of cells; when counts are smaller, use Fisher's exact test.
  • A significant chi-square test confirms an association exists, but standardised residuals must be examined to identify which specific cells drive the divergence.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

# Contingency table: Device (Desktop, Mobile) vs Tier (Free, Pro, Enterprise)
observed = np.array([
    [120, 80, 50],   # Desktop
    [180, 50, 20],   # Mobile
])

# Pearson's chi-square test of independence
chi2_stat, p_val, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(observed)

print("Observed table:\n", observed)
print(f"Expected counts:\n{np.round(expected, 1)}")
print(f"Chi-square statistic: {chi2_stat:.3f}")
print(f"Degrees of freedom:   {dof}")
print(f"p-value:              {p_val:.4e}")
print(f"Independence rejected at alpha=0.05: {p_val <= 0.05}")

Common pitfalls

  • Inputting percentages or conversion rates instead of raw integer counts, which destroys the underlying multinomial sampling variance model.
  • Applying standard chi-square tests to repeated measures or paired categorical data (such as user choices before and after an update); use McNemar's test instead.
  • Ignoring low expected cell frequencies ($E_{ij} < 5$), where the continuous chi-square approximation fails and produces inaccurate p-values.
  • Confusing statistical association with causal direction: a significant chi-square test indicates dependence between variables, not that one variable causes the other.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is it invalid to run a chi-square test on percentages or proportions (e.g. 0.40 and 0.60) rather than raw counts?

Why: Chi-square test statistics rely on Poisson or multinomial sampling variance, where the variance of a count is proportional to $N$. Converting counts to proportions eliminates sample size information.

Q2How many degrees of freedom does a $3 \times 4$ contingency table have in a test of independence?

Why: Degrees of freedom for an $R \times C$ table are $(R - 1)(C - 1)$. For a $3 \times 4$ table, $(3 - 1)(4 - 1) = 2 \times 3 = 6$.

Q3When expected cell counts $E_{ij}$ in a $2 \times 2$ table fall below 5, which test should be used instead of Pearson's chi-square test?

Why: When cell counts are small ($E_{ij} < 5$), the continuous chi-square approximation breaks down. Fisher's exact test computes exact hypergeometric probabilities without relying on asymptotic approximations.

Q4What does a statistically significant result ($p < 0.001$) in a chi-square test of independence demonstrate?

Why: Rejecting the null hypothesis of independence indicates that the row and column classifications are statistically dependent, though it does not imply causal direction.

Q5In a customer survey of $N = 200$ people, 80 are mobile users and 120 are desktop users. Overall, 50 users prefer dark mode. Under the null hypothesis that UI preference is independent of device, what is the expected count of mobile users who prefer dark mode?

Why: Under the null hypothesis of independence, $E_{ij} = (R_i \times C_j) / N$. Here $E = (80 \times 50) / 200 = 4000 / 200 = 20.0$.

Q6What is Cochran's standard criterion for determining whether sample sizes are sufficient for the asymptotic chi-square approximation in an $R \times C$ contingency table?

Why: Cochran (1954) established that the continuous chi-square distribution accurately approximates multinomial cell probabilities when no expected cell frequency is less than 1 and at least 80% of cells have expected frequencies of 5 or more.

Q7100 users are surveyed on whether they like a software interface before and after a major redesign (producing binary Yes/No responses for each user at both time points). Why is Pearson's standard chi-square test of independence invalid for this table?

Why: Pearson's chi-square test requires mutually independent observations. When the same users are evaluated before and after an update, the data is paired (repeated measures); McNemar's test must be used instead.

Q8After obtaining a statistically significant chi-square test of independence in a $3 \times 3$ table, how can an analyst identify which specific combinations of categories deviate most strongly from independence?

Why: Standardised residuals $r_{ij} = (O_{ij} - E_{ij}) / \sqrt{E_{ij}}$ quantify the direction and magnitude of departure in each individual cell. Under $H_0$, they approximate standard normal variates, so values outside $[-2, +2]$ highlight cells that drive the overall significance.

Q9Consider the following code: table = [ [50, 30], [20, 60] ] chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(table, correction=False) What does the parameter correction=False control in this test?

Why: For $2 \times 2$ contingency tables, Yates' continuity correction adjusts for approximating discrete counts with a continuous distribution by subtracting 0.5 from each absolute difference $|O - E|$. Setting correction=False computes the uncorrected Pearson chi-square statistic.

Q10Consider the following $2 \times 2$ observed table: Row 1: [30, 10] (Total = 40) Row 2: [10, 30] (Total = 40) Column totals are 40 and 40, with grand total $N = 80$. Under $H_0$, all four expected counts are $E_{ij} = (40 \times 40) / 80 = 20$. What is the uncorrected Pearson chi-square statistic $\chi^2 = \sum (O - E)^2 / E$?

Why: For each of the 4 cells, $|O - E| = 10$, so $(O - E)^2 = 100$. Dividing by $E = 20$ yields $100 / 20 = 5.0$ per cell. Summing over all 4 cells gives $\chi^2 = 4 \times 5.0 = 20.0$.

Q11An auditor rolls a 6-sided die 60 times to test if it is fair ($p_k = 1/6$, expected count $E_k = 10$ for each face). The observed counts are: Face 1: 15, Face 2: 5, Face 3: 10, Face 4: 10, Face 5: 10, Face 6: 10. What is the chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic $\chi^2$ and its degrees of freedom?

Why: The discrepancy comes entirely from Face 1 ($25/10 = 2.5$) and Face 2 ($25/10 = 2.5$), summing to $\chi^2 = 5.0$. For goodness-of-fit with $K = 6$ categories and no estimated parameters, degrees of freedom are $K - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5$.

Q12A data scientist fits a Poisson distribution to website server crash counts grouped into $K = 8$ discrete frequency bins. The Poisson rate parameter $\lambda$ is not known in advance and must be estimated from the sample data. What are the degrees of freedom for the chi-square goodness-of-fit test?

Why: For a chi-square goodness-of-fit test, estimating $m$ parameters from the data imposes $m$ additional linear constraints on the expected counts. The degrees of freedom are $\nu = K - 1 - m$. With $K = 8$ bins and $m = 1$ parameter ($\lambda$), $\nu = 8 - 1 - 1 = 6$.
