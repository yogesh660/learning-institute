Mixed-effects models

Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMMs / hierarchical linear models) extend classical regression to handle clustered, grouped, longitudinal, and repeated-measures data by simultaneously estimating fixed effects (population-level averages) and random effects (cluster-specific deviations drawn from a probability distribution). By pooling information across groups through empirical Bayes shrinkage ("partial pooling"), mixed models avoid the dual pitfalls of complete pooling (ignoring cluster structure) and no pooling (overfitting isolated groups), providing unbiased variance components and robust inference under non-independent errors.

01 The idea

Suppose you measure patient blood pressure across 50 different hospitals. If you fit a single regression line ignoring hospitals (complete pooling), you violate the independence assumption and produce falsely confident standard errors. If you fit 50 separate regression models for each hospital (no pooling), small clinics with only 3 patients produce wild, noisy estimates. How do you share statistical strength across groups while respecting individual cluster idiosyncrasies? Mixed-effects models provide the mathematically principled answer through partial pooling and variance components.

The Three Regimes of Grouped Data: - Complete Pooling: Fit one global model y = X\beta + \epsilon. Treats all observations as independent. Violates independence, masks Simpson's paradox, and underestimates standard errors. - No Pooling: Fit separate isolated models or dummy indicators for every group. Small groups with few observations overfit wildly. Fails to capitalise on population commonalities. - Partial Pooling (Mixed Effects): Treat group differences as random draws from a latent distribution u_j ~ N(0, \sigma_u^2). Groups with abundant data rely on their own observations; groups with sparse data shrink automatically toward the global mean!

Fixed Effects vs Random Effects: - Fixed Effects (\beta): Non-random parameters representing constant population-wide relationships (e.g. the average effect of a drug dosage across all humans). - Random Effects (u): Random variables representing idiosyncratic cluster deviations (e.g. hospital baseline offsets or clinic-specific dosage sensitivities), governed by covariance matrix G.

Shrinkage and Empirical Bayes: The estimated random effect u_hat_j for group j shrinks toward 0 by shrinkage factor \sigma_u^2 / (\sigma_u^2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2 / n_j). When group sample size n_j is large, shrinkage is negligible; when n_j is small, shrinkage pulls the group toward the population grand mean, protecting against extreme outliers.

In everyday terms

Imagine a school district evaluating teachers based on student test scores. Complete pooling compares raw student scores across all schools, ignoring that some schools are in wealthy suburbs and others in underfunded areas. No pooling ranks teachers strictly within their own school, making a new teacher with only 4 students either the best or worst in the district due to small-sample noise. Mixed-effects models use partial pooling: a teacher with 4 students is gently pulled toward the district average until more student scores are collected, balancing local performance with district-wide context.

03 The maths

  1. Linear Mixed Model Formulation

    \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{u} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \mathbf{u} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{G}), \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}), \quad \mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \; \mathbf{V})

    Here \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} represents fixed effects across the population, and \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{u} represents group-specific random effects. The total marginal covariance matrix is \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{Z}^\top + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}, inducing non-zero correlations among observations from the same cluster.

  2. Empirical Bayes Shrinkage Factor (Random Intercept)

    \hat{u}_j = \frac{n_j \sigma_u^2}{\sigma^2 + n_j \sigma_u^2} \left( \bar{y}_j - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_j^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right) = \lambda_j \left( \bar{y}_j - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_j^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \right), \quad \lambda_j \in [0, 1)

    The random effect prediction for group j shrinks the observed group residual toward zero by shrinkage multiplier \lambda_j. For large sample sizes n_j, \lambda_j \approx 1 (no pooling); for small sample sizes n_j \to 0, \lambda_j \to 0, borrowing strength from the population grand mean (complete pooling).

  3. Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)

    \text{ICC} = \frac{\sigma_u^2}{\sigma_u^2 + \sigma^2} = \text{Corr}(y_{ij}, y_{ik}) \quad \text{for } j \neq k

    The ICC quantifies the proportion of total variance attributable to differences between clusters. It represents the expected correlation between two randomly drawn observations from the same cluster.

04 Key takeaways

  • Linear Mixed-Effects Models combine fixed effects (population averages) and random effects (cluster deviations).
  • Partial pooling via empirical Bayes shrinkage borrows statistical strength across groups, protecting small clusters from noisy overfitting.
  • Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) provides unbiased variance component estimation by accounting for fixed effect degrees of freedom.
  • The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) measures the proportion of variance attributable to grouping structure.

05 In code

python
"""
Linear Mixed-Effects Model Simulation
Demonstrates complete pooling vs no pooling vs partial pooling (LMM)
and empirical Bayes shrinkage across groups of varying sample sizes.
"""

import numpy as np

# Simulate hierarchical multi-centre clinical trial
# 8 clinics with vastly different patient counts (from 3 to 100 patients)
np.random.seed(42)
num_clinics = 8
clinic_sizes = [3, 4, 6, 10, 20, 35, 60, 100]

true_beta_0 = 10.0   # Population baseline score
true_beta_1 = 2.5    # Population treatment effect
true_sigma_u = 3.0   # Between-clinic standard deviation
true_sigma_e = 2.0   # Within-clinic residual noise

# Sample clinic random baseline offsets u_j ~ N(0, sigma_u^2)
clinic_offsets = np.random.normal(0, true_sigma_u, num_clinics)

records = []
for j in range(num_clinics):
    n_j = clinic_sizes[j]
    x_dosage = np.random.uniform(0.5, 4.0, n_j)
    noise = np.random.normal(0, true_sigma_e, n_j)
    y_response = (true_beta_0 + clinic_offsets[j]) + true_beta_1 * x_dosage + noise
    for i in range(n_j):
        records.append({
            "clinic": j,
            "x": x_dosage[i],
            "y": y_response[i]
        })

print(f"=== Hierarchical Dataset: {len(records)} patients across {num_clinics} clinics ===")
print("Clinic sizes:", clinic_sizes)
print(f"True Population Intercept: {true_beta_0:.2f} | True Between-Clinic Std: {true_sigma_u:.2f}\n")

# Calculate group sample means and residuals
clinic_means = []
for j in range(num_clinics):
    y_j = [r["y"] for r in records if r["clinic"] == j]
    clinic_means.append(np.mean(y_j))

grand_mean = np.mean([r["y"] for r in records])

# Partial Pooling (Empirical Bayes Shrinkage calculation)
# Shrinkage weight lambda_j = n_j * sigma_u^2 / (sigma_e^2 + n_j * sigma_u^2)
var_u = true_sigma_u ** 2
var_e = true_sigma_e ** 2

print("=== Comparison: No Pooling vs Partial Pooling (Shrinkage) ===")
print(f"{'Clinic':7s} | {'Size':5s} | {'Raw Mean':10s} | {'Shrinkage lambda':18s} | {'Partial Pooled':15s} | {'Offset from Grand':18s}")
print("-" * 82)

for j in range(num_clinics):
    n_j = clinic_sizes[j]
    raw_mean = clinic_means[j]
    lambda_j = (n_j * var_u) / (var_e + n_j * var_u)
    # Shrunk estimate: grand_mean + lambda_j * (raw_mean - grand_mean)
    partial_pooled = grand_mean + lambda_j * (raw_mean - grand_mean)
    
    print(f"Clinic {j:1d} | {n_j:5d} | {raw_mean:10.2f} | {lambda_j:18.3f} | {partial_pooled:15.2f} | {(partial_pooled - grand_mean):18.2f}")

icc = var_u / (var_u + var_e)
print("-" * 82)
print(f"Grand Mean: {grand_mean:.2f}")
print(f"True Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC): {icc:.3f}")
print("Notice: Small clinics (n=3, 4) experience heavy shrinkage toward the grand mean (lambda ~ 0.85),")
print("while large clinics (n=100) retain their observed data with virtually zero shrinkage (lambda ~ 0.99).")

Common pitfalls

  • Treating cluster variables as fixed effect dummies when predicting on new clusters: Fixed effect models cannot generalise to unseen clusters; random effects models quantify the population distribution of cluster effects.
  • Using standard Maximum Likelihood instead of REML for small sample sizes: ML variance estimates have downward bias of order O(p/n).
  • Ignoring random slopes: If treatment effects vary systematically across clusters, fitting only random intercepts yields overconfident standard errors for treatment.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does partial pooling in mixed-effects models outperform 'no pooling' (separate models per group) on small clusters?

Why: No pooling overfits small groups due to high sampling variance. Partial pooling shrinks small groups toward the population mean while allowing large groups to remain close to their sample means.

Q2What does the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) represent?

Why: ICC = \sigma_u^2 / (\sigma_u^2 + \sigma^2). It measures how much variance is between clusters versus within clusters, defining intra-cluster correlation.

Q3Why is Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) preferred over standard Maximum Likelihood (ML) for estimating variance components?

Why: Just as Bessel's correction divides by n-1 instead of n in sample variance, REML adjusts for estimated fixed effects \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}, yielding strictly unbiased variance component estimates.
