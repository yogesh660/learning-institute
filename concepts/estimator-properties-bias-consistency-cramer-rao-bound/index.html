Estimator properties: bias, consistency, Cramér–Rao bound

Statistical estimation evaluates how empirical sample statistics infer unknown true population parameters. The four fundamental criteria of estimator quality are Bias (systematic deviation from the true parameter), Consistency (convergence in probability to the truth as sample size approaches infinity), Efficiency (achieving the lowest possible variance among unbiased estimators), and Sufficiency. The Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) establishes the theoretical lower limit on estimator variance, governed by the inverse of the Fisher Information matrix I(\theta)^{-1}.

01 The idea

When estimating the variance of a population from n sample points, why do statisticians divide the sum of squared deviations by n-1 instead of n? Why does an estimator that works well on 50 samples fail to converge as you gather 50,000 samples? Evaluating estimators requires a rigorous mathematical vocabulary: bias, consistency, efficiency, and asymptotic normality. The Cramér–Rao inequality sets the ultimate speed limit: no matter how clever your estimator is, its precision is physically bounded by the information contained in the data.

Bias vs Consistency: - Bias is a finite-sample property: across repeated hypothetical experiments of fixed size n, does your estimator hit the bullseye on average? Bias(theta_hat) = E[theta_hat] - theta. - Consistency is an asymptotic property: as sample size n approaches infinity, does the distribution of theta_hat collapse to a single point mass on the exact true value? plim_{n -> inf} theta_hat = theta. - An estimator can be biased in finite samples but completely consistent. A famous example is the maximum likelihood estimator for sample variance: S_n^2 = (1/n) sum (x_i - x_bar)^2. It is biased by a factor of (n-1)/n, but as n -> inf, (n-1)/n -> 1, making it fully consistent.

Efficiency and the Cramér–Rao Bound: If two candidate estimators are both unbiased, which one should you choose? The one with smaller variance. But can variance be made arbitrarily small? No! The Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) proves that for any unbiased estimator, Var(theta_hat) >= 1 / I_n(theta), where I_n(theta) is Fisher Information. An estimator that achieves this theoretical minimum variance is called an efficient estimator or Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimator (MVUE).

The Bias-Variance Trade-off in Estimation: An unbiased estimator is not always the best choice. In many practical settings, introducing a small amount of bias (e.g. Ridge shrinkage, empirical Bayes shrinkage) reduces variance so dramatically that the total Mean Squared Error (MSE = Bias^2 + Variance) is substantially smaller than any unbiased estimator.

In everyday terms

Think of an archer shooting arrows at a target. Bias is how far the centre of the arrow cluster is from the bullseye. Variance is the scatter or spread of the arrows. Consistency means as the archer takes more and more shots, the cluster tightens infinitesimally toward the exact centre. The Cramér–Rao bound is the atmospheric wind limit: physics dictates that even a perfect robotic archer cannot cluster tighter than the fundamental aerodynamic turbulence of the arrows.

03 The maths

  1. Bias, Variance, and Mean Squared Error Decomposition

    \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta, \qquad \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2\right] = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \left[\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})\right]^2

    Mean Squared Error decomposes cleanly into variance (precision of the estimator across repeated samples) plus squared bias (systematic error). An estimator that sacrifices unbiasedness can achieve lower total MSE if the variance reduction outweighs the squared bias penalty.

  2. Fisher Information Formulation

    \mathcal{I}_n(\theta) = n \, \mathbb{E}\left[ \left(\frac{\partial \log f(X; \theta)}{\partial \theta}\right)^2 \right] = -n \, \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{\partial^2 \log f(X; \theta)}{\partial \theta^2} \right]

    Fisher Information measures the expected curvature (Hessian) of the log-likelihood function. If the log-likelihood is sharply curved around the true parameter, small deviations in parameter space cause steep drops in likelihood, meaning observations carry high information about \theta.

  3. The Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)

    \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \ge \frac{\left(1 + b'(\theta)\right)^2}{\mathcal{I}_n(\theta)}, \qquad \text{For unbiased estimators } (b(\theta) = 0): \; \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \ge \frac{1}{\mathcal{I}_n(\theta)}

    The Cramér–Rao inequality sets a fundamental lower bound on the variance of any estimator. For unbiased estimators, no valid statistical procedure can produce a variance smaller than the reciprocal of Fisher Information. Maximum likelihood estimators achieve the CRLB asymptotically as n \to \infty.

04 Key takeaways

  • Bias measures systematic error in finite samples: E[theta_hat] - theta.
  • Consistency measures asymptotic convergence in probability to the truth: as sample size grows, the estimator converges to the true parameter.
  • Fisher Information measures the log-likelihood curvature; higher curvature means samples carry greater parameter precision.
  • The Cramér–Rao Lower Bound defines the mathematical minimum achievable variance for any unbiased estimator: Var(theta_hat) >= 1 / I_n(theta).

05 In code

python
"""
Estimator Properties Simulation
Demonstrating finite-sample bias of sample variance, asymptotic consistency,
and Fisher Information vs Cramér-Rao Lower Bound for Poisson data.
"""

import numpy as np

# 1. Finite-Sample Bias of Variance Estimator (Bessel's Correction)
# Compare S_biased (1/n) vs S_unbiased (1/(n-1)) across 20,000 Monte Carlo trials
true_sigma2 = 25.0  # True variance
true_mu = 100.0
sample_size = 5     # Small sample size where bias is pronounced
num_simulations = 20000

np.random.seed(42)
samples = np.random.normal(loc=true_mu, scale=np.sqrt(true_sigma2), size=(num_simulations, sample_size))

# Sample variance with denominator n (MLE formula)
s2_mle = np.var(samples, axis=1, ddof=0)
# Sample variance with denominator n - 1 (Unbiased formula)
s2_unbiased = np.var(samples, axis=1, ddof=1)

mean_s2_mle = np.mean(s2_mle)
mean_s2_unbiased = np.mean(s2_unbiased)

theoretical_mle_bias = -true_sigma2 / sample_size
empirical_mle_bias = mean_s2_mle - true_sigma2

print(f"=== 1. Sample Variance Bias (n = {sample_size}, True Variance = {true_sigma2}) ===")
print(f"Mean MLE Estimator (ddof=0):     {mean_s2_mle:.3f} (Biased: underestimates variance)")
print(f"Empirical MLE Bias:              {empirical_mle_bias:.3f} | Theoretical Bias: {theoretical_mle_bias:.3f}")
print(f"Mean Unbiased Estimator (ddof=1):{mean_s2_unbiased:.3f} (Unbiased!)")

# 2. Asymptotic Consistency Demonstration
# Show that as n increases, the bias and variance of the MLE estimator vanish toward 0
sample_sizes = [5, 20, 100, 1000]
print("\n=== 2. Consistency: Asymptotic Behaviour as n -> Infinity ===")
for n in sample_sizes:
    s = np.random.normal(loc=true_mu, scale=np.sqrt(true_sigma2), size=(5000, n))
    v = np.var(s, axis=1, ddof=0)
    bias = np.mean(v) - true_sigma2
    mse = np.mean((v - true_sigma2)**2)
    print(f"  n = {n:4d} | Bias: {bias:7.3f} | Variance: {np.var(v):7.3f} | MSE: {mse:7.3f}")

# 3. Fisher Information and Cramér-Rao Lower Bound for Poisson(lambda)
# For Poisson(lambda), f(x) = exp(-lambda) * lambda^x / x!
# Log-likelihood: -lambda + x*log(lambda) - log(x!)
# d/d_lambda = -1 + x/lambda, d^2/d_lambda^2 = -x / lambda^2
# I_n(lambda) = -n * E[-X / lambda^2] = n * lambda / lambda^2 = n / lambda
# CRLB = 1 / I_n(lambda) = lambda / n
true_lambda = 4.0
n_poisson = 15
crlb = true_lambda / n_poisson

# The sample mean X_bar is an unbiased estimator for lambda:
poisson_samples = np.random.poisson(lam=true_lambda, size=(50000, n_poisson))
sample_means = np.mean(poisson_samples, axis=1)

empirical_var_mean = np.var(sample_means)
efficiency = crlb / empirical_var_mean

print(f"\n=== 3. Fisher Information & Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (Poisson lambda={true_lambda}, n={n_poisson}) ===")
print(f"Fisher Information I_n(lambda): {n_poisson / true_lambda:.3f}")
print(f"Theoretical CRLB Lower Bound:   {crlb:.5f}")
print(f"Empirical Var(X_bar):           {empirical_var_mean:.5f}")
print(f"Estimator Efficiency:           {efficiency:.3f} (1.0 = reaches CRLB speed limit exactly!)")

Common pitfalls

  • Assuming an unbiased estimator is always superior to a biased one: Biased estimators (like Ridge regression) often achieve lower total Mean Squared Error by dramatically reducing variance.
  • Confusing unbiasedness with consistency: An estimator can be unbiased yet inconsistent (e.g. taking only the very first sample X_1 regardless of total sample size n).
  • Applying the standard CRLB to biased estimators without adjustment: If an estimator has non-zero bias b(theta), the lower bound is scaled by [1 + b'(theta)]^2.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Bessel's correction divide by n-1 instead of n in sample variance calculation?

Why: Using the sample mean x_bar instead of true mean mu minimises the sum of squared deviations around x_bar, underestimating true variance by sigma^2 / n. Dividing by n-1 restores exact unbiasedness.

Q2What does the Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) establish for an unbiased estimator?

Why: The CRLB sets a strict mathematical lower limit on variance. An unbiased estimator that attains this bound cannot be improved in terms of variance and is termed efficient.

Q3Can an estimator be biased yet consistent?

Why: Consistency requires plim theta_hat = theta as n -> inf. Many maximum likelihood estimators (such as MLE sample variance) have finite-sample bias of order O(1/n), which vanishes asymptotically.
