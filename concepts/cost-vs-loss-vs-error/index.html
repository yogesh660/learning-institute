Error, Loss and Cost: Three Words, Three Meanings

Error is a single mistake, loss scores one prediction, cost (the objective) aggregates loss over data plus any penalty.

01 The idea

People use these words loosely, but the distinction is useful: error is the raw difference for one example, loss turns it into a penalty, and cost averages the loss over the dataset and adds regularisation. Training minimises the cost.

For one house, the error is $y - \hat y = 30$k. The squared loss of that prediction is $900$ (in k²). The cost is the average squared loss over all houses plus, say, $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$. The optimiser only ever sees the cost.

The metric you report (RMSE, accuracy, AUC) can differ from the loss you train on. You train with log-loss because it is smooth and convex, then report accuracy because people understand it. Keeping the four words apart avoids a lot of confusion.

In everyday terms

Error is how far one dart missed; loss is the points you lose for that miss; cost is your total penalty for the round, plus a fine for an overly fancy throwing style.

02 Step by step

  1. Error: $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ (or a 0/1 mistake for classification).
  2. Loss: $\ell(y_i, \hat y_i)$, e.g. $e_i^2$, $|e_i|$, log-loss.
  3. Cost: $J(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_i\ell(y_i, \hat y_i) + \lambda R(\theta)$.
  4. Metric: what you report on held-out data, chosen for the business question.

03 Try it

Five predictions, their errors and losses, and the cost they add up to. Drag the predictions, switch the loss, and add a penalty.

Things to try
  1. Switch from squared to absolute loss and see how the biggest error’s share of the cost drops.
  2. Raise λ: the cost rises even though no prediction changed.

04 The maths

  1. Empirical risk with a regulariser

    $$J(\theta) = \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell\big(y_i, f_\theta(x_i)\big)}_{\text{average loss (empirical risk)}} + \underbrace{\lambda R(\theta)}_{\text{penalty}}$$

    Minimising the first term alone is empirical risk minimisation; the second term is what Regularisation adds.

05 Key takeaways

  • The optimiser sees the cost; you report a metric. They are often different on purpose.
  • Loss is per example; cost is per dataset.
  • A model can lower its training cost and still get worse on the metric you care about: that is overfitting.

06 In code

python
import numpy as np

y, y_hat, w, lam = np.array([3., 5., 2.]), np.array([2.5, 6., 2.]), np.array([0.8, -1.2]), 0.1
error = y - y_hat                       # [ 0.5, -1. ,  0. ]
loss = error ** 2                       # per-example squared loss
cost = loss.mean() + lam * (w ** 2).sum()
rmse = np.sqrt(loss.mean())             # the metric you might report
print(error, loss, round(cost, 3), round(rmse, 3))

Common pitfalls

  • Tuning hyperparameters on training cost instead of a validation metric.
  • Reporting log-loss to stakeholders who expect accuracy, or the reverse.

07 Check yourself

Q1Which quantity does gradient descent actually minimise?

Why: It follows the gradient of the training objective.

Q2What is the fundamental distinction between "loss" and "cost" in machine learning?

Why: By standard convention, loss $\ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i)$ evaluates the error on a single instance, whereas cost $J(\theta)$ is the overall objective function summing or averaging losses across all training examples, plus any regularisation term.

Q3Why can gradient descent not directly optimise classification accuracy as its training objective?

Why: 0/1 mistake loss (and accuracy) has flat plateaus where small parameter tweaks make zero difference to discrete predictions, giving zero gradient. Smooth surrogate losses like log-loss provide continuous non-zero gradients everywhere.

Q4What is "empirical risk minimisation" (ERM)?

Why: The "empirical risk" is the sample average of the loss over observed training examples $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, f_\theta(x_i))$. Minimising it alone without regularisation is empirical risk minimisation (ERM).

Q5A binary classifier predicts a probability of $\hat{p} = 0.99$ for an observation whose true label is $y = 0$. What are the 0/1 classification error (threshold 0.5) and the binary cross-entropy loss $-\ln(1 - \hat{p})$?

Why: Because $\hat{p} = 0.99 \ge 0.5$, it predicts Class 1. Since the true label is $y = 0$, the 0/1 classification error is 1. The binary cross-entropy loss for $y = 0$ is $-\ln(1 - 0.99) = -\ln(0.01) \approx 4.605$, punishing confident wrong predictions heavily.

Q6Why do practitioners frequently train models using log-loss but evaluate and report test set ROC-AUC or accuracy to stakeholders?

Why: Training objectives require smooth gradients for numerical optimisers to find good parameters. Evaluation metrics, however, reflect real-world costs and operational interpretability for decision makers.

Q7During training of a neural network, the training cost $J(\theta)$ steadily decreases across 50 epochs, while the validation metric (error rate) stops decreasing at epoch 15 and begins to climb. What does this indicate?

Why: The optimiser successfully minimises the training cost function, but memorising training quirks harms generalisation to unseen validation data. This divergence is the hallmark of overfitting.

Q8What is the risk of selecting model hyperparameters by minimising training cost $J(\theta)$ rather than evaluating a validation metric?

Why: Zero regularisation ($\lambda = 0$) and maximal model capacity minimise empirical training loss to zero, but cause severe overfitting. Hyperparameters must be tuned against validation metrics that test unseen generalisation.

Q9In the cost function $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, f_\theta(x_i)) + \lambda R(\theta)$, what happens as the regularisation hyperparameter $\lambda \to \infty$?

Why: As $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalty term $R(\theta)$ overwhelmingly dominates the cost function, forcing the weights towards zero and causing severe underfitting.

Q10Consider a dataset of $n = 4$ regression points with true targets $y = [10, 20, 30, 40]$ and model predictions $\hat{y} = [12, 18, 35, 38]$. If the cost function is $J(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \lVert w\rVert^2$ with $\lambda = 2$ and $\lVert w\rVert^2 = 5$, what is the exact value of the cost $J(w)$?

Why: Errors: $e_1 = 10 - 12 = -2$; $e_2 = 20 - 18 = 2$; $e_3 = 30 - 35 = -5$; $e_4 = 40 - 38 = 2$. Squared losses: $(-2)^2 = 4$, $2^2 = 4$, $(-5)^2 = 25$, $2^2 = 4$. Mean squared loss $= \frac{4 + 4 + 25 + 4}{4} = \frac{37}{4} = 8.50$. Regularisation penalty $= \lambda \lVert w\rVert^2 = 2 \times 5 = 10.00$. Total cost $J(w) = 8.50 + 10.00 = 18.50$.

Q11Suppose a binary model outputs probabilities $\hat{p} = [0.90, 0.40]$ for two observations with true classes $y = [1, 0]$. If the model uses log-loss $\ell(y, \hat{p}) = -[y\ln(\hat{p}) + (1-y)\ln(1-\hat{p})]$, what is the average empirical loss across these two observations? (Given $\ln(0.90) \approx -0.1054$, $\ln(0.60) \approx -0.5108$).

Why: For instance 1 ($y = 1$): $\ell_1 = -\ln(0.90) \approx 0.1054$. For instance 2 ($y = 0$): $\ell_2 = -\ln(1 - 0.40) = -\ln(0.60) \approx 0.5108$. Total loss $= 0.1054 + 0.5108 = 0.6162$. Average empirical loss $= \frac{0.6162}{2} = 0.3081$.

Q12A regression model is evaluated on 3 validation points with true values $y = [5, 10, 15]$ and predictions $\hat{y} = [7, 6, 15]$. What are the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) on this validation set?

Why: Errors: $5 - 7 = -2$, $10 - 6 = 4$, $15 - 15 = 0$. Absolute errors: $|-2| = 2$, $|4| = 4$, $|0| = 0$. $\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{2 + 4 + 0}{3} = \frac{6}{3} = 2.00$. Squared errors: $(-2)^2 = 4$, $4^2 = 16$, $0^2 = 0$. Mean squared error $= \frac{4 + 16 + 0}{3} = \frac{20}{3} \approx 6.667$. $\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{20/3} \approx 2.582$.
