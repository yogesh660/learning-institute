The Bias–Variance Trade-off

Test error = bias² + variance + noise. Too simple underfits, too flexible overfits; the art is finding the middle.

01 The idea

A model’s expected error on new data splits into three parts: bias (systematic error from wrong assumptions), variance (sensitivity to the particular training sample) and irreducible noise. Making a model more flexible lowers bias and raises variance.

Train the same model on many different samples of the same size. If the average prediction is far from the truth, that is bias: a straight line cannot follow a curve no matter how much data you give it. If individual predictions scatter widely around their average, that is variance: a degree-15 polynomial bends differently for every sample.

Every regularisation knob in this curriculum (depth of a tree, $k$ in k-NN, $\lambda$ in ridge, number of boosting rounds) slides along this trade-off. Ensembles attack it directly: bagging averages away variance, boosting chips away at bias.

In everyday terms

Darts: bias is aiming at the wrong spot; variance is a shaky hand. You want neither.

02 Step by step

  1. Diagnose: compare training and validation error.
  2. High bias (both high, close together): add features, use a more flexible model, reduce regularisation.
  3. High variance (training low, validation much higher): get more data, regularise, simplify, ensemble.
  4. Tune complexity with cross-validation.

03 Try it

Twenty training sets drawn from the same noisy sine wave, each fitted with the degree you choose. The faint lines are the twenty fits; the bright line is their average.

Things to try
  1. Degree 1: the fits agree with each other but miss the curve (bias).
  2. Degree 12: the average is close to the truth but individual fits scatter wildly (variance).
  3. Find the degree where bias² + variance is smallest.

04 The maths

  1. Decomposition for squared error

    $$\mathbb E\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb E[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(\hat f(x) - \mathbb E[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}$$

    Expectations are over training sets drawn from the same distribution. The noise term sets a floor no model can beat.

05 Key takeaways

  • Training error alone tells you nothing about variance.
  • More data reduces variance, not bias.
  • Modern over-parameterised models can show "double descent", but the classical trade-off still guides everyday tuning.

06 In code

python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0); x_test = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)[:, None]; truth = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x_test).ravel()
for degree in [1, 4, 12]:
    preds = []
    for _ in range(200):                                   # many training sets
        x = rng.uniform(0, 1, (25, 1)); y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x).ravel() + rng.normal(0, .3, 25)
        preds.append(make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree), LinearRegression()).fit(x, y).predict(x_test))
    preds = np.array(preds)
    print(degree, "bias2=%.3f var=%.3f" % (((preds.mean(0) - truth) ** 2).mean(), preds.var(0).mean()))

Common pitfalls

  • Fighting a bias problem with more data.
  • Concluding "the model is bad" from one train/test split when variance is high.

07 Check yourself

Q1Training error 2%, validation error 25%. The problem is mostly:

Why: The model fits the training data but does not generalise.

Q2Which change reduces variance?

Why: Averaging reduces the spread of predictions across training samples.

Q3What is "irreducible error" ($\sigma^2$) in the bias–variance decomposition?

Why: Irreducible error represents unobserved variables, inherent randomness, and measurement noise in the true system. It represents the absolute theoretical lower bound on expected test loss.

Q4If both training error and validation error are high and close to each other (e.g. 35% and 36%), which diagnosis and remedy are most appropriate?

Why: When training error is high, the model lacks the expressiveness or features to capture the underlying pattern. This is high bias (underfitting). Collecting more data will not help a model that cannot fit the training data.

Q5Why does collecting more training data help resolve high variance, but fail to resolve high bias?

Why: Variance stems from estimating parameters on a finite sample; larger datasets make parameter estimates more stable. Bias stems from structural model assumptions: a straight line cannot bend to fit a parabola regardless of whether you train on 100 points or 10 million points.

Q6How does changing the number of neighbours $k$ in a $k$-Nearest Neighbours ($k$-NN) classifier alter the bias–variance trade-off?

Why: At $k = 1$, the decision boundary tightly wraps around every individual training point (100% training accuracy, extreme variance). As $k$ grows, the boundary becomes smoother and simpler, trading flexibility (higher bias) for stability (lower variance).

Q7In ensemble learning, what is the fundamental difference in how Bagging (e.g. Random Forest) and Boosting (e.g. AdaBoost) affect the bias–variance trade-off?

Why: Bagging trains independent deep trees and averages their predictions, which cancels out random sample noise (variance reduction). Boosting fits sequential shallow trees to unexplained errors, progressively lowering bias.

Q8When increasing the regularisation hyperparameter $\lambda$ from $0.001$ to $1{,}000$ in Ridge regression, how do bias and variance behave?

Why: Stronger regularisation constrains weights towards zero, restricting model flexibility. This makes predictions more stable across training sets (lower variance) at the cost of being less capable of fitting the true relationship (higher bias).

Q9What is the phenomenon of "double descent" observed in modern over-parameterised machine learning models?

Why: Classically, increasing capacity past the point of zero training error causes explosive variance. In modern deep learning and kernel methods, extreme over-parameterisation can lead to an implicit minimum-norm fit that smooths predictions, causing test error to decrease again after the interpolation peak.

Q10Suppose the true function is $f(x) = 10.0$ and irreducible noise is $\sigma^2 = 2.0$. An estimator evaluated at $x$ across many training sets has expected prediction $\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] = 8.0$ and variance $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(x)) = 5.0$. What is the expected test Mean Squared Error $\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2]$?

Why: $\text{Bias} = \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x) = 8.0 - 10.0 = -2.0$. Squared bias $= (-2.0)^2 = 4.0$. By the bias–variance decomposition: $\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2] = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2 = 4.0 + 5.0 + 2.0 = 11.0$.

Q11An ensemble averages the predictions of $B = 4$ identical, unbiased estimators $\hat{f}_1, \dots, \hat{f}_4$. Each individual estimator has variance $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}_i) = 12.0$, and the pairwise correlation between estimators is $\rho = 0.25$. Using $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\sigma^2$, what is the ensemble variance?

Why: $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\sigma^2 = 0.25(12.0) + \frac{1 - 0.25}{4}(12.0) = 3.0 + \frac{0.75}{4}(12.0) = 3.0 + 0.1875(12.0) = 3.0 + 2.25 = 5.25$. Averaging reduces variance from $12.0$ down to $5.25$.

Q12You evaluate a model on a training set of 500 rows and a validation set of 100 rows. With degree 1 (linear), training MSE is 45 and validation MSE is 46. With degree 10, training MSE is 4 and validation MSE is 120. With degree 3 with L2 regularisation, training MSE is 18 and validation MSE is 22. What are the dominant error sources for degrees 1 and 10?

Why: For degree 1, training error is high and matches validation error (45 vs 46), characteristic of underfitting (high bias). For degree 10, training error drops to 4 while validation explodes to 120 (gap of 116), characteristic of overfitting (high variance). Regularised degree 3 strikes the optimal compromise.
