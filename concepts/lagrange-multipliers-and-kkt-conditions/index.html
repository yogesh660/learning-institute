01 The idea

Constrained optimisation problems govern much of machine learning, from Support Vector Machines maximising geometric margins to LASSO shrinking coefficients within an $\\ell_1$ budget. Lagrange multipliers provide the analytical machinery to handle equality constraints by balancing objective gradients against constraint normals. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions extend this principle to inequality constraints, providing necessary (and often sufficient) conditions that identify active constraints and optimal solutions.

Imagine rolling a marble down a smooth, bowl-shaped wooden track. If the track has no barriers, the marble rolls straight down to the bottom where the surface is flat (gradient equals zero). Now place a stiff circular fence on the slope. The marble rolls downhill until it collides with the fence and comes to a dead stop. At that resting point, gravity is still pulling the marble downward, but the fence pushes back with an equal and opposite force perpendicular to the barrier.

This physical balance illustrates the Lagrange multiplier. The gradient of the objective function $\\nabla f_0(x)$ represents the downhill pull of gravity. The gradient of the constraint boundary $\\nabla h(x)$ represents the normal force exerted by the fence. At the constrained optimum, these two vectors must align in opposite directions: $\\nabla f_0(x^*) + \\nu^* \\nabla h(x^*) = 0$. The scalar multiplier $\\nu^*$ measures the exact intensity of force the constraint fence exerts to keep the marble in place.

When constraints are inequalities ($f_i(x) \\le 0$), a new question arises: does the marble hit the fence, or does it settle naturally in the interior before reaching it? This is governed by complementary slackness: $\\lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0$. If the unconstrained minimum lies inside the fenced area ($f_i(x^*) < 0$), the fence is inactive and exerts zero force ($\\lambda_i^* = 0$). If the marble is pressed against the fence ($f_i(x^*) = 0$), the fence is active and exerts positive resistance ($\\lambda_i^* > 0$). In Support Vector Machines, this simple condition determines which data points become support vectors.

In everyday terms

A dog resting on a lead: if the dog sits inside the yard, the lead is slack and exerts zero tension; if the dog tries to run past the gate, the lead pulls taut with positive tension exactly counteracting the dog's forward momentum.

02 Step by step

  1. Formulate the constrained problem: minimise objective $f_0(x)$ subject to inequality constraints $f_i(x) \\le 0$ ($i=1,\\dots,m$) and equality constraints $h_j(x) = 0$ ($j=1,\\dots,p$).
  2. Construct the Lagrangian: $L(x, \\lambda, \\nu) = f_0(x) + \\sum_{i=1}^m \\lambda_i f_i(x) + \\sum_{j=1}^p \\nu_j h_j(x)$.
  3. Check Stationarity: set the gradient with respect to $x$ to zero: $\\nabla_x L(x^*, \\lambda^*, \\nu^*) = \\nabla f_0(x^*) + \\sum \\lambda_i^* \\nabla f_i(x^*) + \\sum \\nu_j^* \\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$.
  4. Enforce Primal Feasibility: verify all constraints are satisfied: $f_i(x^*) \\le 0$ and $h_j(x^*) = 0$.
  5. Enforce Dual Feasibility: ensure inequality multipliers are non-negative: $\\lambda_i^* \\ge 0$.
  6. Enforce Complementary Slackness: verify $\\lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0$ for each inequality constraint, separating active constraints ($\\lambda_i^* > 0, f_i(x^*) = 0$) from inactive ones ($\\lambda_i^* = 0, f_i(x^*) < 0$).

03 The maths

  1. The four Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions

    $$\\begin{aligned} \\text{1. Stationarity:} & \\quad \\nabla f_0(x^*) + \\sum_{i=1}^m \\lambda_i^* \\nabla f_i(x^*) + \\sum_{j=1}^p \\nu_j^* \\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0 \\\\ \\text{2. Primal Feasibility:} & \\quad f_i(x^*) \\le 0 \\quad (\\forall i), \qquad h_j(x^*) = 0 \\quad (\\forall j) \\\\ \\text{3. Dual Feasibility:} & \\quad \\lambda_i^* \\ge 0 \\quad (\\forall i) \\\\ \\text{4. Complementary Slackness:} & \\quad \\lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0 \\quad (\\forall i) \\end{aligned}$$

    For any optimisation problem with zero duality gap, any pair of primal and dual optimal points $(x^*, \\lambda^*, \\nu^*)$ must satisfy these four conditions. If the primal problem is convex, the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for global optimality.

  2. Geometric interpretation of stationarity

    $$-\\nabla f_0(x^*) = \\sum_{i \in \\mathcal{A}} \\lambda_i^* \\nabla f_i(x^*) + \\sum_{j=1}^p \\nu_j^* \\nabla h_j(x^*)$$

    where $\\mathcal{A} = \\{i : f_i(x^*) = 0\\}$ is the active constraint set. The negative gradient of the objective (the direction of steepest descent) lies inside the cone spanned by the outward normal vectors of the active constraints.

  3. Complementary slackness in Support Vector Machines

    $$\\alpha_i^* \\big(y_i(w^T x_i + b) - 1\\big) = 0$$

    If a training point lies strictly outside the margin ($y_i(w^T x_i + b) > 1$), its multiplier $\\alpha_i^* = 0$, meaning the point exerts no influence on the decision boundary. Only points lying directly on the margin boundary have $\\alpha_i^* > 0$, forming the sparse set of support vectors.

04 Key takeaways

  • Stationarity balances objective gradients against the linear combination of active constraint gradients.
  • Complementary slackness ($\\lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0$) guarantees that non-binding constraints exert zero force on the optimal solution.
  • For convex optimisation problems satisfying Slater's condition, the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for global optimality.
  • Forms the core mathematical justification for support vectors in SVMs, lasso sparsity, and barrier/interior-point algorithms.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Problem: min 0.5 * ||x - x0||^2 subject to a^T x <= b (halfspace projection)
x0 = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
a = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
b = 5.0

# Check unconstrained violation: a^T x0 - b
viol = a @ x0 - b  # 3*1 + 4*2 - 5 = 6 > 0 (infeasible)
if viol <= 0:
    x_star = x0
    lam_star = 0.0
else:
    # Active constraint: lam = (a^T x0 - b) / ||a||^2
    lam_star = viol / np.sum(a ** 2)
    x_star = x0 - lam_star * a

# Verify the 4 KKT conditions
grad_L = (x_star - x0) + lam_star * a       # 1. Stationarity (~ 0)
primal_feas = a @ x_star - b <= 1e-9        # 2. Primal feasibility
dual_feas = lam_star >= 0.0                 # 3. Dual feasibility
comp_slack = lam_star * (a @ x_star - b)    # 4. Complementary slackness (~ 0)

print(f"Unconstrained point: {x0}")
print(f"Optimal constrained point x*: {x_star.round(4)}")
print(f"Lagrange multiplier lambda*: {lam_star:.4f}")
print(f"1. Stationarity norm: {np.linalg.norm(grad_L):.6f}")
print(f"2. Primal feasible: {primal_feas}")
print(f"3. Dual feasible: {dual_feas}")
print(f"4. Complementary slackness: {comp_slack:.6f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Neglecting dual feasibility sign ($\\lambda_i \\ge 0$): For inequality constraints $f_i(x) \\le 0$, $\\lambda_i$ must be non-negative. A negative $\\lambda_i$ means the constraint is pulling the solution into the forbidden region rather than repelling it.
  • Assuming KKT is sufficient for non-convex problems: In non-convex optimisation, points satisfying the KKT conditions can be saddle points, local maxima, or suboptimal local minima. Sufficiency requires convexity of the objective and constraint functions.
  • Ignoring constraint qualification failure: When gradients of active constraints are linearly dependent at the optimum (such as touching tangent constraints), the standard KKT conditions may fail to hold even at the true global minimum.
  • Misinterpreting inactive constraints: An inactive constraint ($f_i(x^*) < 0$) has $\\lambda_i^* = 0$, meaning removing that constraint entirely leaves the optimal solution unchanged.

06 Check yourself

Q1What does the complementary slackness condition $\\lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0$ signify about an inequality constraint $f_i(x) \\le 0$?

Why: Complementary slackness dictates that if a point lies in the strict interior of the feasible region ($f_i(x^*) < 0$), the constraint imposes no restriction and $\\lambda_i^* = 0$. If $\\lambda_i^* > 0$, the point must lie precisely on the boundary fence ($f_i(x^*) = 0$).

Q2Why are the KKT conditions both necessary AND sufficient for global optimality in convex problems, but only necessary in general problems?

Why: For convex functions, zero gradient implies a global minimum. When paired with constraint qualifications, any point satisfying the KKT conditions is certified to be the global optimum. In non-convex problems, KKT points can be local minima, local maxima, or saddle points.

Q3In a Support Vector Machine, how does complementary slackness explain why only a small subset of training points become support vectors?

Why: The margin constraint is $1 - y_i(w^T x_i + b) \\le 0$. For any point strictly beyond the margin, $1 - y_i(w^T x_i + b) < 0$. By complementary slackness $\\alpha_i (1 - y_i(w^T x_i + b)) = 0$, the multiplier $\\alpha_i$ must be zero, leaving only boundary points with $\\alpha_i > 0$ as support vectors.

Q4What does the magnitude of a Lagrange multiplier $\\lambda_i^*$ represent from an economic or sensitivity perspective?

Why: In sensitivity analysis, $\\lambda_i^*$ measures how much the optimal objective value improves if the $i$-th constraint is relaxed by an infinitesimal amount: $\\frac{\\partial p^*}{\\partial b_i} = -\\lambda_i^*$. It represents the marginal cost of the constraint.
