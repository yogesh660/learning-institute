Thresholding, histograms and morphology

Binarising grayscale images into foreground and background masks is the foundational step in document processing, medical cell segmentation, and industrial inspection. Intensity histograms reveal pixel frequency distributions, guiding automated global threshold selection via Otsu's variance-maximising algorithm. Mathematical morphology then cleans and refines the resulting binary masks through non-linear geometric set operations—erosion, dilation, opening, and closing—governed by structured probe elements.

01 The idea

Before deep neural networks achieved widespread adoption in computer vision, industrial inspection systems sorted manufactured components, read dot-peen serial barcodes, and extracted medical cell boundaries using a two-stage paradigm: thresholding and mathematical morphology. By converting continuous grayscale intensities into discrete binary masks and probing their spatial geometry with structuring elements, vision practitioners eliminate sensor speckles, bridge hairline fractures, and separate touching objects with determinism and sub-millisecond execution.

The image histogram as an empirical probability density: An intensity histogram tallies the frequency count $n_k$ of each grayscale intensity level $k \in \{0, 1, \dots, L-1\}$. Normalising the histogram by the total pixel count $N = H \times W$ yields an empirical probability mass function $p(k) = n_k / N$. When an image contains a dark object on a bright background (or vice versa), its histogram displays a bimodal profile: two prominent probability peaks separated by a deep valley representing the boundary transition.

Otsu's automated variance criterion: How can an algorithm choose an optimal threshold $t^*$ without manual human tuning? In 1979, Nobuyuki Otsu demonstrated that the optimal global threshold maximises between-class variance $\sigma_B^2(t)$ between foreground and background (which is mathematically equivalent to minimising within-class variance $\sigma_W^2(t)$). By treating threshold selection as an optimal one-dimensional clustering problem over the histogram, Otsu's method pinpoints the exact valley floor separating the two statistical populations.

Limitations of global thresholds and the necessity of adaptive methods: Under uniform studio illumination, a single global cutoff works reliably. However, in real-world scenes with spatial lighting gradients, cast shadows, or lens vignetting, global thresholding fails catastrophically: the bright background on the shadowed side of a page may have lower intensity than the dark foreground on the sunlit side. Adaptive (local) thresholding resolves this by calculating dynamic thresholds within a sliding neighbourhood window.

Mathematical morphology as geometric probing: Once an image is binarised into Boolean sets (0s and 1s), linear convolution filters no longer apply. Mathematical morphology replaces linear arithmetic with set theory and order statistics. By translating a small geometric probe called a structuring element (such as a $3 \times 3$ cross, rectangle, or disc) across the binary mask, morphological operators measure how the probe interacts with foreground shapes.

Filtering via opening and closing: Composing the two primary morphological primitives—erosion (which shrinks foreground shapes by requiring the structuring element to fit entirely inside) and dilation (which expands foreground shapes whenever the structuring element touches)—creates powerful geometric filters. Opening (erosion followed by dilation) removes isolated background noise specks and thin protrusions without altering the macro dimensions of large objects. Closing (dilation followed by erosion) bridges narrow hairline gaps, welds broken stroke contours, and seals internal pinhole voids.

In everyday terms

Imagine clearing a manicured lawn strewn with leaves, fallen twigs, and children's toy building blocks. Otsu's thresholding is like an automated metal detector that calibrates its sensitivity knob to find the sharpest mathematical separation between heavy metallic objects and light organic debris. Morphological erosion is like sweeping the lawn with a rigid rotary brush whose bristles have a radius of three centimetres: any leaf or twig narrower than three centimetres is swept away, and the edges of larger toys are slightly pared back. Morphological dilation is like rolling a sticky paint roller over the lawn: it expands the perimeter of every remaining toy outwards by three centimetres. Composing both into an opening operation sweeps away all tiny organic specks while restoring genuine large toys to their original dimensions.

03 The maths

  1. Otsu's between-class variance formulation

    \sigma_B^2(t) = \omega_0(t) \omega_1(t) \left(\mu_0(t) - \mu_1(t)\right)^2

    Here $\omega_0(t) = \sum_{i=0}^t p_i$ is the cumulative probability of class $C_0$, $\omega_1(t) = 1 - \omega_0(t)$ is the probability of class $C_1$, $\mu_0(t) = \frac{1}{\omega_0(t)} \sum_{i=0}^t i\,p_i$ is the mean intensity of class $C_0$, and $\mu_1(t) = \frac{1}{\omega_1(t)} \sum_{i=t+1}^{L-1} i\,p_i$ is the mean intensity of class $C_1$. Maximising $\sigma_B^2(t)$ is equivalent to minimising within-class variance $\sigma_W^2(t)$.

  2. Morphological erosion and dilation

    (A \ominus B)(x) = \min_{y \in B} A(x + y), \quad (A \oplus B)(x) = \max_{y \in B} A(x - y)

    For binary images, erosion corresponds to a spatial local minimum (Boolean AND over structuring element support $B$), shrinking foreground boundaries. Dilation corresponds to a spatial local maximum (Boolean OR over structuring element support $B$), expanding foreground boundaries.

  3. Morphological opening and closing

    A \circ B = (A \ominus B) \oplus B, \quad A \bullet B = (A \oplus B) \ominus B

    Opening is anti-extensive ($A \circ B \subseteq A$) and idempotent ($(A \circ B) \circ B = A \circ B$), filtering out isolated foreground specks. Closing is extensive ($A \subseteq A \bullet B$) and idempotent, bridging hairline gaps and filling internal pinhole voids.

04 Key takeaways

  • Intensity histograms quantify grayscale distribution profiles and expose bimodal contrast separations.
  • Otsu's algorithm automatically determines the optimal global binarisation threshold by maximising inter-class variance.
  • Adaptive thresholding computes localized cutoffs within sliding windows to overcome non-uniform illumination and shadow gradients.
  • Erosion shrinks foreground shapes and destroys small specks; dilation expands foreground perimeters and fills voids.
  • Opening (erosion then dilation) removes background noise without shrinking large shapes; closing (dilation then erosion) bridges cracks and seals holes.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Create an 8x8 synthetic image with bimodal histogram (dark background, bright object)
np.random.seed(42)
img = np.random.randint(20, 60, size=(8, 8)).astype(np.uint8)   # background ~ 40
img[2:6, 2:6] = np.random.randint(180, 220, size=(4, 4))       # square object ~ 200
img[3, 3] = 45                                                  # interior hole
img[0, 0] = 210                                                 # isolated noise speck

# 1. Normalised intensity histogram
hist, bin_edges = np.histogram(img, bins=256, range=(0, 256))
p = hist / float(img.size)

# 2. Otsu's thresholding: maximise between-class variance sigma_B^2(t)
total_mean = np.sum(np.arange(256) * p)
best_t = 0
max_var = -1.0

omega0 = 0.0
sum0 = 0.0

for t in range(256):
    omega0 += p[t]
    if omega0 == 0.0:
        continue
    omega1 = 1.0 - omega0
    if omega1 == 0.0:
        break

    sum0 += t * p[t]
    mu0 = sum0 / omega0
    mu1 = (total_mean - sum0) / omega1
    var_between = omega0 * omega1 * ((mu0 - mu1) ** 2)

    if var_between > max_var:
        max_var = var_between
        best_t = t

print(f"Optimal Otsu threshold: t* = {best_t} (between background ~40 and object ~200)")

# 3. Binary mask
binary = (img >= best_t).astype(np.uint8)
print("\nInitial Binary Mask (1 = foreground, contains speck at (0,0) and hole at (3,3)):")
print(binary)

# 4. Mathematical morphology with 3x3 structuring element
def erode(mask):
    H, W = mask.shape
    out = np.zeros_like(mask)
    for r in range(1, H - 1):
        for c in range(1, W - 1):
            out[r, c] = np.min(mask[r-1:r+2, c-1:c+2])
    return out

def dilate(mask):
    H, W = mask.shape
    out = np.zeros_like(mask)
    for r in range(1, H - 1):
        for c in range(1, W - 1):
            out[r, c] = np.max(mask[r-1:r+2, c-1:c+2])
    return out

# Opening = erode then dilate (removes small isolated foreground specks)
opening = dilate(erode(binary))

# Closing = dilate then erode (fills small interior holes)
closing = erode(dilate(binary))

print(f"\nNoise speck at (0,0) removed by Opening: {opening[0, 0] == 0}")
print(f"Hole at (3,3) filled by Closing:          {closing[3, 3] == 1}")

Common pitfalls

  • Applying Otsu's method to unimodal or multimodal histograms: If an image lacks clear foreground-background contrast separation, Otsu artificially forces a cut through a continuous unimodal distribution, corrupting uniform textures.
  • Relying on global thresholding across non-uniform lighting: In scenes with directional shadows or vignetting, global thresholding fails; local adaptive thresholding (such as Gaussian or mean adaptive thresholding) is required.
  • Inverting binary polarity: Optical character recognition pipelines expect foreground character glyphs to be represented by binary 1s; thresholding black text on white paper requires inverting polarity (using cv2.THRESH_BINARY_INV).
  • Selecting an oversized structuring element: Using an excessively large structuring element during morphological opening will obliterate fine structural features, such as the crossbars on letters 'e' and 't'.
  • Confusing opening with closing: Opening removes isolated foreground specks; closing fills isolated holes. Inverting their sequence in noise removal pipelines leads to either dilated noise blobs or fragmented shapes.

06 Check yourself

Q1What statistical objective does Otsu's thresholding algorithm maximise when selecting the optimal global cutoff $t^*$?

Why: Otsu's method searches for the threshold that maximises between-class variance $\sigma_B^2(t)$, which simultaneously minimises within-class variance $\sigma_W^2(t)$, finding the optimal statistical separation between foreground and background.

Q2Why does global thresholding fail on images of documents captured under uneven, directional lighting?

Why: Under non-uniform illumination, absolute pixel brightness reflects lighting gradients rather than material reflectance. A single threshold cannot separate foreground from background globally; local adaptive thresholding is required.

Q3In mathematical morphology, what operation does an "opening" perform on a binary mask?

Why: Opening ($A \circ B = (A \ominus B) \oplus B$) erodes away small foreground specks that cannot contain the structuring element, and then dilates the surviving core shapes back to their original dimensions.

Q4What morphological filter is best suited to bridge small hairline fractures and fill tiny interior voids within a segmented object?

Why: Closing ($A \bullet B = (A \oplus B) \ominus B$) first dilates the shape to seal small interior gaps and holes, and then erodes the outer perimeter back to its original size, preserving boundary closure.
