Generalised Additive Models (GAMs) and Splines

Generalised Additive Models replace rigid linear coefficients with smooth, penalised spline curves, capturing non-linear relationships while preserving additive interpretability.

01 The idea

Linear models are transparent but miss curved relationships; deep neural networks and tree ensembles model complex curvature but operate as black boxes. Generalised Additive Models (GAMs) strike a balance: each feature enters through a smooth, non-parametric spline function $f_j(x_j)$, enabling visual interpretability while roughness penalties prevent overfitting.

Consider how ambient temperature influences energy demand. On freezing winter days (-5°C), electricity demand surges as heating systems run. In mild spring weather (18°C), demand drops to a minimum. In sweltering summer heat (35°C), demand surges once again as air conditioning units operate at full capacity. A standard linear regression line $y = \beta x$ is ineffective: a positive slope ignores winter heating, while a negative slope ignores summer cooling. High-order polynomials ($x^2, x^3, x^4$) bend, but they oscillate wildly near boundaries (Runge's phenomenon).

Splines resolve this tension by stitching simple, well-behaved cubic polynomials together at dividing boundaries called knots. By requiring the curve, its slope (first derivative), and its curvature (second derivative) to match continuously across every knot, cubic splines flow smoothly across complex landscapes without wild edge wobbles. To ensure the curve captures true underlying physics rather than chasing sample noise, a roughness penalty $\lambda \int [f''(x)]^2 dx$ penalises excessive curvature.

Generalised Additive Models assemble these smooth univariate splines into an additive sum: $y = \beta_0 + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + \dots + f_p(x_p) + \epsilon$. Because the model is strictly additive, each function $f_j(x_j)$ can be plotted on a 2D graph holding other variables constant. A data scientist can show stakeholders the isolated curve for temperature, the separate curve for humidity, and a categorical shift for weekends, making GAMs a premier framework for transparent, audited machine learning.

In everyday terms

If linear regression is a rigid wooden yardstick and a neural network is flexible clay, a GAM is an architect's spline ruler: an elastic strip pinned at designated anchor points that flexes gracefully to match terrain without chaotic flapping.

02 Step by step

  1. Examine bivariate relationships via scatter plots and partial dependence profiles to detect non-linear curves or non-monotonic trends.
  2. Select a spline basis expansion for continuous features, such as natural cubic splines or B-splines, placing interior knots across quantiles of $x_j$.
  3. Construct the basis design matrix $\mathbf{B}$ by evaluating the basis functions at each observed feature coordinate.
  4. Define the penalty matrix $\mathbf{S}$ corresponding to the integrated second derivative of the basis functions to penalise unnecessary curvature.
  5. Estimate spline coefficients and smoothing parameter $\lambda$ using Penalised Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (P-IRLS) with Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) or Generalised Cross-Validation (GCV).
  6. Plot individual partial effect curves $f_j(x_j)$ alongside pointwise confidence intervals, inspecting Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF) to assess whether non-linear flexibility is warranted.

03 The maths

  1. The Generalised Additive Model formulation

    $$g(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]) = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^p f_j(x_j), \quad f_j(x_j) = \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_{jk} \, b_{jk}(x_j)$$

    $g(\cdot)$ denotes a monotonic link function (such as identity for regression or logit for classification), $f_j$ are smooth non-parametric functions, and $b_{jk}(x_j)$ represent pre-specified basis functions (e.g. cubic B-splines) weighted by estimated coefficients $\beta_{jk}$.

  2. Penalised spline objective (smoothing spline)

    $$\min_{\beta} \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^p f_j(x_{ij})\Big)^2 + \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j \int \big(f_j''(t)\big)^2 \, dt$$

    The objective balances fidelity to training data against curve roughness. $\int [f_j''(t)]^2 dt$ quantifies total second-derivative curvature. Smoothing parameter $\lambda_j \ge 0$ controls the trade-off: as $\lambda \to 0$, the curve interpolates data; as $\lambda \to \infty$, the penalty forces $f_j''(t) = 0$, reducing the curve to a standard linear function.

  3. Penalised least squares and Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF)

    $$\hat{\beta} = (\mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{B} + \lambda \mathbf{S})^{-1} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{y}, \quad \operatorname{EDF} = \operatorname{tr}\Big(\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{B} + \lambda \mathbf{S})^{-1} \mathbf{B}^T\Big)$$

    $\mathbf{B}$ is the basis evaluation matrix and $\mathbf{S}$ is the penalty matrix. Effective Degrees of Freedom ($\operatorname{EDF}$) measures the non-linear flexibility of the fitted term: $\operatorname{EDF} \approx 1$ represents a strict linear relationship, while higher $\operatorname{EDF}$ values indicate increasingly complex, multi-inflection curves.

04 Key takeaways

  • Additive transparency: each feature contribution $f_j(x_j)$ can be plotted and explained independently of all other inputs.
  • Curvature penalisation: the smoothing penalty $\lambda$ adaptively contracts unnecessary wiggles, preventing overfitting without manual knot tuning.
  • Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF) provides a principled metric of non-linearity: an EDF near 1 justifies simplifying to a standard linear coefficient.
  • Exponential family flexibility: GAMs seamlessly handle binary, count, and skewed outcomes through canonical GLM link functions.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 80
x = np.sort(np.random.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n))
y_true = np.sin(x)
y = y_true + np.random.normal(0, 0.25, n)

# Natural cubic spline / truncated power basis with 5 interior knots
knots = np.quantile(x, [0.15, 0.35, 0.55, 0.75, 0.9])
def build_basis(x_pts, k_pts):
    cols = [np.ones_like(x_pts), x_pts]
    for k in k_pts:
        cols.append(np.maximum(0.0, x_pts - k)**3)
    return np.column_stack(cols)

B = build_basis(x, knots)
p = B.shape[1]

# Roughness penalty matrix S approximating integrated second derivatives
grid = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 200)
dx = grid[1] - grid[0]
d2_cols = [np.zeros_like(grid), np.zeros_like(grid)]
for k in knots:
    d2_cols.append(6.0 * np.maximum(0.0, grid - k))
D2 = np.column_stack(d2_cols)
S = (D2.T @ D2) * dx

# Solve penalised least squares: (B^T B + lambda * S) beta = B^T y
lam = 0.05
A = B.T @ B + lam * S
beta = np.linalg.solve(A, B.T @ y)

# Effective degrees of freedom: trace of the hat matrix
H = B @ np.linalg.solve(A, B.T)
edf = np.trace(H)
y_pred = B @ beta
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((y - y_pred)**2))

pred_pi2 = (build_basis(np.array([np.pi/2]), knots) @ beta)[0]
pred_3pi2 = (build_basis(np.array([3*np.pi/2]), knots) @ beta)[0]

print("Number of basis functions:", p)
print("Smoothing penalty lambda:", lam)
print("Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF):", round(float(edf), 2))
print("Fit RMSE:", round(float(rmse), 3))
print("Prediction at pi/2 (true=1.0):", round(float(pred_pi2), 3))
print("Prediction at 3pi/2 (true=-1.0):", round(float(pred_3pi2), 3))

Common pitfalls

  • Neglecting feature interactions: GAMs assume additivity by default; interacting features (e.g. temperature and humidity) require explicit 2D tensor-product smooths $f_{12}(x_1, x_2)$.
  • Unbounded extrapolation: polynomial splines can diverge sharply outside the boundary knots; natural cubic splines mitigate this by enforcing linear extrapolation in the tails.
  • Concurvity: the non-linear analogue of multicollinearity where one smooth curve can be approximated by a combination of other curves, destabilising parameter estimation.
  • Under-smoothing ($\lambda$ too small): insufficient penalisation causes the spline to overfit random noise spikes rather than the underlying generative signal.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary advantage of Generalised Additive Models (GAMs) over multi-layer neural networks or gradient boosting?

Why: Because GAMs are additive sums of smooth functions $f_j(x_j)$, each feature's marginal relationship with the target can be plotted on a 2D graph and directly audited.

Q2In penalised spline regression, what happens to the fitted curve as the smoothing parameter $\lambda \to \infty$?

Why: The roughness penalty penalises $\int [f''(t)]^2 dt$. As $\lambda \to \infty$, $f''(t)$ is forced to 0 for all $t$, which is the definition of a straight line.

Q3What does an Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF) value close to 1.0 indicate for a fitted smooth term?

Why: An EDF of 1 corresponds to a linear term with no curvature; higher EDF values (e.g. 3, 5, 8) represent increasing non-linear complexity and inflection points.

Q4What is "concurvity" in the context of Generalised Additive Models?

Why: Concurvity occurs when a smooth curve of one predictor can be approximated by smooth functions of other predictors, destabilising coefficient estimation just like collinearity does in linear models.
