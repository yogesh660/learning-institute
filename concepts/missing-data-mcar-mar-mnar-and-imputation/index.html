01 The idea

Blank cells and missing indicators occur across nearly every real-world dataset. Blindly dropping rows or imputing simple column means distorts feature distributions, collapses variance, and introduces severe sampling bias. Donald Rubin's taxonomy—Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), Missing at Random (MAR), and Missing Not at Random (MNAR)—governs whether missingness can be ignored, conditioned on observed covariates, or modelled as an informative signal.

Imagine administering a medical health survey measuring body weight and blood pressure. Suppose a lab technician accidentally drops five test tubes and their test results shatter on the floor. The missingness was caused by physical clumsiness completely unrelated to the patients' health or weight. This is Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): missing values are a random subsample of the true population. If you discard these rows (complete case analysis), your statistical estimators lose statistical power but remain unbiased.

Now suppose younger patients are far less likely to report their annual blood pressure because they visit doctors less frequently, but among people of the same age, missingness is unrelated to their actual blood pressure. This is Missing at Random (MAR): missingness depends systematically on an observed variable (age), but not on the unobserved value itself. Dropping rows here causes severe bias (your sample over-represents older people). However, if you condition on age (e.g. imputing blood pressure using age-stratified models or regression imputation), unbiased estimates are recovered.

Finally, suppose patients with severe clinical obesity deliberately skip the survey question asking for their weight. The probability that weight is missing depends directly on the unobserved weight itself. This is Missing Not at Random (MNAR). No standard imputation method that relies solely on observed data can fix this bias without explicitly modelling the censoring mechanism itself (e.g. Heckman selection models or adding explicit binary missingness indicator flags).

In everyday terms

Distinguishing between a phone call dropped because of random atmospheric static (MCAR), a call skipped because it rang during business hours (MAR), and a debtor refusing to answer calls from a debt-collection agency (MNAR).

02 Step by step

  1. Quantify missingness topography: calculate the percentage of missing values per column and plot missingness co-occurrence patterns.
  2. Diagnose the missingness mechanism: test whether missingness correlates with other observed features (ruling out MCAR via logistic regression or Little's test).
  3. Select an imputation strategy: use median or mode for quick baseline models, or iterative multivariate imputation (MICE / MissForest / KNN) for MAR data.
  4. Preserve uncertainty with Multiple Imputation: generate $M$ imputed datasets, fit models on each, and pool parameters via Rubin's rules to avoid underestimating standard errors.
  5. Encode missingness as a feature: append binary indicator columns ($\mathbf{1}_{x \text{ is missing}}$) to allow tree models to learn informative MNAR patterns.

03 The maths

  1. Rubin's formal missingness mechanisms

    $$P(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) = \begin{cases} P(M) & \text{MCAR} \\ P(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}) & \text{MAR} \\ P(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) & \text{MNAR} \end{cases}$$

    Here $M \in \{0, 1\}^n$ is the binary missingness indicator vector, $Y_{\text{obs}}$ are observed entries, and $Y_{\text{mis}}$ are missing values. Under MCAR, missingness is independent of all data. Under MAR, missingness depends only on observed covariates $Y_{\text{obs}}$. Under MNAR, missingness depends on the unobserved values $Y_{\text{mis}}$ themselves.

  2. Variance collapse under single mean imputation

    $$\mathbb{E}[s^2_{\text{imp}}] = \frac{n_{\text{obs}} - 1}{n - 1} \sigma^2 < \sigma^2$$

    Imputing missing values with the constant sample mean artificially pulls the empirical variance toward zero by factor $\frac{n_{\text{obs}} - 1}{n - 1}$. This deflation distorts confidence intervals and artificially inflates test statistics, generating false positive discoveries.

  3. Rubin's pooling rules for Multiple Imputation

    $$\bar{\theta} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{\theta}^{(m)}, \qquad T = \bar{W} + \Big(1 + \frac{1}{M}\Big) B, \quad \bar{W} = \frac{1}{M}\sum W_m, \quad B = \frac{1}{M-1}\sum (\hat{\theta}^{(m)} - \bar{\theta})^2$$

    Multiple Imputation draws $M$ plausible values for each missing cell to create $M$ complete datasets. The pooled parameter $\bar{\theta}$ averages point estimates, while total variance $T$ combines within-imputation variance $\bar{W}$ and between-imputation variance $B$, accurately propagating imputation uncertainty into downstream inferences.

04 Key takeaways

  • Rubin's classification: understanding whether missingness is MCAR, MAR, or MNAR dictates whether complete-case deletion or imputation is valid.
  • Variance deflation: single mean imputation artificially compresses variance and distorts covariance structures, inflating false positive rates.
  • Informative missingness: in tree-based machine learning, adding binary missing indicators ($\mathbf{1}_{\text{missing}}$) allows models to leverage MNAR signals directly.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 100
age = np.random.uniform(20, 60, n)
true_income = 20000 + 1200 * age + np.random.normal(0, 5000, n)

# Missingness depends on age: younger people less likely to report income (MAR)
prob_missing = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(0.15 * (age - 35)))
is_missing = np.random.binomial(1, prob_missing).astype(bool)

obs_income = true_income.copy()
obs_income[is_missing] = np.nan

mean_imputed = obs_income.copy()
mean_imputed[is_missing] = np.nanmean(obs_income)

poly = np.polyfit(age[~is_missing], obs_income[~is_missing], deg=1)
reg_imputed = obs_income.copy()
reg_imputed[is_missing] = np.polyval(poly, age[is_missing])

print(f"Missing records: {np.sum(is_missing)} / {n} ({np.sum(is_missing)/n*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"True population mean income:    {np.mean(true_income):.2f}")
print(f"Complete-case (dropped) mean:   {np.nanmean(obs_income):.2f} (Biased!)")
print(f"Mean imputed variance:          {np.var(mean_imputed):.2f} vs True: {np.var(true_income):.2f}")
print(f"Regression imputed mean income: {np.mean(reg_imputed):.2f} (Unbiased under MAR)")

Common pitfalls

  • Defaulting to dropna() without inspection: dropping missing rows under MAR or MNAR introduces severe sample truncation and survivor bias.
  • Imputing before train/test splitting: computing the mean or median over the entire dataset before splitting leaks test set statistics into training data.
  • Believing MAR means "at random": despite its confusing name, MAR is NOT random; missingness depends systematically on other observed features.

06 Check yourself

Q1In Rubin's missing data taxonomy, what distinguishes Missing at Random (MAR) from Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)?

Why: MCAR means missingness is pure random noise ($P(M \mid Y) = P(M)$). MAR means missingness depends systematically on other observed variables ($P(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}}, Y_{\text{mis}}) = P(M \mid Y_{\text{obs}})$); conditioning on those observed variables resolves the bias.

Q2Why is imputing missing values with a single column mean considered hazardous for subsequent hypothesis testing?

Why: Replacing varying unobserved points with an identical constant value clusters data around the mean, shrinking the estimated variance $\sigma^2$ and standard error, which leads to artificially small p-values and inflated Type I error rates.

Q3When evaluating a machine learning model, why must imputation transformers (like median or KNN imputers) be fitted strictly on the training split?

Why: If you compute the imputation statistic (e.g. mean or median) across the entire dataset before train/test splitting, information from test labels and features leaks into the training pipeline, leading to over-optimistic performance estimates.
