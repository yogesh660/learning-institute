01 The idea

Evaluating time-series forecasts requires fundamentally different metrics than standard cross-sectional regression. Traditional percentage errors like MAPE explode or divide by zero when demand is intermittent, while scale-dependent metrics like RMSE penalize high-volume series while ignoring slow-moving items. Scale-independent metrics such as MASE, sMAPE, and WAPE provide robust, business-interpretable evaluations across massive forecasting hierarchies.

Imagine managing inventory for a national supermarket chain with 50,000 distinct items. For high-velocity goods like two-litre milk bottles, a single store sells 5,000 units weekly; your forecast of 4,800 units is off by 200 units, representing an error of 4%. For slow-moving specialty items like saffron or organic truffle oil, the store sells 1 unit every other week (average 0.5 units). If your model predicts 0 units when 1 unit is sold, the absolute error is just 1 unit, but the classic Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) is 100%. If actual sales are 0 and the model predicts 1, MAPE divides by zero and becomes mathematically undefined! Averaging MAPE across 50,000 items produces an infinite or completely distorted score dominated by slow movers.

To resolve scale disparities without dividing by zero, Rob Hyndman and Anne Koehler introduced Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE). MASE divides the model's test Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by the in-sample MAE of a naive baseline (predicting tomorrow using yesterday's actuals on historical training data). If MASE is less than 1.0, the forecast outperforms a trivial random-walk persistence baseline; if MASE exceeds 1.0, the forecast is literally worse than doing nothing. Because the scaling denominator comes from the training series, MASE never divides by zero on non-constant series and remains directly comparable across high-volume and low-volume products.

For supply chain executives and inventory planners, aggregate financial discrepancies matter more than unweighted item percentages. Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE) sums the absolute forecast errors across all time steps and items and divides by the total actual sales volume. Unlike unweighted MAPE, WAPE cannot divide by zero at individual item timestamps, and it automatically weights errors in proportion to each product's sales volume.

In everyday terms

Scoring a golfer relative to par on different courses (MASE) rather than counting raw strokes, or measuring an entire company's budget accuracy by total revenue discrepancy (WAPE) rather than averaging departmental percentage errors.

02 Step by step

  1. Audit series for zero demand: identify sparsity, intermittency, or zeros that rule out standard percentage metrics like MAPE.
  2. Establish naive baseline benchmark: compute the in-sample Mean Absolute Error of a one-step persistence forecast on training history.
  3. Compute scale-free MASE: divide forecast test MAE by the training naive benchmark MAE to measure relative skill.
  4. Calculate portfolio WAPE: sum absolute errors across all time points and SKUs, dividing by total actual volume to obtain a volume-weighted percentage error.
  5. Inspect directional bias: calculate mean error or sMAPE to ensure the model does not systematically over-forecast or under-forecast demand.

03 The maths

  1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE)

    \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{t=1}^H |y_t - \hat{y}_t|, \qquad \text{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^H |y_t - \hat{y}_t|}{\sum_{t=1}^H y_t}

    Where $H$ is the forecast horizon, $y_t$ is actual demand, and $\hat{y}_t$ is the forecast. WAPE aggregates errors across all time points and items before dividing by total volume, avoiding zero-division singularities and weighting errors by volume.

  2. Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE)

    \text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{t=1}^H |y_t - \hat{y}_t|}{\frac{1}{T-1} \sum_{t=2}^T |y_t - y_{t-1}|}

    Where the denominator is the in-sample Mean Absolute Error of the naive persistence forecast computed across training history of length $T$. A value $\text{MASE} < 1$ indicates that the forecast outperforms the trivial persistence baseline.

  3. Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE)

    \text{sMAPE} = \frac{100\%}{H} \sum_{t=1}^H \frac{|y_t - \hat{y}_t|}{(|y_t| + |\hat{y}_t|) / 2}

    sMAPE bounds percentage errors between $0\%$ and $200\%$ by normalising by the average of actual and predicted values, though it penalises under-forecasting more heavily than over-forecasting.

04 Key takeaways

  • MASE normalises error by the in-sample naive persistence baseline, enabling unbiased comparisons across series with radically different scales.
  • WAPE weights errors by volume, providing a robust aggregate percentage metric that never divides by zero on intermittent demand.
  • Standard MAPE fails on intermittent demand series where actual values equal zero, producing infinities and heavily skewed portfolio summaries.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

def mae(actual, pred):
    return np.mean(np.abs(actual - pred))

def wape(actual, pred):
    return np.sum(np.abs(actual - pred)) / np.sum(actual)

def mase(actual, pred, train_history):
    naive_mae = np.mean(np.abs(np.diff(train_history)))
    return mae(actual, pred) / naive_mae

def smape(actual, pred):
    denom = (np.abs(actual) + np.abs(pred)) / 2.0
    return np.mean(np.where(denom == 0, 0, np.abs(actual - pred) / denom)) * 100

# Realistic intermittent retail demand series
train_history = np.array([12, 15, 11, 14, 0, 8, 10, 13, 16, 12, 14, 15], dtype=float)
y_actual = np.array([14, 0, 9, 12, 15, 0], dtype=float)
y_pred = np.array([13, 2, 10, 11, 14, 1], dtype=float)

print(f"MAE:   {mae(y_actual, y_pred):.2f} units")
print(f"WAPE:  {wape(y_actual, y_pred):.1%} of total volume")
print(f"MASE:  {mase(y_actual, y_pred, train_history):.3f} (< 1 beats naive baseline)")
print(f"sMAPE: {smape(y_actual, y_pred):.1f}%")

Common pitfalls

  • Using traditional MAPE on datasets with zero actual values, resulting in division by zero or explosive values.
  • Computing the MASE naive benchmark denominator on the test set instead of the historical training set.
  • Overlooking sMAPE directional asymmetry: predicting 0 when actual is 100 yields 200% error, whereas predicting 200 when actual is 100 yields only 66.7% error.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE) preferred over Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) when evaluating intermittent demand forecasts with frequent zero sales?

Why: MAPE has actual demand in its denominator, creating division-by-zero singularities whenever sales are 0. MASE scales by the historical naive baseline error, avoiding zero division on non-trivial series.

Q2A retail demand forecasting model achieves a MASE of 0.65 on a holdout test horizon. How should an analyst interpret this result?

Why: A MASE below 1.0 indicates superior performance relative to the one-step naive baseline; a score of 0.65 means the model's error is 65% of the naive baseline error (a 35% error reduction).

Q3In supply chain forecasting across multiple products, why is Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE) often preferred over unweighted portfolio MAPE?

Why: Unweighted MAPE gives equal importance to an item selling 1 unit and an item selling 10,000 units. WAPE sums errors across all items before dividing by total volume, accurately reflecting overall business impact.
