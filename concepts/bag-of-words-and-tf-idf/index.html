Bag of words and TF-IDF

The Bag of Words (BoW) model represents text documents as fixed-length numeric vectors of token counts, discarding grammatical syntax and word order while preserving vocabulary frequency statistics. Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF, Karen Spärck Jones, 1972) refines raw counts by multiplying local Term Frequency ($\text{tf}(t, d)$) by the global Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{idf}(t, D) = \log\frac{N}{|\{d \in D : t \in d\}|}$), systematically damping ubiquitous generic words while elevating informative, domain-specific keywords. Evaluated with cosine similarity over $L_2$-normalised sparse vectors, TF-IDF powers classical document retrieval, topic clustering, and linear text classification.

01 The idea

How can an algorithm determine whether a news article discusses astrophysics, international diplomacy, or organic chemistry when computers process only numbers? The simplest representation is to treat a document as a literal bag of words: toss all the words into a sack, shake away grammatical word order, and tally how many times each dictionary term appears. Yet raw word counts deceive: the word "the" might appear fifty times in every document, while the critical distinguishing word "supernova" appears only three times. TF-IDF resolves this disparity mathematically, weighting words by how uniquely informative they are across the entire document corpus.

The Bag of Words vector space model: Given a vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ of $V$ unique words, any document $d$ can be mapped to a $V$-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x} = [c_1, c_2, \dots, c_V]$, where $c_i$ is the count of occurrences of word $w_i$ in document $d$. This representation possesses two profound properties: (1) it transforms arbitrary-length text into fixed-length mathematical vectors that can be fed directly to linear classifiers, support vector machines, or clustering algorithms; and (2) it discards word order completely ("dog bites man" and "man bites dog" yield identical vectors). Despite this radical simplification, word frequencies capture strong topical semantics.

The limitation of raw term frequencies: If you compute Euclidean distance or dot products between raw count vectors, two flaws arise: (1) Longer documents have larger counts simply because they contain more words, creating artificial bias; and (2) Common words that appear everywhere ("said", "year", "people") dominate the dot product, drowning out rare, highly specific keywords ("mitochondria", "amortisation").

Inverse Document Frequency as information content: In 1972, British computer scientist Karen Spärck Jones introduced Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) based on information theory. If a word $t$ appears in every single document across a corpus ($|\{d : t \in d\}| = N$), it carries zero discriminative information: $\log(N / N) = \log(1) = 0$. Conversely, if a word appears in only two documents out of ten thousand, it is a rare, highly specialized topic descriptor, producing a large positive IDF score. Multiplying Term Frequency by Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{TF} \times \text{IDF}$) scales each word's weight by its uniqueness.

Cosine similarity over normalised sparse vectors: Because document vocabularies span tens of thousands of words, TF-IDF vectors are extremely sparse (over 99% of entries are zeros). Normalising each vector to unit Euclidean norm ($L_2$ normalisation) eliminates document length bias: dividing by vector magnitude ensures that a short 100-word essay and a 1,000-word technical report on the same topic map to identical directions in vector space. The inner dot product between two $L_2$-normalised vectors equals their cosine similarity $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|} \in [0, 1]$, providing a fast, scale-invariant metric for search engine document ranking.

In everyday terms

Imagine you are an investigator trying to identify which international culinary cuisine a mystery recipe belongs to. If you simply count raw ingredient quantities (Bag of Words), the recipe lists "4 cups water, 2 tablespoons salt, 1 tablespoon oil". These high-frequency ingredients make up 90% of the bowl, but water and salt appear in nearly every kitchen on Earth: they tell you nothing about whether the dish is French, Indian, or Mexican. Term Frequency is how much of an ingredient is in the bowl. Inverse Document Frequency is like consulting a global culinary spice pantry: salt is in 100% of pantries (IDF near zero); olive oil is in 40% (modest IDF); but "garam masala" is found in only 3% of pantries, and "fenugreek" in only 1%. Multiplying quantity by spice rarity immediately highlights the teaspoon of fenugreek as the true signature of the dish, correctly identifying the cuisine in seconds.

03 The maths

  1. Smooth Inverse Document Frequency (scikit-learn standard)

    \operatorname{idf}(t, D) = \log\left(\frac{1 + N}{1 + \operatorname{df}(t)}\right) + 1

    Here $N = |D|$ is the total number of documents in the corpus, and $\operatorname{df}(t) = |\{d \in D : t \in d\}|$ is the number of documents containing term $t$. The additive smoothing $+1$ ensures that words present in all documents do not receive a weight of zero.

  2. TF-IDF term weight calculation

    \operatorname{tfidf}(t, d, D) = \operatorname{tf}(t, d) \cdot \operatorname{idf}(t, D)

    Term frequency $\operatorname{tf}(t, d)$ rewards words that appear frequently within document $d$, while $\operatorname{idf}(t, D)$ penalises words that appear commonly across the entire collection $D$, maximising the weight of domain-specific keywords.

  3. Cosine similarity over L2-normalised vectors

    \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \|\mathbf{y}\|_2} = \sum_{t \in \mathcal{V}} \tilde{x}_t \cdot \tilde{y}_t \in [0, 1]

    Because TF-IDF entries are strictly non-negative ($x_t \ge 0$), the cosine of the angle between two document vectors is bounded in $[0, 1]$. An angle of $0^\circ$ ($\cos=1$) denotes identical relative term distributions, while an angle of $90^\circ$ ($\cos=0$) indicates completely disjoint vocabularies.

04 Key takeaways

  • The Bag of Words model represents text as fixed-length vectors of token counts, discarding grammatical syntax and word order.
  • TF-IDF multiplies local Term Frequency by global Inverse Document Frequency to elevate uniquely informative keywords.
  • Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) penalises ubiquitous terms, automatically dampening function words like "the" and "is".
  • $L_2$ vector normalisation eliminates document length bias, allowing fair comparison between short abstracts and long treatises.
  • Cosine similarity over normalised sparse TF-IDF vectors powers classical search ranking, document clustering, and text classification.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import re

# Small corpus of 4 documents across two distinct topics (machine learning vs cooking)
corpus = [
    "Machine learning models require training data and algorithms.",
    "Deep neural networks learn representations from training data.",
    "The chef prepared a delicious pasta recipe with garlic sauce.",
    "Italian pasta dishes require garlic, olive oil, and fresh herbs."
]

# 1. Build Vocabulary
def tokenize(text):
    return [w for w in re.split(r'\W+', text.lower()) if len(w) > 2]

tokenized_corpus = [tokenize(doc) for doc in corpus]
vocab = sorted(list(set(w for doc in tokenized_corpus for w in doc)))
word_to_idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
N = len(corpus)
V = len(vocab)

print(f"Corpus size: {N} documents")
print(f"Vocabulary size: {V} unique terms")

# 2. Compute Count Matrix (Bag of Words)
count_matrix = np.zeros((N, V), dtype=np.float32)
for doc_idx, tokens in enumerate(tokenized_corpus):
    for tok in tokens:
        if tok in word_to_idx:
            count_matrix[doc_idx, word_to_idx[tok]] += 1.0

# 3. Compute Smooth Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)
# idf(t) = log((1 + N) / (1 + df(t))) + 1
doc_freq = np.sum(count_matrix > 0, axis=0)  # number of documents containing term
smooth_idf = np.log((1.0 + N) / (1.0 + doc_freq)) + 1.0

# 4. Compute TF-IDF Matrix and L2 Normalisation
tf_matrix = count_matrix  # using raw count TF
tfidf = tf_matrix * smooth_idf

# L2 vector normalisation per document row
row_norms = np.linalg.norm(tfidf, axis=1, keepdims=True)
tfidf_norm = tfidf / np.maximum(row_norms, 1e-8)

print("\nSample IDF weights:")
for sample_word in ["training", "garlic", "pasta", "data"]:
    w_idx = word_to_idx[sample_word]
    print(f"  '{sample_word:<10}': df={doc_freq[w_idx]:.0f}/{N}, IDF={smooth_idf[w_idx]:.4f}")

# 5. Information Retrieval Query Matching via Cosine Similarity
query = "pasta recipe garlic"
q_tokens = tokenize(query)
q_counts = np.zeros(V, dtype=np.float32)
for tok in q_tokens:
    if tok in word_to_idx:
        q_counts[word_to_idx[tok]] += 1.0

q_tfidf = q_counts * smooth_idf
q_norm = q_tfidf / np.maximum(np.linalg.norm(q_tfidf), 1e-8)

# Cosine similarity: dot product of normalized vectors
similarities = tfidf_norm @ q_norm

print(f"\nQuery: '{query}'")
print("Document relevance rankings (Cosine Similarity):")
ranked_indices = np.argsort(similarities)[::-1]
for rank, idx in enumerate(ranked_indices):
    print(f"  Rank {rank+1} (Score: {similarities[idx]:.4f}): \"{corpus[idx]}\"")

assert ranked_indices[0] == 2, "Top ranked document should be doc 3 (pasta recipe)!"
print("\nVerified: TF-IDF vectorisation and cosine similarity ranking executed successfully!")

Common pitfalls

  • Ignoring vocabulary out-of-vocabulary (OOV) terms at test time: Words in test documents that never appeared in the training corpus have undefined IDF weights and must be ignored or mapped to an unknown token.
  • Fitting TF-IDF on test data (data leakage): Computing IDF on the combination of training and test sets leaks global corpus statistics; always fit the TfidfVectorizer strictly on training data and transform test data.
  • Failing to apply sub-linear TF scaling: Raw count TF assumes that twenty mentions of a word imply twenty times more importance than a single mention; sub-linear scaling ($1 + \log(\text{tf})$) mitigates this distortion.
  • Dense array conversion memory blowup: Storing TF-IDF matrices as dense NumPy arrays ($N \times V$) consumes tens of gigabytes of RAM; always maintain representations as scipy.sparse CSR matrices.
  • Polysemy and synonymy blindness: TF-IDF treats "sofa" and "couch" as completely orthogonal dimensions (synonymy), and cannot distinguish between "bank" of a river and financial "bank" (polysemy); dense neural embeddings resolve these semantic gaps.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) take the logarithm of inverse document frequency ($\log\frac{N}{\operatorname{df}(t)}$)?

Why: Linear inverse document frequency ($N / \operatorname{df}$) would scale by millions for rare typos or hapax legomena, swamping all other words. The logarithmic damping scales rarity gracefully according to information theory.

Q2What is the primary purpose of applying $L_2$ normalisation to document TF-IDF vectors?

Why: Long documents naturally contain higher word counts purely by virtue of length. $L_2$ normalisation projects each vector onto the unit hypersphere, making cosine similarity sensitive purely to relative word distributions rather than absolute volume.

Q3What classic semantic limitation does the Bag of Words model suffer from due to discarding word sequence order?

Why: Because the Bag of Words representation pools all word counts into a single histogram, all syntactic order, grammatical roles, and phrase compositions are discarded, making permutations of the same words indistinguishable.

Q4Why is it critical to compute IDF weights strictly on the training set and avoid fitting on test data?

Why: IDF reflects global corpus statistics. If test documents are included when calculating document frequencies, the model gains illicit foresight into the test distribution (data leakage). Preprocessors must be fit exclusively on training data.
