01 The idea

Probability quantifies uncertainty on a principled scale from 0 to 1. Bayes' theorem provides the mathematical machinery to invert conditional probabilities, updating prior beliefs into posterior distributions in the light of fresh empirical data.

Suppose a clinic screens 1,000 patients for a rare disease affecting 1% of the population (10 people). A diagnostic test boasts a 90% true positive rate (detecting 9 of the 10 sick patients) and an 8% false positive rate (falsely flagging roughly 79 of the 990 healthy patients). If a patient tests positive, what is the chance they actually have the disease? Most people guess 90%, yet the actual probability is only 9 out of (9 + 79) = 88 positive tests, which is roughly 10.2%.

This counter-intuitive outcome is known as the base rate fallacy. Because healthy individuals vastly outnumber sick individuals, even a modest false positive rate creates far more false alarms than true cases. To compute the probability of disease after seeing a positive test, you cannot evaluate the test in a vacuum; you must weigh the likelihood of the test against the prior prevalence of the disease.

This exact reasoning powers probabilistic machine learning. A spam filter assessing an email does not merely check how often the word "lottery" appears in junk mail; it balances that evidence against the base rate of spam in your inbox. In models like Gaussian Naive Bayes and Bayesian inference, Bayes' theorem is the formal engine that turns likelihoods into actionable class probabilities.

In everyday terms

Updating beliefs with Bayes' theorem is like spotting dark clouds: they strongly indicate rain in Manchester, but rarely mean rain in the Atacama desert.

02 Step by step

  1. Define the sample space $\Omega$ and assign valid prior probabilities $P(A_k)$ that sum to 1 over all mutually exclusive outcomes.
  2. Calculate conditional probabilities using the multiplication rule: $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B)$.
  3. Sum across all mutually exclusive scenarios via the law of total probability to establish the marginal evidence $P(B)$.
  4. Invert the conditional direction with Bayes' theorem: multiply the prior $P(A)$ by the likelihood $P(B \mid A)$ and divide by $P(B)$.
  5. Use the resulting posterior distribution $P(A \mid B)$ to drive decision thresholds or serve as the prior for future observations.

05 The maths

  1. Probability Axioms and Fundamental Rules

    $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B), \qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B)$$

    Kolmogorov's axioms require probabilities to be non-negative, bounded in $[0, 1]$, and sum to 1 over the sample space. The addition rule subtracts the intersection $P(A \cap B)$ so overlapping events are not counted twice. When events are mutually exclusive, $P(A \cap B) = 0$. When events are statistically independent, conditional conditioning vanishes: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, reducing the intersection to $P(A)P(B)$.

  2. The Law of Total Probability

    $$P(B) = \sum_{k=1}^K P(B \mid A_k)P(A_k)$$

    If the events $\{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_K\}$ form a partition of the sample space (meaning they are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, $\bigcup_k A_k = \Omega$), the total marginal probability of any event $B$ is the weighted average of its conditional probabilities across all possible states $A_k$.

  3. Bayes' Theorem

    $$P(A_j \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_j)P(A_j)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A_j)P(A_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^K P(B \mid A_k)P(A_k)}$$

    Bayes' theorem translates the prior probability $P(A_j)$ into the posterior probability $P(A_j \mid B)$ by multiplying by the likelihood $P(B \mid A_j)$ and normalising by the total probability of the evidence $P(B)$. It allows us to infer hidden causes from observed effects.

06 Key takeaways

  • Conditional probability $P(A \mid B)$ is mathematically distinct from $P(B \mid A)$; confusing them is the prosecutor's fallacy.
  • A rare prior requires overwhelming likelihood evidence to produce a convincing posterior probability.
  • Statistical independence is a property of probabilities ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$), not of physical separation.
  • Bayes' theorem is the foundation for updating models sequentially as streaming batches of data arrive.

07 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Medical screening base-rate calculation
prior_disease = 0.01          # P(D) = 1%
prior_healthy = 1.0 - prior_disease  # P(~D) = 99%

# Test characteristics: sensitivity and false positive rate
sensitivity = 0.90            # P(+ | D) = 90%
false_positive_rate = 0.08    # P(+ | ~D) = 8%

# Total evidence: Law of total probability
p_positive = (sensitivity * prior_disease) + (false_positive_rate * prior_healthy)

# Bayes' theorem: Posterior P(D | +)
posterior = (sensitivity * prior_disease) / p_positive
print(f"P(Positive Test) = {p_positive:.4f}")
print(f"P(Disease | Positive) = {posterior:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Base rate neglect: evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of a classifier or test while ignoring class imbalance.
  • Assuming events are independent when latent confounding factors or serial correlations link them together.
  • Confusing mutually exclusive events ($P(A \cap B) = 0$) with independent events ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$); mutually exclusive events with non-zero probability can never be independent.
  • Forgetting to normalise by the denominator $P(B)$, which is essential whenever absolute posterior probabilities are required.

08 Check yourself

Q1A rare condition has a prevalence of 0.1%. A test has 99% sensitivity and a 2% false positive rate. If someone tests positive, their posterior probability of having the condition is closest to:

Why: $P(+) = (0.99 \times 0.001) + (0.02 \times 0.999) = 0.00099 + 0.01998 = 0.02097$. The posterior $P(\text{condition} \mid +) = 0.00099 / 0.02097 \approx 0.0472$ (or 4.7%).

Q2Two events $A$ and $B$ both have probability 0.5 and are mutually exclusive. What is $P(A \cap B)$?

Why: Mutually exclusive events cannot occur simultaneously by definition, so their intersection is the empty set with probability 0.

Q3Under what condition does $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$?

Why: By definition of statistical independence, knowledge that $B$ has occurred provides zero information about the occurrence of $A$.

Q4Why does the denominator in Bayes' theorem sum over all hypotheses $A_k$?

Why: The denominator is the marginal probability of the evidence across the full partition, acting as the normalising constant so the posteriors form a valid probability distribution.

Q5If $P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.5$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.1$, what is the union probability $P(A \cup B)$?

Why: The addition rule gives $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.4 + 0.5 - 0.1 = 0.8$. Simply adding $0.4 + 0.5$ would double-count the intersection.

Q6Can two events $A$ and $B$ with strictly positive probabilities ($P(A) > 0, P(B) > 0$) be simultaneously mutually exclusive and statistically independent?

Why: Mutual exclusivity requires $P(A \cap B) = 0$. Statistical independence requires $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Because both probabilities are strictly positive, their product is positive, so both conditions cannot hold at once.

Q7A factory uses two assembly lines. Line 1 produces 60% of all parts with a defect rate of 2%, while Line 2 produces 40% with a defect rate of 5%. What is the overall probability that a randomly chosen part is defective?

Why: By the law of total probability, $P(D) = P(D \mid L_1)P(L_1) + P(D \mid L_2)P(L_2) = (0.02 \times 0.60) + (0.05 \times 0.40) = 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.032$ (or 3.2%). Averaging 2% and 5% ignores line volume.

Q8A diagnostic test runs in code with prior = 0.20, likelihood = 0.80, and marginal evidence p_evidence = 0.40. Evaluating (likelihood * prior) / p_evidence produces:

Why: Applying Bayes' theorem directly: $(0.80 \times 0.20) / 0.40 = 0.16 / 0.40 = 0.40$.

Q9In a medical screening scenario with a very rare disease (prevalence 0.1%), why does cutting the false-positive rate from 8% to 1% increase posterior disease probability far more than raising sensitivity from 90% to 99%?

Why: When 99.9% of people are healthy, even a modest false-positive rate generates dozens of false alarms that swamp the few genuine positive cases. Reducing false alarms directly cuts the dominant term in the denominator.

Q10On the factory lines (Line 1: 60% volume, 2% defects; Line 2: 40% volume, 5% defects; overall defect rate 3.2%), an inspected part turns out to be defective. What is the posterior probability that it came from Line 2?

Why: Bayes' inversion gives $P(L_2 \mid D) = P(D \mid L_2)P(L_2) / P(D) = (0.05 \times 0.40) / 0.032 = 0.020 / 0.032 = 20/32 = 0.625$ (62.5%). Even with lower output volume, Line 2's higher defect rate produces the majority of defects.

Q11A patient tests positive on a test with 10% disease prior, 80% sensitivity, and 10% false-positive rate. A second, independent test from an external laboratory with identical characteristics also returns positive. What is the posterior probability after both positive tests?

Why: After one test, the posterior is $(0.8 \times 0.1) / (0.8 \times 0.1 + 0.1 \times 0.9) = 0.08 / 0.17 = 8/17 \approx 47.1\%$. With independent errors, the joint likelihoods are $0.8^2 \times 0.10 = 0.064$ for disease and $0.1^2 \times 0.90 = 0.009$ for healthy, giving $0.064 / (0.064 + 0.009) = 64/73 \approx 87.7\%$.

Q12Confusing the probability of evidence given an innocence hypothesis, $P(E \mid I)$, with the probability of innocence given that evidence, $P(I \mid E)$, is an error known as:

Why: The prosecutor's fallacy equates the probability of innocent evidence $P(E \mid I)$ with the posterior $P(I \mid E)$ by neglecting the prior probability of guilt or innocence.
