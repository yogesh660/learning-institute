Propensity scores and matching

Propensity score methods estimate causal treatment effects from observational data by addressing non-random treatment assignment and covariate imbalance. The propensity score e(\mathbf{x}) = P(D = 1 | \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}) acts as a balancing score: conditional on e(\mathbf{x}), the distribution of measured baseline covariates \mathbf{X} is statistically independent of treatment assignment D. By pairing, stratifying, or weighting units via Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW), propensity methods emulate randomised controlled trials, isolating the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) and Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) under the assumption of unconfoundedness.

01 The idea

Suppose you want to know whether a premium customer subscription increases annual spending. In observational logs, customers who purchase the subscription already spend vastly more than non-subscribers before ever buying the membership. Comparing raw averages is wildly confounded by selection bias. Randomised controlled trials are often impossible, unethical, or prohibitively expensive. How can we synthesise a fair "apples-to-apples" comparison from observational data? Propensity score matching creates balanced synthetic cohorts, pairing each subscriber with an identical non-subscriber who had the exact same propensity to subscribe.

The Balancing Score Property (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983): If you have 50 baseline covariates (age, income, browsing frequency, geography), matching on all 50 dimensions simultaneously suffers from the curse of dimensionality—no two humans are identical across 50 features. Rosenbaum and Rubin proved a remarkable theorem: you do not need to match on all 50 covariates. You only need to match on a single 1D scalar: the propensity score e(x) = P(D=1 | X=x)! Conditional on having the same probability of receiving treatment, the distribution of all 50 covariates is balanced between treated and control units.

Common Support and Positivity: For a valid causal comparison, there must be "common support" (positivity: 0 < e(x) < 1). If certain customers have a 100% probability of subscribing (e.g. enterprise accounts), no comparable control unit exists. Units outside the common support overlap region must be trimmed.

Matching vs Weighting (IPW): - Propensity Score Matching (PSM): For each treated unit, find the nearest control unit with the closest propensity score (e.g. within a caliper of 0.05). - Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW): Keep all data, but weight each unit by the inverse probability of receiving their actual treatment: w_i = D_i / e(x_i) + (1 - D_i) / (1 - e(x_i)). This upweights under-represented units to create a balanced pseudo-population.

In everyday terms

Imagine comparing the driving performance of sports car owners versus minivan drivers. Sports car owners might speed more, but is it the car or the fact that sports car owners are younger and more aggressive? A propensity score model calculates each driver's probability of buying a sports car based on age, income, and lifestyle. Matching then pairs a 28-year-old sports car owner with a 28-year-old minivan driver who had the exact same 70% probability of buying a sports car but happened to buy a minivan instead. Comparing these matched twins isolates the true causal effect of the vehicle.

03 The maths

  1. The Propensity Score Balancing Property

    e(\mathbf{x}) \equiv P(D = 1 \mid \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}), \qquad \mathbf{X} \perp D \mid e(\mathbf{x})

    The propensity score is the coarsest balancing score. Conditioning on e(\mathbf{x}) renders the multi-dimensional covariate vector \mathbf{X} statistically independent of treatment assignment D, removing all observable confounding.

  2. Unconfoundedness (Conditional Independence Assumption)

    \big(Y(1), Y(0)\big) \perp D \mid \mathbf{X} \implies \big(Y(1), Y(0)\big) \perp D \mid e(\mathbf{x})

    If potential outcomes (Y(1), Y(0)) are independent of treatment conditional on \mathbf{X} (no unmeasured confounders), then conditional independence holds given the one-dimensional propensity score e(\mathbf{x}).

  3. Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) Estimator for ATE

    \hat{\tau}_{\text{ATE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \frac{D_i Y_i}{\hat{e}(\mathbf{x}_i)} - \frac{(1 - D_i) Y_i}{1 - \hat{e}(\mathbf{x}_i)} \right)

    IPW reweights the sample so that both treated and control groups reflect the covariate distribution of the combined population. Treated units with low propensity and control units with high propensity receive higher weights.

04 Key takeaways

  • The propensity score e(x) collapses high-dimensional baseline covariates into a 1D scalar balancing score.
  • Conditional on the propensity score, treatment assignment is independent of measured baseline covariates: X \perp D | e(x).
  • Propensity score matching creates balanced synthetic cohorts, isolating true causal effects from observational selection bias.
  • Standardised Mean Difference (SMD) < 0.10 confirms that matched treatment and control groups achieve satisfactory covariate balance.

05 In code

python
"""
Propensity Score Matching (PSM) vs Naive Comparison
Demonstrating confounding correction, covariate balance diagnostics,
and nearest-neighbour caliper matching on observational data.
"""

import numpy as np

# Simulate observational customer study
# True Causal Effect of Premium Membership on Annual Spend = £50.00
np.random.seed(42)
N = 1000

# Confounders: Age and Prior Year Spending (in £)
age = np.random.normal(38.0, 8.0, N)
prior_spend = np.random.normal(300.0, 80.0, N)

# Treatment assignment D (Premium Membership): Older and higher-spending users are far more likely to buy
# Logit propensity = -6.0 + 0.08 * age + 0.012 * prior_spend
logit_prop = -6.0 + 0.08 * age + 0.012 * prior_spend
true_propensity = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-logit_prop))
membership = np.random.binomial(1, true_propensity)

# Observed Outcome Y (Annual Spend in £):
# True effect is +£50, but prior spend and age strongly drive spending
true_effect = 50.0
annual_spend = 100.0 + true_effect * membership + 1.2 * prior_spend + 2.0 * age + np.random.normal(0, 25, N)

# 1. Naive Comparison (Raw Difference in Means)
naive_treated_mean = np.mean(annual_spend[membership == 1])
naive_control_mean = np.mean(annual_spend[membership == 0])
naive_diff = naive_treated_mean - naive_control_mean

print("=== Observational Data Summary ===")
print(f"Total Customers: {N} | Treated (Members): {np.sum(membership)} | Control: {N - np.sum(membership)}")
print(f"Naive Observed Difference: £{naive_diff:.2f} (True Causal Effect is £{true_effect:.2f} -> Massively Overestimated!)")

# 2. Estimate Propensity Scores via Logistic Regression
# Design matrix
X_cov = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), age, prior_spend])
# Quick Newton-Raphson logistic fit
beta_prop = np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(15):
    p_hat = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(X_cov @ beta_prop, -30, 30)))
    w = np.maximum(p_hat * (1 - p_hat), 1e-10)
    grad = X_cov.T @ (membership - p_hat)
    hess = -X_cov.T @ (w[:, np.newaxis] * X_cov)
    beta_prop += np.linalg.solve(-hess, grad)

est_propensity = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(X_cov @ beta_prop, -30, 30)))

# 3. Nearest-Neighbour Caliper Matching (1:1 with replacement)
treated_indices = np.where(membership == 1)[0]
control_indices = np.where(membership == 0)[0]
caliper = 0.05

matched_pairs = []
for t_idx in treated_indices:
    p_t = est_propensity[t_idx]
    diffs = np.abs(est_propensity[control_indices] - p_t)
    best_c_idx = control_indices[np.argmin(diffs)]
    if diffs[np.argmin(diffs)] <= caliper:
        matched_pairs.append((t_idx, best_c_idx))

matched_t = [pair[0] for pair in matched_pairs]
matched_c = [pair[1] for pair in matched_pairs]

# 4. Covariate Balance Check: Standardised Mean Difference (SMD)
def calc_smd(feat, treat_mask):
    m1, m0 = np.mean(feat[treat_mask]), np.mean(feat[~treat_mask])
    v1, v0 = np.var(feat[treat_mask]), np.var(feat[~treat_mask])
    return (m1 - m0) / np.sqrt((v1 + v0) / 2.0)

smd_age_before = calc_smd(age, membership == 1)
smd_spend_before = calc_smd(prior_spend, membership == 1)

smd_age_after = (np.mean(age[matched_t]) - np.mean(age[matched_c])) / np.std(age)
smd_spend_after = (np.mean(prior_spend[matched_t]) - np.mean(prior_spend[matched_c])) / np.std(prior_spend)

print("\n=== Covariate Balance Diagnostics ===")
print(f"Age SMD:         Before Matching = {smd_age_before:+.3f} | After Matching = {smd_age_after:+.3f} (Balanced!)")
print(f"Prior Spend SMD: Before Matching = {smd_spend_before:+.3f} | After Matching = {smd_spend_after:+.3f} (Balanced!)")

# 5. Treatment Effect on Matched Sample (ATT)
matched_diff = np.mean(annual_spend[matched_t]) - np.mean(annual_spend[matched_c])
print(f"\nMatched Cohort Size: {len(matched_pairs)} pairs")
print(f"Propensity Matched ATT Estimate: £{matched_diff:.2f} (True: £{true_effect:.2f} -> Causal Effect Recovered!)")

Common pitfalls

  • Unmeasured confounding: Propensity scores only balance measured covariates; if an unobserved variable influences both treatment and outcome, estimates remain confounded.
  • Violating common support: Including treated units with extreme propensity scores that have zero counterpart controls forces dangerous extrapolation.
  • Extreme weights in IPW: When propensity scores approach 0 or 1, inverse probability weights explode, causing extreme variance; weight trimming or stabilised weights are required.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary theorem proved by Rosenbaum and Rubin regarding propensity scores?

Why: Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983) proved the balancing score property: adjusting for the 1D propensity score balances all underlying covariates simultaneously, overcoming the curse of dimensionality.

Q2What threshold of Standardised Mean Difference (SMD) is widely accepted as indicating satisfactory covariate balance after matching?

Why: An SMD below 0.10 indicates that the difference in covariate means between treated and control cohorts is negligible, mirroring a randomised trial.

Q3Why must units outside the common support overlap region be trimmed in propensity score analysis?

Why: The positivity assumption requires 0 < P(D=1 | X) < 1. If a unit has a propensity of 1.0, there are no comparable controls, making counterfactual comparison impossible without speculative extrapolation.
