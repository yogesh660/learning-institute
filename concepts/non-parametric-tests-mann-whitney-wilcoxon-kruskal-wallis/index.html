01 The idea

When data violates normality through heavy skewness, severe outliers or ordinal scales, classical t-tests and ANOVA produce unreliable p-values and lose statistical power. Non-parametric rank tests discard absolute numerical values in favour of relative order, providing exact, distribution-free hypothesis tests. The Mann–Whitney U test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and Kruskal–Wallis H-test form the complete non-parametric counterpart to two-sample, paired, and multi-group parametric inference.

Consider measuring web service response times. Out of 35 requests, 34 complete in 40 to 90 milliseconds, but one request suffers a database lock timeout and hangs for 45,000 milliseconds (45 seconds). In a standard parametric $t$-test, this single massive outlier drastically distorts both the sample mean and the sample standard deviation, inflating the denominator and destroying our ability to detect genuine improvements in typical user experience.

Non-parametric tests solve this by replacing raw milliseconds with ranks: the fastest request gets rank 1, the second fastest rank 2, and the 45,000 ms timeout simply receives rank 35. Whether that timeout took 200 ms or 200,000 ms, its rank is exactly 35. By working with ranks, extreme values can never pull the test statistic off course.

If we have two independent services, the Mann–Whitney $U$ test pools all observations, ranks them from 1 to $N$, and sums the ranks for each service. If one service systematically responds faster than the other, its rank sum will be much smaller than expected by chance. Similarly, for before-and-after paired data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test ranks the absolute differences, attaching positive or negative signs. And for three or more independent groups, the Kruskal–Wallis $H$-test generalises rank sums to evaluate whether any group dominates stochastically.

In everyday terms

Non-parametric tests are like ranking marathon runners by their finish place rather than their stopwatch times: a runner who stumbles and finishes an hour late does not disrupt who came first, second, and third.

02 Step by step

  1. Inspect the measurement scale and distribution: choose rank tests when data is ordinal, heavily skewed, or contaminated with outliers that invalidate normality.
  2. Select the appropriate rank design: Mann–Whitney $U$ for two independent groups, Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired samples, or Kruskal–Wallis $H$ for three or more independent groups.
  3. Pool observations and assign ascending integer ranks from 1 to $N$, replacing ties with their average rank.
  4. Compute the test statistic by summing ranks per group (or summing positive and negative ranks of paired differences).
  5. Evaluate the p-value: using exact permutation distributions for small sample sizes, or asymptotic normal or chi-square approximations for $N > 20$.
  6. Interpret the test as an assessment of stochastic dominance or median shift under the assumption of similarly shaped distributions.

03 The maths

  1. Mann–Whitney U Statistic (Two Independent Samples)

    $$U_1 = R_1 - \frac{n_1(n_1 + 1)}{2}, \qquad U_2 = n_1 n_2 - U_1, \qquad U = \min(U_1, U_2)$$

    Here $R_1$ is the sum of ranks for group 1, and $n_1, n_2$ are sample sizes. The statistic $U_1$ directly counts the number of times an observation from group 1 is smaller than an observation from group 2. Under the null hypothesis of equal distributions, $E[U] = n_1 n_2 / 2$ and $\text{Var}(U) = n_1 n_2 (n_1 + n_2 + 1) / 12$.

  2. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Statistic (Paired Samples)

    $$D_i = X_{1,i} - X_{2,i}, \qquad W = \min\left(\sum_{D_i > 0} \text{rank}(|D_i|), \, \sum_{D_i < 0} \text{rank}(|D_i|)\right)$$

    For paired observations, non-zero differences $D_i$ are ranked by absolute magnitude $|D_i|$. Ranks for positive differences and negative differences are summed separately. Under the null hypothesis of symmetric, zero-median differences, positive and negative rank sums are balanced with expectation $E[W] = n(n + 1) / 4$.

  3. Kruskal–Wallis H-Statistic (K Independent Groups)

    $$H = \frac{12}{N(N + 1)} \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{R_k^2}{n_k} - 3(N + 1) \sim \chi^2(K - 1)$$

    Here $N = \sum n_k$ is the total pooled sample size, and $R_k$ is the rank sum of the $k$-th group. The statistic $H$ measures how far average group ranks deviate from the overall expected average rank $(N + 1) / 2$. Under $H_0$, $H$ converges asymptotically to a chi-square distribution with $K - 1$ degrees of freedom.

04 Key takeaways

  • Non-parametric tests do not assume Gaussian distributions; they operate on ranks, making them invariant to monotonic transformations.
  • The Mann–Whitney U test tests whether one distribution stochastically dominates another ($P(X > Y) \ne 0.5$); it is only a test of medians if distributions share identical shape and spread.
  • The asymptotic relative efficiency of the Mann–Whitney test compared to Student's t-test is 95.5% on perfect Gaussian data, and can be substantially higher on heavy-tailed distributions.
  • When sample sizes are small ($n < 20$), exact permutation p-values should be computed rather than normal approximations.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 35

# Skewed latency distributions for two services (exponential)
service_a = rng.exponential(scale=150.0, size=n)
service_b = rng.exponential(scale=80.0, size=n)

# 1. Mann-Whitney U test (two independent samples)
u_stat, p_mann = stats.mannwhitneyu(service_b, service_a, alternative='less')

# 2. Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired benchmark on same 35 workload queries)
latency_before = rng.exponential(scale=120.0, size=n)
latency_after = latency_before * rng.uniform(0.70, 0.95, size=n)
w_stat, p_wilc = stats.wilcoxon(latency_after, latency_before, alternative='less')

# 3. Kruskal-Wallis H-test (three independent groups)
service_c = rng.exponential(scale=200.0, size=n)
h_stat, p_kruskal = stats.kruskal(service_a, service_b, service_c)

print(f"Mann-Whitney U: stat={u_stat:.1f}, p={p_mann:.4f}")
print(f"Wilcoxon signed-rank: stat={w_stat:.1f}, p={p_wilc:.4e}")
print(f"Kruskal-Wallis H: stat={h_stat:.3f}, p={p_kruskal:.4e}")

Common pitfalls

  • Claiming a significant Mann–Whitney U test proves a difference in medians when group variances or distributional shapes differ substantially.
  • Discarding zero differences in the Wilcoxon signed-rank test without realising it reduces effective sample size and can induce bias.
  • Using parametric tests on ordinal survey ratings (e.g. Likert 1–5 scales) where spacing between numbers is not metric or uniform.
  • Forgetting that non-parametric tests still require independent and identically distributed observations within groups.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why are rank-based tests like Mann–Whitney and Wilcoxon robust against severe outliers?

Why: Converting measurements to ranks ensures that whether an extreme value is 100 or 1,000,000, its impact on the test statistic is limited strictly to its position in the sorted list.

Q2What does the Mann–Whitney $U$ test formally evaluate without making any distributional shape assumptions?

Why: Mann–Whitney U evaluates stochastic dominance. It only reflects a pure shift in population medians if one assumes the two distributions possess identical shape and variance.

Q3How does the statistical efficiency of the Mann–Whitney test compare to Student's $t$-test on purely Gaussian data?

Why: The Hodges–Lehmann asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) of Mann–Whitney relative to the t-test on normal data is $3/\pi \approx 0.955$, meaning very little efficiency is lost even when normality holds.

Q4Which non-parametric test serves as the distribution-free counterpart to one-way ANOVA for three or more independent groups?

Why: The Kruskal–Wallis H-test generalises the two-sample Mann–Whitney rank test to three or more independent groups, evaluating whether group rank sums differ significantly.
