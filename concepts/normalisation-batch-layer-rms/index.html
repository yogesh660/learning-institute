Normalisation: Batch, Layer, and RMS Normalisation

Intermediate activations shift and rescale during training as upstream weights update. Normalisation standardises activations across batch, channel, or feature dimensions to smooth the loss landscape and accelerate convergence.

01 The idea

Deep neural networks are notoriously vulnerable to internal activation drift: small weight changes in early layers cause cascading shifts in the mean and scale of downstream inputs. Normalisation techniques—principally Batch Normalisation for vision, Layer Normalisation for sequences, and RMS Normalisation for modern language models—stabilise internal distributions, allowing higher learning rates and dramatically smoother optimisation landscapes.

Imagine training layer 30 in a deep network. At every step, layers 1 through 29 update their parameters. Even if each update is modest, their combined effect alters the mean, variance, and distribution of the inputs arriving at layer 30. Layer 30 is perpetually trying to learn a mapping on a moving target. Without calibration, activations can saturate nonlinearities or drift toward extreme magnitudes, slowing training to a crawl.

Batch Normalisation (BatchNorm) tackles this by computing the mean and variance across the batch dimension for each feature channel independently. By forcing the mini-batch activations to have zero mean and unit variance, it smooths the loss landscape and permits learning rates ten times larger. However, BatchNorm couples the representations of distinct examples within a batch, requires maintaining running statistics for inference, and deteriorates when mini-batch sizes are small ($B < 8$) or sequences vary in length.

Layer Normalisation (LayerNorm) eliminates batch dependencies by computing the mean and variance across the hidden feature dimension for each individual sample independently. Because it operates within a single example, LayerNorm behaves identically during training and evaluation, handles dynamic sequence lengths effortlessly, and serves as the structural foundation of Transformers. Building on this, RMS Normalisation (RMSNorm) observes that the primary stabilisation benefit stems from scaling invariance rather than mean-centring; by normalising activations strictly by their root-mean-square, RMSNorm achieves identical training stability while cutting normalisation compute overhead by 10% to 50% in architectures like Llama and Mistral.

In everyday terms

Like tuning instruments in an orchestra: BatchNorm adjusts each instrument section by averaging volume across the whole day's rehearsals (the batch), whereas LayerNorm and RMSNorm balance all instruments within each individual musician's performance independently.

02 Step by step

  1. Match normalisation type to architecture: deploy BatchNorm for 2D/3D ConvNets with large stable batches ($B \ge 32$); deploy LayerNorm or RMSNorm for Transformers, sequence models, or small-batch regimes.
  2. Compute reduction statistics: for LayerNorm, calculate sample mean $\mu_i$ and sample variance $\sigma_i^2$ across the hidden dimension $D$; for RMSNorm, calculate the root-mean-square $\operatorname{RMS}(x_i) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_j x_{i,j}^2 + \epsilon}$.
  3. Standardise activations: centre and divide by scale for LayerNorm; divide raw activations directly by the root-mean-square for RMSNorm.
  4. Apply learnable affine parameters: scale by learnable vector $\gamma$ and shift by $\beta$ ($y = \gamma \odot \hat{x} + \beta$), guaranteeing the network can recover any optimal activation distribution.
  5. Select architectural placement: in Transformer blocks, place normalisation before attention and feed-forward sublayers (Pre-LN: $x + \operatorname{SubLayer}(\operatorname{Norm}(x))$) to guarantee stable gradient flow without brittle warm-up requirements.

03 The maths

  1. Batch Normalisation (per feature channel across batch)

    $$\hat{x}_{i,k} = \frac{x_{i,k} - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \quad y_{i,k} = \gamma_k \hat{x}_{i,k} + \beta_k, \qquad \mu_B = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B x_{i,k}, \quad \sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B (x_{i,k} - \mu_B)^2$$

    $B$ is batch size, $x_{i,k}$ is the activation of sample $i$ at channel $k$, $\epsilon$ is a small numerical floor (e.g. $10^{-5}$), and $\gamma_k, \beta_k$ are learnable affine parameters. In inference, $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are replaced by frozen running population averages $\mu_{\text{run}}$ and $\sigma^2_{\text{run}}$.

  2. Layer Normalisation (per sample across hidden features)

    $$\hat{x}_{i,j} = \frac{x_{i,j} - \mu_i}{\sqrt{\sigma_i^2 + \epsilon}}, \quad y_{i,j} = \gamma_j \hat{x}_{i,j} + \beta_j, \qquad \mu_i = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=1}^D x_{i,j}, \quad \sigma_i^2 = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=1}^D (x_{i,j} - \mu_i)^2$$

    $D$ is the hidden representation dimension, and statistics are computed across features $j=1,\dots,D$ for each individual sample $i$ independently. Because no cross-sample averaging occurs, training and inference calculations are identical and completely invariant to batch size.

  3. Root Mean Square Normalisation (RMSNorm)

    $$\bar{x}_{i,j} = \frac{x_{i,j}}{\operatorname{RMS}(x_i)} \cdot \gamma_j, \qquad \operatorname{RMS}(x_i) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=1}^D x_{i,j}^2 + \epsilon}$$

    RMSNorm discards the mean-centring step ($\mu_i=0$) and scales activations solely by their quadratic root-mean-square norm. By removing two sequential memory passes (mean computation and mean subtraction), RMSNorm accelerates execution latency on modern GPU tensor cores while preserving regularised scaling stability.

04 Key takeaways

  • BatchNorm normalises across samples in a mini-batch; LayerNorm normalises across hidden features within a single sample.
  • RMSNorm removes mean subtraction from LayerNorm, matching its training stability while substantially reducing memory bandwidth and kernel overhead.
  • Learnable affine parameters ($\gamma, \beta$) preserve representational capacity by allowing the network to scale or shift standardised activations if an identity mapping is optimal.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Synthetic batch: 2 samples, 5 hidden features
np.random.seed(42)
X = np.random.randn(2, 5) * 3.0 + 2.0  # mean ~ 2, std ~ 3

# 1. LayerNorm: zero mean and unit variance per sample across features
mu_ln = np.mean(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
var_ln = np.var(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
X_ln = (X - mu_ln) / np.sqrt(var_ln + 1e-5)

# 2. RMSNorm: unit root-mean-square per sample across features
rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(X**2, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-5)
X_rms = X / rms

print("Original Sample 0:      ", np.round(X[0], 2).tolist())
print("Sample 0 mean and std:  ", round(float(np.mean(X[0])), 2), round(float(np.std(X[0])), 2))
print("LayerNorm Sample 0:     ", np.round(X_ln[0], 2).tolist())
print("LayerNorm mean and std: ", round(float(np.mean(X_ln[0])), 4), round(float(np.std(X_ln[0])), 4))

Common pitfalls

  • Using Batch Normalisation with small mini-batch sizes ($B \le 4$), where high sampling noise in batch statistics degrades convergence and final accuracy.
  • Failing to set the model to evaluation mode (`model.eval()`) during inference, causing BatchNorm to compute statistics on test batches instead of using tracked population averages.
  • Using Post-LN (placing LayerNorm after the residual addition) without extensive learning rate warm-up, which causes sharp gradient instabilities in deep Transformer backbones.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is Layer Normalisation preferred over Batch Normalisation in Transformers and natural language processing?

Why: LayerNorm operates along the channel/feature dimension for each individual token and sample independently. It does not mix information across the batch dimension, allowing identical execution during training and single-token inference.

Q2What key computational modification distinguishes RMSNorm from standard LayerNorm?

Why: RMSNorm posits that the scaling property of LayerNorm provides the primary training benefit. By omitting mean-centring ($\mu_i=0$), it reduces memory traffic and execution time while maintaining equivalent stability.

Q3What role do the learnable parameters $\gamma$ (scale) and $\beta$ (shift) serve in normalisation layers?

Why: Without $\gamma$ and $\beta$, normalisation strictly forces activations to zero mean and unit variance, which might limit the representational power of non-linear activations. Learnable affine parameters allow the model to adapt or even undo the normalisation if optimal.

Q4What happens if you deploy a model containing Batch Normalisation in production but forget to switch it to evaluation mode?

Why: In training mode, BatchNorm computes statistics from the current batch. During inference on a single query ($B=1$), the batch variance is zero, producing undefined divisions or nonsensical outputs unless frozen running statistics are used.
