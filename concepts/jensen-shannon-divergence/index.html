01 The idea

While the Kullback–Leibler divergence is fundamental to information theory, its asymmetry and unbounded nature create severe numerical instability when distributions have non-overlapping support. The Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) resolves these flaws by averaging KL divergences to a shared mixture distribution. Symmetrical, always finite, and bounded between 0 and 1, the square root of Jensen–Shannon divergence forms a true mathematical metric widely used in generative adversarial networks (GANs) and distribution drift monitoring.

Imagine auditing data drift between two model deployment environments. In training, user age follows distribution $P$; in production, user age follows distribution $Q$.

If there is even a single age category present in production that never occurred in training (say, users aged 95+), $P(95+) = 0$ while $Q(95+) > 0$. If you compute the forward KL divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P)$, the ratio $Q(x) / P(x)$ divides by zero, blowing the score up to infinity! A single rare event completely blinds the metric to how closely the remaining 99.9% of the distribution matches.

The Jensen–Shannon divergence solves this by introducing a midpoint: the balanced mixture distribution $M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$. Because $M$ contains half of $P$ and half of $Q$, $M(x)$ is strictly positive whenever either $P(x) > 0$ or $Q(x) > 0$. Neither distribution can ever divide by zero against $M$. JSD then measures the average divergence from both distributions to this midpoint: $\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M)$. For our financial market example ($P = [0.70, 0.20, 0.10]$ and $Q = [1/3, 1/3, 1/3]$), $M = [0.517, 0.267, 0.217]$. The divergences to $M$ produce $\text{JSD} \approx 0.108$ bits. Taking the base 2 logarithm guarantees JSD is bounded between 0 (identical) and 1 bit (completely disjoint). Furthermore, $\sqrt{\text{JSD}}$ satisfies the triangle inequality, creating a true metric distance between probability distributions.

In everyday terms

If KL divergence is like measuring the elevation difference between two hikers on a mountain where one hiker cannot see the other's cliff, Jensen–Shannon divergence is like finding the exact halfway basecamp and measuring how far each hiker must walk to meet in the middle.

02 Step by step

  1. Given two distributions $P$ and $Q$ over the same space, construct the equal-weight midpoint mixture $M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$.
  2. Compute the forward KL divergence from $P$ to the mixture: $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M)$.
  3. Compute the forward KL divergence from $Q$ to the mixture: $D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M)$.
  4. Average the two divergences to obtain the Jensen–Shannon divergence: $\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M)$.
  5. If using logarithm base 2, note that JSD is bounded in $[0, 1]$; if using natural logarithm $\ln$, JSD is bounded in $[0, \ln 2]$.
  6. Compute the Jensen–Shannon distance $\sqrt{\text{JSD}}$ whenever a true mathematical metric satisfying the triangle inequality is required.

03 The maths

  1. Jensen–Shannon Divergence Definition

    $$\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M), \qquad M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$$

    Here $M$ is the equal-weight mixture distribution. Because $M(x) > 0$ wherever either $P(x) > 0$ or $Q(x) > 0$, the ratio never divides by zero, ensuring JSD is always well-defined and finite even for distributions with disjoint supports. Furthermore, JSD is strictly symmetric: $\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = \text{JSD}(Q \parallel P)$.

  2. Entropy Formulation and Boundedness

    $$\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = H(M) - \frac{1}{2}\left[H(P) + H(Q)\right]$$

    JSD equals the Shannon entropy of the mixture distribution minus the average entropies of the individual distributions. By the strict concavity of Shannon entropy, $\text{JSD} \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $P = Q$. When measured in base 2 logarithm, JSD is bounded in $[0, 1]$ bit, reaching its theoretical maximum of $1.0$ bit when $P$ and $Q$ have completely disjoint support.

  3. The Jensen–Shannon Distance Metric

    $$d_{\text{JS}}(P, Q) = \sqrt{\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q)}$$

    In 2003, Endres and Schindelin proved that the square root of the Jensen–Shannon divergence satisfies the triangle inequality: $d_{\text{JS}}(P, R) \le d_{\text{JS}}(P, Q) + d_{\text{JS}}(Q, R)$. Combined with non-negativity, symmetry, and identity of indiscernibles, $d_{\text{JS}}$ forms a true mathematical metric.

04 Key takeaways

  • Jensen–Shannon divergence is strictly symmetric: $\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = \text{JSD}(Q \parallel P)$.
  • JSD is bounded in $[0, 1]$ (when measured in bits) and remains finite even when distributions have completely disjoint support.
  • The square root $\sqrt{\text{JSD}}$ is a valid mathematical metric satisfying the triangle inequality.
  • The original formulation of Generative Adversarial Networks (Goodfellow et al., 2014) showed that an optimal discriminator minimax game is mathematically equivalent to minimising the Jensen–Shannon divergence between real and generated distributions.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.spatial.distance as dist
import scipy.stats as stats

p = np.array([0.70, 0.20, 0.10])
q = np.array([1.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 3.0])

# Midpoint mixture distribution M
m = 0.5 * (p + q)

# Jensen-Shannon divergence in bits
jsd = 0.5 * stats.entropy(p, m, base=2) + 0.5 * stats.entropy(q, m, base=2)

# Jensen-Shannon distance metric: sqrt(JSD)
js_distance = dist.jensenshannon(p, q, base=2)

# Completely disjoint distributions (maximal JSD)
p_disjoint = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
q_disjoint = np.array([0.0, 1.0])
m_disjoint = 0.5 * (p_disjoint + q_disjoint)
jsd_max = 0.5 * stats.entropy(p_disjoint, m_disjoint, base=2) + \
          0.5 * stats.entropy(q_disjoint, m_disjoint, base=2)

print(f"JSD(P || Q):          {jsd:.4f} bits")
print(f"JS distance metric:   {js_distance:.4f}")
print(f"Disjoint support JSD: {jsd_max:.4f} bits (bounded maximum = 1.0)")

Common pitfalls

  • Using natural logarithm instead of $\log_2$ and assuming the upper bound is 1.0 (with natural log $\ln$, the upper bound is $\ln 2 \approx 0.6931$).
  • Forgetting that when distributions have disjoint support with zero overlap, JSD saturates at its maximum value (1.0), causing vanishing gradients in GAN training.
  • Confusing JSD (which is not a metric, but a squared distance) with the Jensen–Shannon distance $\sqrt{\text{JSD}}$ (a true metric).
  • Applying JSD to unnormalised weight vectors without ensuring $\sum p_i = 1$ and $\sum q_i = 1$.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is the Jensen–Shannon divergence preferred over the Kullback–Leibler divergence for monitoring data drift in production pipelines?

Why: If a production distribution has an outcome unseen in training, KL divergence divides by zero and explodes to infinity. JSD compares against the average mixture $M$, remaining bounded and stable.

Q2When calculated using base 2 logarithms, what is the maximum possible value of the Jensen–Shannon divergence $\text{JSD}(P \parallel Q)$?

Why: When distributions $P$ and $Q$ have completely disjoint support (no shared non-zero states), $\text{JSD} = \log_2(2) = 1.0$ bit.

Q3Which mathematical transformation turns the Jensen–Shannon divergence into a true distance metric that satisfies the triangle inequality?

Why: By the Endres–Schindelin theorem (2003), the square root of the Jensen–Shannon divergence satisfies the triangle inequality, making $\sqrt{\text{JSD}}$ a true metric.

Q4What issue arises in generative adversarial network (GAN) training when real and generated distributions have disjoint support under Jensen–Shannon divergence?

Why: When supports do not overlap, JSD is constantly 1.0 regardless of how far apart the distributions are in space, producing vanishing gradients that motivated Wasserstein GANs.
