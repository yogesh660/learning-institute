Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average Models

ARIMA combines autoregression, differencing, and moving-average error shocks into a flexible framework for non-seasonal and seasonal time-series forecasting.

01 The idea

Real-world temporal sequences exhibit persistence, non-stationarity, and recurring seasonal cycles. ARIMA($p, d, q$) integrates autoregressive lag terms of order $p$, differencing of degree $d$ to enforce stationarity, and moving-average error shocks of order $q$. SARIMA extends this formulation with seasonal operators $(P, D, Q)_s$ to capture periodic cycles without inflating parameter counts.

Consider monthly retail revenue. Sales steadily climb over years (trend), spike dramatically every December (seasonality), and display momentum from quarter to quarter (persistence). To forecast next month's sales, three sources of information help: (1) where sales stood last month (autoregression, AR), (2) how much the series drifted recently (differencing, I), and (3) unexpected shocks—such as a promotional surge or a freight delay—whose effects linger into subsequent months (moving average, MA).

ARIMA integrates these three mechanisms into a single linear equation. The parameter $d$ specifies how many times successive differences ($y_t - y_{t-1}$) are taken to eliminate non-stationary drift and stabilise the mean. Once differenced, the AR($p$) component models the current value as a weighted sum of its own previous $p$ values, while the MA($q$) component models it as a linear combination of the current and prior $q$ unobserved white-noise forecast errors.

When dynamics repeat across seasonal periods of length $s$ (e.g. $s = 12$ for monthly cycles), fitting a high-order non-seasonal ARIMA model would require estimating dozens of intermediate coefficients, wasting statistical power. SARIMA resolves this with multiplicative seasonal operators: seasonal differencing $\Delta_s y_t = y_t - y_{t-s}$ eliminates cyclical baselines, while seasonal autoregressive ($P$) and moving-average ($Q$) terms link observations across annual intervals using only a handful of well-identified parameters.

In everyday terms

ARIMA steers a vessel by holding course based on recent heading (AR), trimming against prevailing drift (I), and damping turbulence from rogue waves (MA); SARIMA consults an annual tidal chart to anticipate periodic swells.

02 Step by step

  1. Examine time plots, variance stability, and unit-root tests (ADF) to determine the required regular differencing order $d \in \{0, 1, 2\}$.
  2. Inspect seasonal cycles across period $s$ and apply seasonal differencing $D \in \{0, 1\}$ if repeating annual or weekly patterns persist.
  3. Examine ACF and PACF plots of the differenced series: non-seasonal spikes suggest orders $p$ and $q$, while recurring spikes at seasonal multiples ($s, 2s$) suggest $P$ and $Q$.
  4. Estimate model coefficients ($\phi, \theta, \Phi, \Theta$) using conditional maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or non-linear least squares.
  5. Perform residual diagnostic checks: verify residuals resemble white noise using the Ljung–Box test ($p > 0.05$) and inspect residual ACF for leftover signal.
  6. Compute multi-step point forecasts alongside analytical prediction intervals ($1.96 \times \text{SE}(h)$) that expand with horizon $h$.

03 The maths

  1. The non-seasonal ARIMA($p, d, q$) equation

    $$\phi(B)(1 - B)^d y_t = c + \theta(B)\epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$$

    $B$ denotes the backshift operator ($B^k y_t = y_{t-k}$), $\phi(B) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i B^i$ is the autoregressive polynomial, $(1 - B)^d$ denotes $d$-th order differencing, and $\theta(B) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^q \theta_j B^j$ is the moving-average polynomial. Constant $c$ governs deterministic drift, and $\epsilon_t$ is zero-mean white noise with variance $\sigma^2$.

  2. The multiplicative SARIMA($p, d, q) imes (P, D, Q)_s$ model

    $$\Phi_P(B^s)\,\phi(B)(1 - B^s)^D(1 - B)^d y_t = \Theta_Q(B^s)\,\theta(B)\epsilon_t$$

    $\Phi_P(B^s) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^P \Phi_k B^{ks}$ models seasonal autoregression, $(1 - B^s)^D$ represents seasonal differencing of period $s$, and $\Theta_Q(B^s) = 1 + \sum_{m=1}^Q \Theta_m B^{ms}$ models seasonal moving-average errors. Multiplying seasonal and non-seasonal operators yields a parsimonious composite filter.

  3. Stationarity and invertibility roots

    $$\text{Roots of } \phi(z) = 0 \text{ and } \theta(z) = 0 \text{ must lie strictly outside the unit circle } (|z| > 1)$$

    Characteristic roots outside the complex unit circle guarantee that the AR polynomial is stable (stationary) and the MA polynomial is invertible. Invertibility ensures that unobserved noise shocks $\epsilon_t$ can be expressed uniquely as a convergent infinite autoregression of observed past values.

04 Key takeaways

  • The "I" stands for integrated: a series differenced $d$ times must be integrated (cumulatively summed) $d$ times to recover original forecast levels.
  • Parsimony avoids parameter redundancy: adding both redundant AR and MA terms can cause roots to nearly cancel, destabilising numerical optimisation.
  • Moving-average terms model unobserved past forecast errors ($\epsilon_{t-j}$), not a simple rolling average of observed data points.
  • Forecast uncertainty expands with horizon: for $d \ge 1$, prediction interval widths grow without bound as time horizon $h \to \infty$.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 200
phi_true, c_true = 0.65, 0.2
eps = np.random.normal(0, 1.0, n)

# Generate stationary AR(1) process w_t
w = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    w[t] = c_true + phi_true * w[t-1] + eps[t]

# Integrate to create ARIMA(1, 1, 0) series
y = np.cumsum(w) + 100.0

# Difference series to achieve stationarity
dy = np.diff(y)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(dy) - 1), dy[:-1]])
y_target = dy[1:]
beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y_target, rcond=None)[0]
c_hat, phi_hat = beta[0], beta[1]
res = y_target - X @ beta
sigma2_hat = np.var(res, ddof=2)

# Multi-step point forecast for horizon h = 1, 2, 3
current_dy = dy[-1]
forecast_dy = []
for _ in range(3):
    current_dy = c_hat + phi_hat * current_dy
    forecast_dy.append(current_dy)
forecast_y = y[-1] + np.cumsum(forecast_dy)

print("Estimated drift c:", round(float(c_hat), 3), "true:", c_true)
print("Estimated phi:", round(float(phi_hat), 3), "true:", phi_true)
print("Residual variance:", round(float(sigma2_hat), 3))
print("Last observed y:", round(float(y[-1]), 2))
print("Forecasts y_{t+1..3}:", [round(float(val), 2) for val in forecast_y])

Common pitfalls

  • Over-differencing the series: applying $d=2$ when $d=1$ suffices induces an artificial moving-average root $\theta \approx 1$ and inflates forecast variance.
  • Neglecting the invertibility condition: non-invertible MA models yield unstable recursive error estimates and unreliable out-of-sample confidence intervals.
  • Relying on in-sample fit metrics ($R^2$): high-order ARIMA specifications easily overfit historical noise; model selection should minimise AIC or BIC.
  • Skipping residual diagnostic testing: failing to evaluate the Ljung–Box statistic leaves unmodelled serial dependence buried in forecast residuals.

06 Check yourself

Q1In an ARIMA($p, d, q$) model, what does the parameter $d$ represent?

Why: The parameter $d$ represents the integration order—the number of consecutive differencing operations $(1-B)^d$ required to achieve covariance stationarity.

Q2What does the moving-average component MA($q$) model?

Why: The MA($q$) component models current deviations as a linear combination of past random shocks $\epsilon_t + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q}$.

Q3Why is the multiplicative SARIMA parameterisation preferred over including separate indicator dummy variables for every seasonal period?

Why: SARIMA models seasonal autoregression and moving averages parsimoniously across period $s$, capturing periodic dynamics with few parameters rather than estimating $s-1$ separate coefficients.

Q4What condition must the roots of the autoregressive characteristic polynomial $\phi(z) = 0$ satisfy for the model to be stationary?

Why: Stationarity requires the roots of $\phi(z) = 1 - \sum \phi_i z^i = 0$ to lie outside the unit circle ($|z| > 1$), ensuring past shocks decay to zero rather than exploding over time.
