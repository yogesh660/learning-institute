01 The idea

For decades, deep learning was trained exclusively in 32-bit single-precision floating point (FP32). Modern GPUs (NVIDIA Volta, Ampere, Hopper) feature dedicated Tensor Cores that execute 16-bit matrix multiplications up to 8x faster while consuming half the memory bandwidth. However, naive 16-bit training causes gradients to vanish into numerical zero (underflow) or explode into infinity. Mixed precision training combines 16-bit computation with 32-bit master weights and dynamic loss scaling.

Standard IEEE 754 FP32 consists of 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 mantissa (fraction) bits, offering a dynamic range of $\approx 10^{-38}$ to $10^{38}$. Standard FP16 has only 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits, with a narrow dynamic range of $2^{-14} \approx 6.1 \times 10^{-5}$ to $65,504$.

In deep networks, backpropagated gradients are frequently tiny (e.g. $10^{-6}$). In raw FP16, any gradient smaller than $6.1 \times 10^{-5}$ flushes to exact zero (underflow), stalling learning completely. Conversely, activations larger than 65,504 overflow to `+Inf` / `NaN`.

Mixed precision solves this via three techniques: 1) Master Weights: Weights and optimiser momentum states are maintained in full FP32, while forward and backward passes execute in fast FP16. 2) Dynamic Loss Scaling: Loss is multiplied by a large scale factor $S$ (e.g. 1024 or 65536) before backpropagation, shifting small gradients upward into the representable FP16 range. Gradients are then unscaled by $1/S$ before applying weight updates. 3) Precision-sensitive ops (Softmax, LayerNorm, loss) remain in FP32.

BF16 (Bfloat16, Brain Floating Point) preserves the full 8 exponent bits of FP32 with 7 mantissa bits. It matches FP32's dynamic range, eliminating the need for complex loss scaling entirely.

In everyday terms

Imagine an architect sketching blueprints. When brainstorming room layouts and drawing wall lengths, quick pencil sketches on half-sheet paper (16-bit) are fast and take up little desk space. But when calculating precise structural beam load equations where millimeter rounding errors could collapse the building, the engineer switches to an ultra-precise digital computer with double precision (32-bit). Mixed precision sketches quickly in 16-bit while anchoring critical structural sums in 32-bit.

02 Step by step

  1. Select precision format: Choose BF16 on modern hardware (Ampere, Hopper, TPU) or FP16 with dynamic loss scaling on older GPUs (Turing, Volta).
  2. Maintain FP32 master weights: Store canonical model weights and optimiser states (Adam $m_t$ and $v_t$) in 32-bit precision.
  3. Cast activations for forward pass: Automatically cast input tensors and weights to FP16/BF16 during matrix multiplications and convolutions.
  4. Apply dynamic loss scaling: Multiply calculated scalar loss by scale factor $S$ prior to calling `.backward()`.
  5. Detect overflow and unscale: Inspect FP16 gradients for `Inf` or `NaN`. If detected, skip the optimiser step and decrease $S$; otherwise unscale gradients by $1/S$ and update FP32 master weights.
  6. Cast weights back to 16-bit: Copy updated FP32 master weights into 16-bit tensors for the subsequent training iteration.

03 The maths

  1. Floating Point Bit Layout Comparison

    \begin{aligned} \text{FP32:} & \quad 1 \text{ sign}, \; 8 \text{ exp}, \; 23 \text{ mantissa} \quad [\text{Range } 10^{\pm 38}] \\ \text{FP16:} & \quad 1 \text{ sign}, \; 5 \text{ exp}, \; 10 \text{ mantissa} \quad [\text{Range } 10^{\pm 5}, \; \max = 65,504] \\ \text{BF16:} & \quad 1 \text{ sign}, \; 8 \text{ exp}, \; 7 \text{ mantissa} \quad [\text{Range } 10^{\pm 38}, \; \max = 3.39 \times 10^{38}] \end{aligned}

    BF16 allocates 8 exponent bits identical to FP32, preventing gradient underflow without requiring loss scaling.

  2. Dynamic Loss Scaling Gradient Shift

    g_{\mathrm{scaled}} = \nabla_w (S \cdot \mathcal{L}) = S \cdot \nabla_w \mathcal{L}, \quad g_{\mathrm{true}} = \frac{1}{S} \cdot g_{\mathrm{scaled}}

    Multiplying loss by scale factor $S$ shifts tiny gradients into the representable positive exponent range of FP16.

  3. Dynamic Scale Factor Adaptation Rule

    S_{t+1} = \begin{cases} S_t \cdot \frac{1}{2} & \text{if } \operatorname{has\_inf\_nan}(g_{\mathrm{scaled}}) \\ S_t \cdot 2 & \text{if no inf/nan for } M \text{ consecutive steps} \\ S_t & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

    Adapts loss scale dynamically: shrinking immediately upon numerical overflow and expanding when training is stable.

04 Key takeaways

  • Mixed precision accelerates training 2–3x and cuts activation memory in half using Tensor Cores.
  • FP16 has a narrow dynamic range, requiring dynamic loss scaling to prevent tiny gradients from flushing to zero.
  • BF16 shares FP32's 8-bit exponent, matching FP32 dynamic range and eliminating loss scaling.
  • Master weights and optimiser states must remain in FP32 to accumulate tiny weight delta updates.

05 In code

python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

# Standard PyTorch Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) training pattern
device = 'cuda' if torch.cuda.is_available() else 'cpu'

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(1024, 2048),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(2048, 10)
).to(device)

optimiser = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
criterion = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

# Initialise GradScaler for FP16 loss scaling (on CUDA)
scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler('cuda', enabled=(device == 'cuda'))

# Dummy input batch
x = torch.randn(64, 1024, device=device)
target = torch.randint(0, 10, (64,), device=device)

optimiser.zero_grad()

# Forward pass under autocast context
# Tensor Core operations (Linear, Conv) run in fast FP16/BF16
with torch.amp.autocast(device_type=device, dtype=torch.float16, enabled=(device == 'cuda')):
    output = model(x)
    loss = criterion(output, target)

# Backward pass with scaled gradients to prevent underflow
scaler.scale(loss).backward()

# Unscale gradients and update FP32 master weights
scaler.step(optimiser)
scaler.update()

print(f"Mixed precision step completed on {device}. Current scale factor: {scaler.get_scale()}")

Common pitfalls

  • Applying FP16 to Softmax or LayerNorm: Operations that sum exponents (Softmax) or compute variances easily overflow 65,504; always run normalisation in FP32.
  • Accumulating Adam optimiser states in FP16: In Adam, $v_t$ squares tiny gradients ($10^{-8}$), causing complete underflow in 16-bit; optimiser states must be FP32.
  • Using FP16 without GradScaler: Running raw FP16 backward passes without gradient scaling causes 30–50% of gradients to underflow to zero, stalling convergence.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why are master weights and optimiser momentum states stored in FP32 even during mixed precision training?

Why: Learning rate multiplied by gradient is often small ($10^{-5}$). Adding this tiny delta to an FP16 weight results in no change due to lack of mantissa bits. FP32 master weights preserve small accumulations.

Q2What is the primary advantage of BF16 (Brain Floating Point) over classical FP16?

Why: By keeping 8 exponent bits, BF16 spans the same dynamic range ($10^{\pm 38}$) as FP32, making underflow and overflow virtually non-existent without complex loss scalers.

Q3What is the purpose of dynamic loss scaling when training in FP16?

Why: Many backpropagated gradients are smaller than $2^{-14} \approx 6 \times 10^{-5}$. Scaling loss by e.g. 1024 shifts gradients into the representable positive exponent range.
