Reading Loss Curves and Debugging Training

Loss curves are the primary telemetry of machine learning. Diagnosing divergence, underfitting, overfitting, gradient collapse, and bad learning rates from training versus validation trajectories.

01 The idea

Deep learning models rarely converge cleanly on the first try. Training and validation loss curves provide an unfiltered diagnostic window into internal optimisation dynamics: learning rates that are too aggressive, representational underfitting, noise memorisation, or corrupted labels each leave unmistakable graphical signatures.

When training a neural network, logging training loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}$ and validation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$ at regular intervals creates a pair of diagnostic curves. In a healthy run, both curves decline steadily in tandem; the training curve settles slightly below the validation curve, and both decelerate into a smooth asymptotic plateau as parameters approach a high-quality basin.

When learning fails, the curves reveal the underlying pathology. In **overfitting**, the training loss continues its monotonic descent toward zero while the validation loss reaches a trough and turns sharply upward (the classic U-shaped curve). The model has exhausted generalisable patterns and has begun memorising idiosyncratic sample noise. In **underfitting**, both curves flatten almost immediately at an unacceptably high loss plateau: the model lacks sufficient capacity, features lack signal, or the optimisation has stalled.

The single most powerful diagnostic protocol is the **single-batch sanity check**: take 10 to 20 samples from your dataset and train the model on them without regularisation. If an overparameterised network cannot drive training loss to absolute zero on 10 samples within 50 epochs, there is an algorithmic or coding defect in your pipeline—such as inverted loss signs, detached autograd tensors, mismatched label dimensions, or unscaled input features.

In everyday terms

Like reading an intensive care telemetry monitor: a steady downward rhythm reflects healthy recovery, erratic vertical spikes indicate cardiac fibrillation (exploding learning rate), and flatlining indicates complete system failure (dead gradients or frozen weights).

02 Step by step

  1. Perform the single-batch check: train on 10 fixed samples with zero regularisation; confirm that loss drives to zero ($< 10^{-4}$) to rule out implementation bugs.
  2. Check initial loss magnitude: verify that initial epoch loss aligns with random guessing (e.g. $-\ln(1/C) = \ln(C)$ for cross-entropy with $C$ balanced classes).
  3. Plot curves on identical step axes: chart $\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$ simultaneously, smoothing noisy batch fluctuations with exponential moving averages.
  4. Measure the generalisation gap: compute $\Delta = \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}} - \mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}$; an expanding gap signals the onset of overfitting, dictating early stopping or increased weight decay.
  5. Calibrate learning rate from curvature: if loss oscillates wildly or produces NaNs, decrease the rate by $3\times$ to $10\times$; if loss slopes downward at a glacial pace, increase the learning rate or apply warm-up.

03 The maths

  1. Expected initial cross-entropy loss baseline

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{init}} = -\sum_{c=1}^C \frac{1}{C} \ln\left(\frac{1}{C}\right) = \ln(C)$$

    $C$ is the number of mutually exclusive target classes. For a randomly initialised network where all output logits are approximately zero, predicted probabilities are uniform ($1/C$). For CIFAR-10 ($C=10$), initial loss must be near $\ln(10) \approx 2.3026$. An initial loss of $15.0$ or $0.1$ signals broken initialisation, incorrect loss criteria, or faulty label indices.

  2. Generalisation gap tracking

    $$\mathcal{G}(t) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(t) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}(t), \qquad \frac{d\mathcal{G}}{dt} > 0 \quad \text{when } \frac{d\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}}{dt} > 0 \text{ and } \frac{d\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}}{dt} < 0$$

    $\mathcal{G}(t)$ measures the generalisation gap at epoch $t$. When the derivative of validation loss turns positive while training loss continues declining, the model transitions from representation learning to memorisation, indicating that early stopping should trigger.

04 Key takeaways

  • Overfitting a single mini-batch of 10 samples to zero loss is the fastest method to verify that data loading, forward passes, autograd, and loss calculation work correctly.
  • Initial cross-entropy loss on $C$ balanced classes should always be approximately $\ln(C)$; substantial divergence warns of unnormalised inputs or corrupted labels.
  • A rising validation loss paired with a collapsing training loss marks the textbook onset of overfitting, calling for increased regularisation or early stopping.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# 1. Theoretical initial loss check for C=10 classes
C = 10
expected_init = np.log(C)
print(f"Theoretical initial loss for {C} balanced classes: {expected_init:.4f}")

# 2. Diagnose training trajectories from loss history
epochs = list(range(1, 7))
train_curve = [2.30, 1.80, 1.20, 0.60, 0.20, 0.05]
val_curve   = [2.31, 1.85, 1.30, 0.95, 1.10, 1.45]

gap = val_curve[-1] - train_curve[-1]
is_overfitting = (val_curve[-1] > val_curve[-2]) and (train_curve[-1] < train_curve[-2])

print(f"Epoch 6 - Training loss:   {train_curve[-1]:.2f}")
print(f"Epoch 6 - Validation loss: {val_curve[-1]:.2f}")
print(f"Generalisation gap:        {gap:.2f}")
if is_overfitting:
    print("Diagnosis: Severe overfitting detected (val loss rising while train loss drops)")

Common pitfalls

  • Tuning regularisation hyperparameters before proving that the network can overfit a single mini-batch to zero loss.
  • Calculating training loss as a cumulative running average across an epoch while evaluating validation loss at epoch end, falsely making validation loss appear lower than training loss.
  • Confusing optimisation stagnation (loss plateauing early due to vanishing gradients or a tiny learning rate) with genuine model convergence.

06 Check yourself

Q1You initialise a neural network for a 100-class classification problem. What should the initial loss be approximately if weights are randomised properly?

Why: With uniform random predictions across $C=100$ balanced classes, predicted probabilities are $1/100 = 0.01$. The expected negative log-likelihood is $-\ln(0.01) = \ln(100) \approx 4.6052$.

Q2What is the primary purpose of the "single-batch sanity check" in deep learning?

Why: An overparameterised network has ample capacity to fit 10 samples perfectly. If it cannot achieve zero training loss on a tiny fixed batch, an algorithmic or coding defect exists in the model or loss setup.

Q3If both training loss and validation loss flatten early at a very high error rate and do not improve with further training, what is the most likely diagnosis?

Why: When both curves plateau together at an unacceptably high loss, the model is failing to fit the training data itself (underfitting), indicating capacity or optimisation constraints rather than variance issues.

Q4During training, you observe that training loss decreases steadily while validation loss decreases to a minimum at epoch 12 and then steadily rises. What action should you take?

Why: The divergence of validation loss away from training loss indicates that the model has crossed into noise memorisation. Restoring the epoch 12 checkpoint or applying stronger regularisation directly addresses this overfitting.
