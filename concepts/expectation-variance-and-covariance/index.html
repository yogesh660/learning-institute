Expectation, Variance and Covariance

The three pillars of summary statistics: expected value as centre of mass, variance as spread, and covariance as joint linear co-movement.

01 The idea

Expectation measures the long-run average outcome of a random process, variance quantifies the dispersion around that average, and covariance tracks how two variables move together. Together, they form the algebraic backbone of loss functions, risk analysis, and matrix methods in machine learning.

Imagine a roulette game where you bet £10 on red. Eighteen out of 37 slots are red, 18 are black, and 1 is green (zero). If red hits, you win £10; otherwise, you lose your £10. On any single spin, you either gain £10 or lose £10—you never see a fractional amount. But if you play 1,000 times, your average outcome per spin converges toward $(18/37)(+10) + (19/37)(-10) \approx -£0.27$. That long-run average is the expected value $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

Now compare two investments. Asset A returns 5% every single year without fail. Asset B returns 45% in good years and loses 35% in bad years, averaging 5%. Both assets have the exact same expected return of 5%, yet Asset B is dramatically riskier. Variance $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ captures this risk by measuring the average squared deviation from the mean, while its square root—standard deviation $\sigma$—puts the spread back into original units like pounds or percentage points.

When we track multiple variables, we need to know whether they move in sympathy. If temperature rises and ice cream sales climb while hot tea sales drop, temperature shares positive covariance with ice cream and negative covariance with tea. In linear regression and PCA, covariance matrices summarise all pairwise relationships to find optimal projections and regression slopes.

In everyday terms

Expectation is the fulcrum where a seesaw balances; variance is how widely the weights are distributed along the plank; covariance is whether two passengers lean in unison.

02 Step by step

  1. Calculate expected value $\mathbb{E}[X]$ by weighting every possible outcome by its probability (or integrating $x f(x) dx$).
  2. Exploit linearity of expectation: $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y]$ holds whether variables are independent or not.
  3. Compute variance via the computational shortcut: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$.
  4. Evaluate covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$.
  5. Assemble pairwise covariances into a symmetric, positive semi-definite covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ for multivariate modelling.

03 The maths

  1. Expected Value and Linearity

    $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \begin{cases} \sum_x x\,p(x) & \text{discrete} \\[6pt] \int_{-\infty}^\infty x\,f(x)\,dx & \text{continuous} \end{cases}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y] + c$$

    The expected value is the probability-weighted average (first moment). Linearity of expectation is an unconditional property that holds regardless of correlation or non-linear dependence between $X$ and $Y$.

  2. Variance and Standard Deviation

    $$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$$

    Variance is the second central moment, quantifying dispersion around the mean in squared units. Adding a constant shift $b$ leaves spread unchanged, while multiplying by a scale factor $a$ magnifies variance by $a^2$. Standard deviation is $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$.

  3. Covariance and Sum of Correlated Variables

    $$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X \pm Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) \pm 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$$

    Covariance measures joint linear tendency. If high values of $X$ coincide with high values of $Y$, covariance is positive; if they coincide with low values, it is negative. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, though zero covariance does not necessarily imply independence.

04 Key takeaways

  • Linearity of expectation requires zero assumptions about independence; it holds universally across all random variables.
  • Variance scales quadratically ($\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$), which is why standard deviation is preferred for intuitive scale comparisons.
  • Zero covariance indicates absence of linear association, but strong non-linear relationships (such as $Y = X^2$ for symmetric $X$) can still exist.
  • Sample variance divides by $n - 1$ (Bessel's correction) rather than $n$ to guarantee an unbiased estimator of population variance.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Seeded inline data generation
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = rng.normal(loc=10.0, scale=2.0, size=1000)
# y shares linear co-movement with x plus noise
y = 1.5 * x + rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=1.0, size=1000)

mean_x, mean_y = np.mean(x), np.mean(y)
var_x = np.var(x, ddof=1)
cov_xy = np.cov(x, y)[0, 1]
var_sum = np.var(x + y, ddof=1)
theoretical_sum = var_x + np.var(y, ddof=1) + 2 * cov_xy

print(f"E[X] = {mean_x:.2f}, E[Y] = {mean_y:.2f}")
print(f"Var(X) = {var_x:.3f}, Cov(X, Y) = {cov_xy:.3f}")
print(f"Var(X + Y) empirical = {var_sum:.3f}, identity = {theoretical_sum:.3f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Writing $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a\operatorname{Var}(X) + b$; constants vanish under variance and scalar multipliers are squared ($a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$).
  • Assuming that $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ implies $X$ and $Y$ are independent; covariance only checks for linear co-movement.
  • Confusing sample variance (divided by $n-1$) with population variance (divided by $n$) when computing degrees of freedom.
  • Comparing raw covariances across different features without standardising; features with larger numerical units naturally produce artificially massive covariances.

06 Check yourself

Q1If $X$ is a random variable with $\mathbb{E}[X] = 4$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 9$, what is $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$?

Why: Using the identity $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$, we have $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = 9 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$.

Q2If $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4$, what is $\operatorname{Var}(-3X + 5)$?

Why: Constants shift the distribution without changing spread, and scalar multipliers are squared: $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X) = (-3)^2 \times 4 = 9 \times 4 = 36$.

Q3If two random variables $X$ and $Y$ have $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, which statement is strictly true?

Why: Zero covariance guarantees zero linear correlation. However, they can still share a deterministic non-linear relationship (e.g. $Y = X^2$ for symmetric $X$).

Q4Why does sample variance use $n - 1$ in the denominator instead of $n$?

Why: Using the sample mean $\bar{x}$ instead of the true population mean $\mu$ slightly underestimates squared deviations; dividing by $n - 1$ (Bessel's correction) yields an unbiased estimator.

Q5Under what conditions does linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]$, hold?

Why: Linearity of expectation is an algebraic property of integrals and summations. It holds universally for all random variables with finite expectations, with zero assumptions about independence.

Q6What is the covariance of any random variable $X$ with itself, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X)$?

Why: By definition, $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(X - \mu_X)] = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

Q7A game pays £20 with probability 0.2, £10 with probability 0.3, and £0 with probability 0.5. If it costs £8 to play, what is the net expected profit or loss per game?

Why: Expected payout is $\mathbb{E}[\text{payout}] = (20 \times 0.2) + (10 \times 0.3) + (0 \times 0.5) = 4 + 3 + 0 = £7.00$. Subtracting the £8.00 entry fee leaves a net expected loss of $£7 - £8 = -£1.00$.

Q8If two independent random variables $X$ and $Y$ have variances $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 25$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 16$, what is the variance of their difference, $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y)$?

Why: Variances of independent variables always add, even when subtracting the variables: $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (-1)^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y) = 25 + 16 = 41$. Subtracting variances to get 9 is a classic pitfall.

Q9Evaluating np.var(data) and np.var(data, ddof=1) on data = np.array([2, 4, 4, 4, 6]) yields:

Why: The mean is 4. Sum of squared differences is $(-2)^2 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 2^2 = 8$. Default np.var divides by $n = 5$ (giving 1.6), whereas ddof=1 applies Bessel's correction, dividing by $n - 1 = 4$ (giving 2.0).

Q10Suppose $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 9$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 16$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = -6$. What is the variance of the linear combination $Z = 2X - Y$?

Why: $\operatorname{Var}(2X - Y) = 2^2\operatorname{Var}(X) + (-1)^2\operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2(2)(-1)\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 4(9) + 16 - 4(-6) = 36 + 16 + 24 = 76$.

Q11Two binary random variables $X, Y \in \{0, 1\}$ have joint distribution $P(0,0)=0.4, P(0,1)=0.1, P(1,0)=0.1, P(1,1)=0.4$. What is their covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$?

Why: Marginal expectations are $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.5$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.5$. The product expectation is $\mathbb{E}[XY] = 1 \times P(1, 1) = 0.40$. Thus $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0.40 - (0.50 \times 0.50) = 0.40 - 0.25 = 0.15$.

Q12Let $X$ take values $\{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}$ each with probability $0.2$, and let $Y = X^2$. What is $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ and what does it reveal about independence?

Why: By symmetry, $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0$. However, $Y$ is completely determined by $X$, demonstrating that zero covariance does not imply statistical independence.
