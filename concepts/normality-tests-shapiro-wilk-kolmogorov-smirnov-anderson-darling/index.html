01 The idea

Many classical statistical procedures—from linear regression inference to Student's t-tests and ANOVA—rely on assumptions of underlying normality. Rather than relying on wishful thinking or visual inspection of histograms alone, formal normality tests quantify whether sample data deviates significantly from a Gaussian distribution. Understanding the distinct mathematical mechanics of Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, and Anderson–Darling tests enables practitioners to diagnose skewness, kurtosis, and tail anomalies accurately.

Imagine training a regression model and checking whether its residuals conform to the standard assumption of normally distributed errors. You generate 60 residual values. Visually, a histogram or Q-Q plot might look "roughly bell-shaped", but human eyes are notoriously prone to subjective bias.

How can we mathematically evaluate Gaussian adherence? There are three distinct perspectives:

First, correlation with normal order statistics: if data is truly normal, plotting sorted sample values against expected standard normal quantiles should form an almost perfect straight line. The Shapiro–Wilk test computes this exact correlation squared ($W$). For a genuine normal sample of size 60, $W \approx 0.982$ with $p = 0.502$ (no deviation detected). For exponential or skewed data, points curl away from the line, dropping $W$ to $0.833$ with $p \approx 10^{-6}$ (overwhelming rejection). Second, maximum gap between cumulative curves: the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test compares the empirical step-function distribution against the theoretical smooth normal cumulative curve, measuring the single largest vertical gap ($D$). Third, weighted tail sensitivity: the KS test is insensitive in the extreme tails because cumulative curves are anchored to 0 and 1 at the boundaries. The Anderson–Darling test solves this by weighting discrepancies by $1 / [F(x)(1 - F(x))]$, placing heavy mathematical emphasis on the tails where critical anomalies occur.

In everyday terms

Testing normality with these three methods is like inspecting a tailor-made suit: Shapiro–Wilk checks if the overall silhouette matches the pattern, Kolmogorov–Smirnov checks the single widest seam gap, and Anderson–Darling inspects the hems and cuffs where wear and tear happen first.

02 Step by step

  1. Inspect the sample visually with a Q-Q (quantile-quantile) plot to develop an initial diagnostic intuition of skewness and tail weight.
  2. State the formal null hypothesis $H_0$: the sample was drawn from a normally distributed population, versus $H_1$: the parent population is non-normal.
  3. For general univariate data ($n \le 5,000$), run the Shapiro–Wilk test as the most statistically powerful omnibus test of normality.
  4. If tail discrepancies or extreme events are the primary operational risk (such as latency SLAs), evaluate the Anderson–Darling test.
  5. If parameters $(\mu, \sigma)$ are estimated from the sample data rather than known beforehand, do not use standard Kolmogorov–Smirnov tables; use the Lilliefors correction.
  6. Synthesise test results with sample size: in very large samples, slight harmless departures trigger $p < 0.05$; in small samples, low power can mask severe non-normality.

03 The maths

  1. Shapiro–Wilk Test Statistic

    $$W = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i X_{(i)}\right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2}$$

    Here $X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \dots \le X_{(n)}$ are ordered sample statistics, and $\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{m}^T \mathbf{V}^{-1}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{m}^T \mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{m}}}$ incorporates the expected values $\mathbf{m}$ and covariance matrix $\mathbf{V}$ of standard normal order statistics. The statistic $W$ lies in $(0, 1]$, where $W = 1$ indicates perfect linearity between sample quantiles and normal quantiles.

  2. Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test Statistic

    $$D = \sup_{x} |F_n(x) - F_0(x)|$$

    Here $F_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}_{X_i \le x}$ is the empirical cumulative distribution function, and $F_0(x)$ is the reference continuous cumulative distribution function. $D$ represents the supremum absolute vertical distance between the empirical step function and theoretical curve.

  3. Anderson–Darling Test Statistic

    $$A^2 = -n - \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{2i - 1}{n} \left[\ln F_0(X_{(i)}) + \ln(1 - F_0(X_{(n+1-i)}))\right]$$

    The Anderson–Darling statistic weights squared discrepancies by the inverse variance function $[F_0(x)(1 - F_0(x))]^{-1}$. This weighting amplifies discrepancies in the lower and upper tails, making $A^2$ far more sensitive to heavy tails and skewness than the unweighted Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic.

04 Key takeaways

  • The Shapiro–Wilk test exhibits the highest overall statistical power across a broad range of non-normal distributions for sample sizes up to several thousand.
  • Standard Kolmogorov–Smirnov assumes a fully specified reference distribution; plugging in sample mean and sample variance requires the Lilliefors correction to avoid conservative bias.
  • Anderson–Darling prioritises tail deviations, making it the preferred diagnostic when detecting heavy tails or extreme-value risk.
  • In huge datasets ($n > 10,000$), tiny, practically trivial deviations from Gaussian perfection will trigger $p < 0.001$; always combine formal tests with Q-Q plots.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 60

# Normal sample vs skewed exponential sample
normal_data = rng.normal(loc=10.0, scale=2.5, size=n)
skewed_data = rng.exponential(scale=2.5, size=n)

# 1. Shapiro-Wilk test (highest omnibus power)
w_norm, p_sw_norm = stats.shapiro(normal_data)
w_skew, p_sw_skew = stats.shapiro(skewed_data)

# 2. Kolmogorov-Smirnov test against fitted normal distribution
ks_skew, p_ks_skew = stats.kstest(skewed_data, 'norm',
    args=(np.mean(skewed_data), np.std(skewed_data, ddof=1)))

# 3. Anderson-Darling test (tail-weighted)
ad_skew = stats.anderson(skewed_data, dist='norm', method='interpolate')

print(f"Normal data: Shapiro W={w_norm:.4f}, p={p_sw_norm:.4f}")
print(f"Skewed data: Shapiro W={w_skew:.4f}, p={p_sw_skew:.4e}")
print(f"Skewed data: KS stat={ks_skew:.4f}, p={p_ks_skew:.4f}")
print(f"Skewed data: Anderson A2={ad_skew.statistic:.3f}, p={ad_skew.pvalue:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Plugging sample estimates of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ into the standard Kolmogorov–Smirnov test without using Lilliefors critical values, producing falsely inflated p-values.
  • Mistaking a non-significant normality test in a small sample ($n < 25$) as positive confirmation that the population is normal.
  • Discarding linear regression models when a massive sample fails a normality test, overlooking that regression coefficient estimators remain asymptotically normal via the central limit theorem.
  • Testing normality exclusively with numbers while skipping graphical Q-Q plots, missing the structural nature of departures (skewness vs kurtosis vs outliers).

06 Check yourself

Q1Which normality test is empirically proven to deliver the highest statistical power against the widest variety of non-normal alternative distributions for moderate sample sizes?

Why: Extensive Monte Carlo studies confirm that the Shapiro–Wilk test, which evaluates the squared correlation between sample order statistics and normal quantiles, offers superior statistical power across diverse alternatives.

Q2What occurs if you test normality with the standard Kolmogorov–Smirnov test after estimating $\mu$ and $\sigma$ from the sample?

Why: Estimating parameters from the sample forces the theoretical CDF closer to the empirical CDF than an independent reference curve would be, shrinking $D$ and inflating p-values unless the Lilliefors correction is applied.

Q3What is the primary advantage of the Anderson–Darling test over the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test?

Why: Anderson–Darling divides deviations by $F_0(x)(1 - F_0(x))$, which prevents tail discrepancies from being compressed to zero near the boundaries and magnifies sensitivity to heavy tails.

Q4Why is a formal normality test often unhelpful when analysing huge datasets ($n = 100,000$)?

Why: With huge sample sizes, test power approaches 100% for microscopic departures that have zero practical impact on downstream estimation or model validity.
