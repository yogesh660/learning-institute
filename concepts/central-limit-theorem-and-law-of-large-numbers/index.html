Central Limit Theorem and Law of Large Numbers

Why averages converge and bell curves appear everywhere: the Law of Large Numbers pins down the mean, while the Central Limit Theorem dictates the shape of sample sums.

01 The idea

The Law of Large Numbers guarantees that sample averages converge to the true population expectation as sample size grows. The Central Limit Theorem goes further, proving that the standardised sum or mean of independent random variables converges to a Gaussian distribution, regardless of the underlying distribution's shape.

Imagine rolling a fair six-sided die. A single roll has a completely flat, discrete uniform distribution: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6 each appear with equal probability 1/6. The true expected value is 3.5. If you roll the die 10 times, your average might be 2.9 or 4.1. But roll it 10,000 times, and the average will be virtually indistinguishable from 3.5. That stability is the Law of Large Numbers (LLN): random noise cancels out across large samples.

Now, instead of one long run, imagine taking 1,000 separate players. Each player rolls 30 dice and computes their own average. If you plot the histogram of those 1,000 averages, something remarkable emerges: the flat uniform distribution has completely vanished. In its place sits a smooth, bell-shaped Gaussian curve centred at 3.5.

This is the Central Limit Theorem (CLT). Even if the original population is skewed, bimodal, or uniform, the distribution of sample averages becomes approximately normal once sample size $n$ is sufficiently large (typically $n \ge 30$). This explains why Gaussian assumptions dominate machine learning: measurement errors and composite features are usually sums of many small, independent underlying effects.

In everyday terms

The Law of Large Numbers tells you that a fleet of ships will on average reach their port; the Central Limit Theorem describes the bell-shaped dispersion pattern of their arrival times.

02 Step by step

  1. Draw independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ from any population with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.
  2. Compute the sample average $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.
  3. Observe the Law of Large Numbers in action: as $n \to \infty$, the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ converges in probability to the true population mean $\mu$.
  4. Apply the Central Limit Theorem: standardise the sum or mean to form $Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$.
  5. Approximate probabilities of sample averages using the standard Gaussian CDF $\Phi(z)$ whenever $n$ is adequately large.

03 The maths

  1. Weak and Strong Laws of Large Numbers

    $$\text{Weak Law (WLLN): } \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) = 0, \qquad \text{Strong Law (SLLN): } P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1$$

    The Weak Law (Khinchin's theorem) guarantees convergence in probability: for any margin $\varepsilon > 0$, the probability of a discrepancy larger than $\varepsilon$ decays to 0. The Strong Law (Kolmogorov's theorem) guarantees almost sure convergence: the sample trajectory itself converges to the constant $\mu$ with probability 1.

  2. The Lindeberg–Lévy Central Limit Theorem

    $$\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma}\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

    For any sequence of i.i.d. random variables with finite variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$, the distribution of the normalised error converges in distribution ($\xrightarrow{d}$) to a standard Gaussian. Equivalently, the unstandardised sample mean follows approximately $\bar{X}_n \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$.

  3. Berry–Esseen Rate of Convergence

    $$\sup_{z \in \mathbb{R}} \big| F_n(z) - \Phi(z) \big| \le \frac{C \cdot \mathbb{E}\big[|X - \mu|^3\big]}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{n}}$$

    The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the maximum discrepancy between the actual CDF of the standardised sample mean $F_n(z)$ and the standard normal CDF $\Phi(z)$. The convergence rate is $O(1/\sqrt{n})$, scaled by the population skewness. Heavily skewed distributions require larger $n$ before the normal approximation becomes accurate.

04 Key takeaways

  • LLN guarantees that empirical risk converges to expected risk in machine learning as training set size grows.
  • CLT applies to the sum or average of random variables, not to the raw data distribution itself; raw data does not magically become normal.
  • Finite variance is an absolute requirement; heavy-tailed distributions like Cauchy have no finite variance and violate the CLT entirely.
  • The spread of sample averages shrinks at the rate $1/\sqrt{n}$, meaning halving estimation error requires quadrupling the sample size.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
# Draw from a heavily skewed Exponential distribution (scale = 2.0)
scale = 2.0
n_samples = 50
n_experiments = 5000

# True parameters: mean = scale, var = scale^2
true_mean = scale
true_se = scale / np.sqrt(n_samples)

# Simulate 5,000 separate sample means of size n = 50
means = rng.exponential(scale=scale, size=(n_experiments, n_samples)).mean(axis=1)

empirical_mean = np.mean(means)
empirical_se = np.std(means, ddof=1)
# Fraction of sample averages falling within 1.96 standard errors of the mean
coverage = np.mean(np.abs(means - true_mean) <= 1.96 * true_se)

print(f"True Mean: {true_mean:.2f}, Simulated Mean: {empirical_mean:.4f}")
print(f"Theoretical SE: {true_se:.4f}, Simulated SE: {empirical_se:.4f}")
print(f"95% Gaussian Coverage: {coverage * 100:.2f}%")

Common pitfalls

  • Believing the raw data becomes normally distributed with more samples; only sample sums and averages become normal.
  • Applying CLT to Cauchy or Pareto distributions with infinite variance where averages wander erratically forever.
  • Assuming $n = 30$ is always sufficient; heavily skewed or zero-inflated distributions can require hundreds or thousands of samples for good Gaussian fits.
  • Confusing standard deviation of the population ($\sigma$) with standard error of the mean ($\sigma / \sqrt{n}$).

06 Check yourself

Q1What happens to the distribution of individual data points $X_i$ as the sample size $n$ increases?

Why: The CLT applies exclusively to sample sums and averages. The raw individual observations continue to reflect their true underlying population distribution regardless of sample size.

Q2If you wish to cut the standard error of a sample mean in half, by what factor must you increase the sample size?

Why: The standard error is $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$. To divide the error by 2, $n$ must increase by a factor of $2^2 = 4$.

Q3Which fundamental condition must a probability distribution satisfy for the classical Lindeberg–Lévy CLT to hold?

Why: The classical CLT requires independent, identically distributed observations with finite mean and finite variance ($\sigma^2 < \infty$). Distributions with infinite variance (like Cauchy) violate CLT.

Q4What is the conceptual difference between the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) and the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)?

Why: LLN pins down the destination ($\bar{X}_n \to \mu$), whereas CLT provides the rate and probability distribution of the fluctuations around that destination ($\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \to \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$).

Q5Which of the following distributions is a celebrated counterexample where sample averages fail to converge under the Law of Large Numbers because it lacks a finite mean?

Why: The Cauchy distribution has heavy polynomial tails and undefined mean and variance. Averaging $n$ Cauchy variables produces the exact same Cauchy spread, never concentrating around a point.

Q6What distinguishes the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN) from the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)?

Why: The Weak Law (Khinchin) proves that $P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. The Strong Law (Kolmogorov) proves that the sample average trajectory converges to $\mu$ with probability 1.

Q7A machine packs bags with mean weight 250 g and standard deviation 5 g. For a box containing 100 independently filled bags, what are the expected total weight and standard deviation of the box?

Why: Expected values add directly ($\mathbb{E}[S] = 100 \times 250 = 25{,}000\text{ g}$). Variances add ($\operatorname{Var}(S) = 100 \times 5^2 = 2{,}500$), so standard deviation is $\sqrt{2{,}500} = 50\text{ g}$.

Q8A simulation averages 64 independent $U(0, 1)$ random variables ($\mu = 0.5, \sigma \approx 0.2887$) across 10,000 runs. What should the empirical standard deviation of these 10,000 means be closest to?

Why: By the CLT, the standard error of the sample mean is $\sigma / \sqrt{n} = 0.2887 / \sqrt{64} = 0.2887 / 8 \approx 0.0361$.

Q9According to the Berry–Esseen theorem, what property of the underlying population primarily governs how quickly sample averages converge to normality?

Why: Berry–Esseen bounds the maximum discrepancy from a normal CDF by $\frac{C \cdot \mathbb{E}[|X - \mu|^3]}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{n}}$. Heavily skewed populations converge much more slowly than symmetric ones.

Q10Server request times have mean $\mu = 40\text{ ms}$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 30\text{ ms}$. For a random sample of $n = 100$ requests, what is the approximate probability that the sample mean latency exceeds $45\text{ ms}$? (Given: $P(Z > 1.67) \approx 0.048$).

Why: Standard error is $\sigma / \sqrt{n} = 30 / \sqrt{100} = 3\text{ ms}$. The $z$-score is $(45 - 40) / 3 = 5/3 \approx 1.67$. Using standard normal tables, $P(Z > 1.67) \approx 0.048$ (or 4.8%).

Q11Battery lifetime has population standard deviation $\sigma = 80\text{ hours}$. What is the minimum sample size $n$ required so that the standard error of the sample mean is at most $4\text{ hours}$?

Why: Setting $\sigma / \sqrt{n} \le 4$ gives $80 / \sqrt{n} \le 4$, so $\sqrt{n} \ge 20$, which implies $n \ge 20^2 = 400$.

Q12Suppose $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution with finite mean $\mu$ but infinite variance $\sigma^2 = \infty$. How does the normalised sample average behave as $n \to \infty$?

Why: The classical Lindeberg–Lévy CLT requires finite variance. When variance is infinite, the generalised CLT states that sums of independent variables converge to Lévy alpha-stable distributions.
