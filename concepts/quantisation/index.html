Model Quantisation: FP16, INT8, and INT4

Slashing model memory and accelerating inference by converting 32-bit floating-point weights and activations into compact 8-bit or 4-bit integers with minimal loss in accuracy.

01 The idea

Modern deep neural networks and large language models contain billions of parameters, demanding tens of gigabytes of expensive high-bandwidth GPU memory (VRAM). Model quantisation compresses 32-bit floating-point numbers (`FP32`) into compact lower-precision representations—such as 16-bit floats (`FP16`/`BF16`), 8-bit integers (`INT8`), or 4-bit formats (`INT4`)—reducing memory footprints by up to 75% and multiplying inference throughput on commodity hardware.

In standard deep learning, every single weight and activation is stored as a 32-bit IEEE 754 float: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 mantissa bits. A 7-billion parameter language model in FP32 consumes $7 \times 10^9 \times 4 \text{ bytes} \approx 28 \text{ GB}$ of VRAM just to store parameter weights! Loading this enormous volume of weights from memory into GPU compute cores during autoregressive generation becomes an acute memory-bandwidth bottleneck.

Fortunately, neural networks are remarkably robust to minor numerical perturbations. The exact value of a weight being $0.18374921$ rather than $0.18$ almost never alters the model's final prediction. **Uniform Quantisation** maps a continuous floating-point range $[x_{\text{min}}, x_{\text{max}}]$ into discrete integer buckets: 1. **Scale factor** ($S$): the real-valued step size represented by each integer increment ($S = \frac{\max(x) - \min(x)}{2^b - 1}$). 2. **Zero-point** ($Z$): the integer corresponding to real-valued zero ($0.0$). 3. **Quantise**: $q = \operatorname{clip}(\operatorname{round}(x / S) + Z, q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}})$. 4. **Dequantise**: $\hat{x} = S \cdot (q - Z)$. In symmetric quantisation, the range is centred around zero ($x_{\text{max}} = -x_{\text{min}}$), setting $Z = 0$ and drastically simplifying integer matrix multiplication kernels.

Two primary methodologies govern deployment: **Post-Training Quantisation (PTQ)** quantises an already trained FP32 model directly using a small calibration dataset without retraining. For aggressive compressions (like 4-bit weights in GPTQ and AWQ), **Quantisation-Aware Training (QAT)** simulates low-precision integer rounding in the forward pass during training. Because rounding has a derivative of zero almost everywhere, QAT employs the **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)** to pass gradients directly through the rounding operator during backpropagation, allowing weights to adapt naturally to low-precision boundaries.

In everyday terms

Like converting a high-resolution 24-bit colour photograph to an 8-bit 256-colour indexed palette: while colour gradients become discrete steps, the visual composition remains indistinguishable to the human eye while cutting file size to a fraction.

02 Step by step

  1. Select precision and target format: choose FP16/BF16 (2 bytes/param) for general GPU acceleration; INT8 (1 byte/param) for edge or CPU deployment; or INT4 (0.5 bytes/param) for hosting large language models in consumer VRAM.
  2. Gather calibration data: run 100 to 512 representative domain inputs through the network to record the dynamic activation ranges ($x_{\text{min}}, x_{\text{max}}$) across layers.
  3. Compute scale and zero-point parameters: calculate scale $S = \frac{\max(x) - \min(x)}{2^b - 1}$ and zero-point $Z$ per tensor, per channel, or per group.
  4. Quantise parameters and activations: map continuous values to discrete integers using $q = \operatorname{clip}(\operatorname{round}(x / S) + Z, q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}})$.
  5. Deploy accelerated integer matrix kernels: execute matrix operations using native INT8/INT4 Tensor Core instructions, accumulating sums in 32-bit integer registers before dequantising outputs back to float.
  6. Validate accuracy against baseline: measure validation perplexity or task accuracy to ensure the quantised model remains within acceptable tolerances (typically $< 0.5\%$ accuracy loss for INT8).

03 The maths

  1. Uniform affine quantisation and dequantisation

    $$q = \operatorname{clip}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{x}{S} \right\rceil + Z, \, q_{\text{min}}, \, q_{\text{max}}\right), \qquad \hat{x} = S \cdot (q - Z)$$

    $x \in \mathbb{R}$ is the original continuous floating-point value, $S \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the scale factor, $Z \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the zero-point offset, $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ denotes round-to-nearest-integer, and $[q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}]$ are integer bounds (e.g. $[-128, 127]$ for signed INT8).

  2. Scale calculation from dynamic tensor range

    $$S = \frac{\alpha - \beta}{q_{\text{max}} - q_{\text{min}}}, \qquad Z = \left\lfloor \frac{-\beta}{S} \right\rceil + q_{\text{min}}$$

    $[\beta, \alpha]$ is the dynamic range of the tensor (often calibrated using percentiles to mitigate outlier distortion). In symmetric quantisation, $\beta = -\alpha$, which enforces $Z = 0$, simplifying integer dot-product computations.

  3. Straight-Through Estimator (STE) for Quantisation-Aware Training

    $$\frac{\partial \hat{x}}{\partial x} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x \in [\beta, \alpha] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

    Because the derivative of the rounding function $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ is zero almost everywhere, standard gradient descent cannot update parameters. The STE replaces the zero derivative with the identity pass-through ($1.0$) within the valid dynamic range.

04 Key takeaways

  • Quantisation reduces memory footprint by $2\times$ (FP16), $4\times$ (INT8), or $8\times$ (INT4), enabling large models to fit into consumer GPU VRAM.
  • Symmetric quantisation sets zero-point $Z = 0$, eliminating zero-point offset arithmetic during high-throughput matrix multiplications.
  • Quantisation-Aware Training (QAT) uses the Straight-Through Estimator to adapt neural weights to low-precision discrete grids, preserving accuracy where post-training quantisation degrades.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Demonstrate symmetric 8-bit quantisation on synthetic weight tensor
np.random.seed(42)
N = 100
# Generate FP32 weights (mean 0, std 0.5)
W_fp32 = (np.random.randn(N) * 0.5).astype(np.float32)

# 1. Symmetric INT8 scale: map [-max_val, max_val] to [-127, 127]
max_abs = float(np.max(np.abs(W_fp32)))
scale = max_abs / 127.0

# 2. Quantise to signed 8-bit integers
W_int8 = np.clip(np.round(W_fp32 / scale), -127, 127).astype(np.int8)

# 3. Dequantise back to floating-point representation
W_reconstructed = W_int8.astype(np.float32) * scale

mse_noise = float(np.mean((W_fp32 - W_reconstructed)**2))
bytes_fp32 = W_fp32.nbytes
bytes_int8 = W_int8.nbytes

print(f"FP32 memory footprint: {bytes_fp32} bytes")
print(f"INT8 memory footprint: {bytes_int8} bytes ({bytes_fp32 / bytes_int8:.1f}x compression)")
print(f"Quantisation Mean Squared Error: {mse_noise:.6f}")
print(f"Sample weight 0: original = {W_fp32[0]:.4f} -> dequantised = {W_reconstructed[0]:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Activation outlier collapse: large language models exhibit extreme outlier features in specific channels ($100\times$ larger than average); naive per-tensor quantisation destroys accuracy unless per-channel or group-wise scaling is applied.
  • Benchmarking latency on unsupported hardware: executing INT8 models on hardware without native integer compute instructions requires dequantising to float before every multiplication, resulting in slower execution.
  • Quantising sensitive layers: aggressively quantising initial convolutional embeddings, attention softmax denominators, or final classification logits to 4-bit often causes severe divergence; keep sensitive operations in FP16.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does symmetric quantisation ($Z = 0$) provide computational advantages over asymmetric affine quantisation?

Why: When $Z=0$, dequantisation simplifies to $\hat{x} = S \cdot q$. The product of two matrices becomes $S_1 S_2 (Q_1 Q_2)$, allowing hardware to perform pure integer GEMM without accumulating zero-point cross-terms.

Q2What is the function of the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) in Quantisation-Aware Training?

Why: Rounding $\lfloor x \rceil$ has derivative zero everywhere (and undefined at half-integers). Without STE, backpropagation would produce zero gradients, preventing the optimiser from adjusting weights.

Q3Why do Large Language Models (LLMs) suffer severe accuracy degradation under naive per-tensor INT8 quantisation?

Why: Research (such as LLM.int8()) showed that Transformers develop emergent outlier activation dimensions with values $100\times$ larger than average. A global scale factor stretches the bucket width, collapsing normal features to zero unless per-channel or group-wise scaling is used.

Q4How does 4-bit weight quantisation (INT4) reduce inference latency in large language model text generation?

Why: Autoregressive text generation is memory-bandwidth bound: the GPU spends most of its time fetching weights from VRAM to compute a single token. Shrinking weights to 4 bits cuts bytes read by $4\times$ compared to FP16, speeding up generation.
