Stemming vs lemmatisation

Words occur in diverse inflected, derived, and conjugated surface forms across grammatical moods, numbers, and tenses ("organise", "organises", "organising", "organised"). Stemming and lemmatisation are two distinct linguistic normalisation techniques designed to collapse these surface variants into a common base representation, consolidating vocabulary size and enhancing document recall. Stemming applies heuristic, rule-based suffix-stripping algorithms (such as the Porter and Snowball stemmers) that chop word endings into crude root stems ("organ"), often yielding non-dictionary words and suffering from over-stemming and under-stemming. Lemmatisation applies full morphological analysis using comprehensive lexical databases (such as WordNet) and Part-of-Speech context to reduce words to their authentic canonical dictionary lemma ("better" $\to$ "good", "was" $\to$ "be").

01 The idea

If a user searches an e-commerce catalogue for "running shoes", they expect the engine to return products described as "runs", "runner", and "ran". If a sentiment classifier encounters the word "frustrated", it should leverage everything it learned about "frustrates", "frustrating", and "frustration". Without morphological normalisation, each inflected variant becomes an independent vocabulary dimension, fragmenting training counts and causing severe sparsity. But should we chop word endings with heuristic rules, or look them up in a morphological dictionary? Understanding the speed, precision, and grammatical trade-offs between stemming and lemmatisation is crucial for designing robust text processing pipelines.

The morphology challenge: In natural languages, inflectional morphology modifies words to express grammatical features (tense, person, number, case) without changing the core semantic meaning ("walk", "walks", "walked", "walking"). Derivational morphology forms completely new words from existing roots ("organise" $\to$ "organisation"). In un-normalised text, these variations inflate the vocabulary exponentially, spreading statistical frequencies thin across dozens of rare tokens.

Stemming: Heuristic rule-based suffix chopping: In 1980, Martin Porter introduced the Porter Stemmer, which remains the most widely deployed stemming algorithm in computer science. Porter stemming consists of five sequential phases of hand-crafted cascading rewrite rules: for instance, mapping "sses" to "ss" ("caresses" $\to$ "caress"), "ies" to "i" ("ponies" $\to$ "poni"), and stripping "ing" or "ed" if the preceding stem contains a vowel ("motoring" $\to$ "motor"). Stemmers are blindingly fast because they rely purely on string pattern matching without lexical dictionaries. However, stems are often non-words ("poni", "commun"), and stemmers cannot handle irregular English grammar ("went" cannot be stemmed to "go").

The twin pitfalls: Over-stemming and under-stemming: Stemmers suffer from two fundamental error modes: (1) Over-stemming occurs when two words with completely different semantic meanings are conflated to the identical root stem (e.g. "universe", "universal", and "university" all stem to "univers", erroneously equating higher education with astrophysics). (2) Under-stemming occurs when two words expressing the exact same semantic concept fail to stem to the same root (e.g. "European" and "Europe" yield "european" and "europ", remaining disjoint in the index).

Lemmatisation: Lexical analysis with Part-of-Speech context: Lemmatisation resolves the shortcomings of stemming by performing complete morphological analysis. A lemmatiser requires two inputs: the surface word and its grammatical Part-of-Speech (POS) tag. By consulting comprehensive lexical databases like WordNet, a lemmatiser handles irregular inflections with ease: "better" with POS=adjective becomes "good", "saw" with POS=verb becomes "see", whereas "saw" with POS=noun remains "saw" (the cutting tool). Lemmatisation guarantees that the resulting base is a legitimate, human-interpretable dictionary headword ("lemma"), providing superior semantic fidelity at the expense of computational lookup overhead.

In everyday terms

Think of pruning and tending a large overgrown botanical garden. Stemming is like a groundskeeper armed with a petrol-powered hedge trimmer who walks along hedges blindly shearing three inches off the end of every branch: it is extraordinarily fast and reduces the wild bush to a tidy uniform height, but occasionally it shears off valuable roses, cuts electrical garden cables, or leaves splintered bare twigs (non-words like "poni"). Lemmatisation is like a skilled master horticulturist who examines each plant individually with pruning shears, identifies the exact plant species from its tag (Part-of-Speech context), traces the branches back to their healthy botanical joints, and trims them neatly to their canonical root system. The master horticulturist takes significantly longer than the petrol hedge trimmer, but every plant remains pristine and identifiable.

03 The maths

  1. Porter Stemmer measure of syllable complexity

    [C](VC)^m [V], \quad m = \text{measure of syllable alternation}

    The Porter stemmer represents any word as an optional initial consonant sequence $[C]$, followed by $m$ alternating vowel-consonant sequences $(VC)^m$, followed by an optional trailing vowel sequence $[V]$. Suffix stripping rules fire only if the remaining stem satisfies a minimum syllable measure $m > 0$ or $m > 1$, preventing short root words like "bed" or "sing" from being destroyed into single letters.

  2. Over-stemming and Under-stemming error rates

    E_{\text{over}} = \frac{|\{(w_i, w_j) : \operatorname{stem}(w_i) = \operatorname{stem}(w_j) \land \text{lemma}(w_i) \neq \text{lemma}(w_j)\}|}{\text{Total Pairs}}, \quad E_{\text{under}} = \frac{|\{(w_i, w_j) : \operatorname{stem}(w_i) \neq \operatorname{stem}(w_j) \land \text{lemma}(w_i) = \text{lemma}(w_j)\}|}{\text{Total Related Pairs}}

    Over-stemming measures false conflations (unrelated words mapped to identical stems, harming search precision), while under-stemming measures missed conflations (related words mapped to different stems, harming search recall).

  3. Vocabulary reduction efficiency ratio

    \text{Compression Ratio} = 1 - \frac{|\mathcal{V}_{\text{normalised}}|}{|\mathcal{V}_{\text{raw}}|}, \quad |\mathcal{V}_{\text{stemmed}}| \le |\mathcal{V}_{\text{lemmatised}}| < |\mathcal{V}_{\text{raw}}|

    Stemming achieves higher vocabulary compression than lemmatisation because aggressive suffix stripping frequently collapses distinct semantic words together. Lemmatisation balances vocabulary compression while strictly preserving dictionary headwords.

04 Key takeaways

  • Stemming applies heuristic suffix stripping rules without dictionaries, producing crude roots that are often non-words.
  • Lemmatisation applies full morphological analysis using lexical databases and Part-of-Speech tags, guaranteeing valid dictionary lemmas.
  • Over-stemming erroneously conflates semantically unrelated words (e.g. "university" and "universe"), reducing retrieval precision.
  • Under-stemming fails to group related morphological inflections (e.g. "Europe" and "European"), reducing retrieval recall.
  • Stemming is favoured in high-speed search indexing; lemmatisation is preferred for syntactic parsing and semantic understanding.

05 In code

# Comparative implementation of Stemming vs Lemmatisation in pure Python

# 1. Simplified rule-based suffix stripper (mini-Porter style rules)
def simple_porter_stem(word):
    w = word.lower()
    # Step 1a: plurals and past participles
    if w.endswith('sses'): w = w[:-2]
    elif w.endswith('ies'): w = w[:-2]  # ponies -> poni
    elif w.endswith('ss'): pass
    elif w.endswith('s') and len(w) > 2: w = w[:-1]
    
    # Step 1b: -ed, -ing
    if w.endswith('eed'):
        if len(w) > 4: w = w[:-1]
    elif w.endswith('ed'):
        stem = w[:-2]
        if any(c in 'aeiou' for c in stem): w = stem
    elif w.endswith('ing'):
        stem = w[:-3]
        if any(c in 'aeiou' for c in stem): w = stem
        
    # Step 2: derivational suffixes
    if w.endswith('ational'): w = w[:-7] + 'ate'  # relational -> relate
    elif w.endswith('tional'): w = w[:-6] + 'tion'
    elif w.endswith('izer') or w.endswith('iser'): w = w[:-4] + 'ise'
    elif w.endswith('ly'): w = w[:-2]
    
    return w

# 2. Dictionary-based Lemmatiser with POS-context support
# Minimal WordNet-style irregular inflection dictionary
lemma_dict = {
    # Verbs: (word, 'v') -> lemma
    ('went', 'v'): 'go', ('gone', 'v'): 'go', ('going', 'v'): 'go', ('goes', 'v'): 'go',
    ('saw', 'v'): 'see', ('seen', 'v'): 'see', ('seeing', 'v'): 'see', ('sees', 'v'): 'see',
    ('running', 'v'): 'run', ('ran', 'v'): 'run', ('runs', 'v'): 'run',
    ('studying', 'v'): 'study', ('studied', 'v'): 'study', ('studies', 'v'): 'study',
    # Nouns: (word, 'n') -> lemma
    ('mice', 'n'): 'mouse', ('geese', 'n'): 'goose', ('feet', 'n'): 'foot',
    ('ponies', 'n'): 'pony', ('studies', 'n'): 'study', ('saw', 'n'): 'saw',
    # Adjectives: (word, 'a') -> lemma
    ('better', 'a'): 'good', ('best', 'a'): 'good', ('worse', 'a'): 'bad'
}

def simple_lemmatize(word, pos='n'):
    w = word.lower()
    # Direct irregular dictionary lookup
    if (w, pos) in lemma_dict:
        return lemma_dict[(w, pos)]
        
    # Regular inflection rules
    if pos == 'n' and w.endswith('s') and len(w) > 3:
        if w.endswith('ies'): return w[:-3] + 'y'
        return w[:-1]
    if pos == 'v' and w.endswith('ing') and len(w) > 4:
        return w[:-3]
    return w

# 3. Test words showing key distinctions
test_cases = [
    ("running", "v", "Regular verb inflection"),
    ("went", "v", "Irregular verb"),
    ("ponies", "n", "Plural noun"),
    ("better", "a", "Irregular comparative adjective"),
    ("saw", "v", "Ambiguous: past tense verb 'to see'"),
    ("saw", "n", "Ambiguous: noun tool 'saw'"),
    ("university", "n", "Over-stemming candidate"),
    ("universe", "n", "Over-stemming candidate")
]

print(f"{'Input Word':<12} | {'POS':<4} | {'Stem (Porter)':<15} | {'Lemma (WordNet)':<15} | {'Note':<30}")
print("-" * 85)

for word, pos, note in test_cases:
    stem_val = simple_porter_stem(word)
    lemma_val = simple_lemmatize(word, pos)
    print(f"{word:<12} | {pos:<4} | {stem_val:<15} | {lemma_val:<15} | {note:<30}")

print("\nKey observations:")
print("1. Stemming turns 'ponies' into non-word 'poni'; Lemmatisation returns valid dictionary word 'pony'.")
print("2. Stemming fails on irregular 'went' ('went'); Lemmatisation correctly maps to root 'go'.")
print("3. POS context allows Lemmatisation to distinguish verb 'saw' ('see') from noun 'saw' ('saw').")

Common pitfalls

  • Lemmatising without providing Part-of-Speech tags: Most lemmatisers default to Noun if no POS tag is supplied; attempting to lemmatise the verb "running" without POS='v' leaves it un-lemmatised as "running" instead of "run".
  • Over-stemming in specialized domains: Applying aggressive stemmers to medical or legal documents conflates distinct terminology (e.g. "policy" and "police", or "medical" and "medication"), corrupting domain retrieval.
  • Assuming stemming produces valid dictionary words: Stemming outputs like "commun", "poni", and "relat" are algorithmically convenient index keys, but cannot be displayed to end users in polished search results.
  • Computational latency bottlenecks with lemmatisation: Lemmatisation requires dictionary hash lookups and prior POS tagging, running orders of magnitude slower than simple regex stemming on multi-gigabyte corpora.
  • Irregular verb failure in stemmers: Rule-based stemmers cannot map irregular past tenses ("went" $\to$ "go", "sang" $\to$ "sing", "mice" $\to$ "mouse") because irregular inflections require lexical dictionary mapping.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary operational difference between stemming and lemmatisation?

Why: Stemming chops word endings using cascading heuristic string matching rules (producing roots like "poni" or "comput"). Lemmatisation consults a morphological lexicon (WordNet) with grammatical context to output valid dictionary headwords ("pony", "compute").

Q2What does "over-stemming" mean in natural language processing?

Why: Over-stemming is a false positive conflation error: the stemmer strips too aggressively, equating distinct concepts (such as "universal" and "university") under the same root, degrading search precision.

Q3Why does effective lemmatisation require providing the Part-of-Speech (POS) tag of a word?

Why: Many English surface forms are grammatically ambiguous. Without POS context, a lemmatiser cannot know whether "leaves" is the plural of noun "leaf" or the third-person present form of verb "leave".

Q4Under what scenario is stemming typically preferred over lemmatisation in production systems?

Why: Stemming executes orders of magnitude faster than lemmatisation because it avoids POS tagging and dictionary lookups. For web search engines indexing billions of documents, its speed and recall improvements make it the industry standard.
