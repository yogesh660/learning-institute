Sequential testing and the peeking problem

Continuous monitoring ("peeking") at incoming experimental data in classical fixed-horizon A/B testing inflates False Positive Rates (Type I error) from a nominal 5% to over 30–40%. Because the test statistic performs a random walk across time, repeatedly evaluating whether p < 0.05 creates multiple testing across time, virtually guaranteeing that chance fluctuations will trigger a false rejection. Sequential testing frameworks—including Group Sequential Designs (O'Brien-Fleming alpha spending), Wald's Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT), and Always-Valid p-values via martingales (mSPRT)—allow continuous experiment evaluation and early stopping while strictly controlling overall Type I error.

01 The idea

In digital product experimentation, product managers cannot resist checking experiment dashboards every morning. If the dashboard flashes "Statistically Significant (p < 0.05)" on Day 3, the temptation to declare victory and stop the experiment immediately is overwhelming. What happens if you stop an A/B test early the moment p < 0.05? You have committed the "peeking problem", drastically corrupting your statistical guarantees. How can data teams safely monitor live experiments and stop early for massive wins without destroying statistical validity?

The Random Walk of the p-Value: Under the null hypothesis (two identical variants), the difference in sample means is a stochastic Wiener process (Brownian motion). Even though the true effect is zero, random noise causes the difference to occasionally spike into the "rejection zone" before reverting to zero. If you only look once at sample size N, the chance of being in the rejection zone is 5%. But if you look every single day, you are giving random noise 30 chances to breach the threshold! Over a 30-day experiment, the true false alarm rate exceeds 35%.

Alpha Spending Functions: You have a total budget of \alpha = 0.05 to spend across the lifetime of the experiment. If you want to peek at multiple interim milestones, you must "spend" a tiny fraction of alpha early on and reserve the bulk for the end. The O'Brien-Fleming boundary sets extremely conservative early thresholds (e.g. requiring z > 4.5, p < 0.0001 on Day 1), ensuring that only colossal effects stop the test prematurely.

Always-Valid p-Values and Martingales (mSPRT): Developed by Robbins and modernised by tech platforms (Netflix, experimentation platforms), mixture Sequential Probability Ratio Tests (mSPRT) construct test statistics that are mathematical martingales. By applying Ville's inequality, an always-valid p-value can be checked continuously after every single user with an ironclad mathematical guarantee: P(exists t : p_t <= \alpha) <= \alpha.

In everyday terms

Imagine flipping a fair coin 10,000 times. By the law of large numbers, the percentage of heads will be close to 50% at the end. However, early on (e.g. at flip 5), you might see 4 heads and 1 tail (80% heads). If you allow yourself to stop the game whenever heads leads by 3 flips and declare "the coin is biased toward heads!", you will always find a moment to stop and "prove" the coin is biased. Fixed-horizon testing requires waiting until all 10,000 flips conclude. Sequential testing allows stopping early only if the coin produces a run of heads so astronomical that no fair coin could ever produce it.

03 The maths

  1. Type I Error Rate Inflation Under Repeated Peeking

    \alpha_{\text{inflated}} = P_{H_0}\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^K \big\{ |Z_k| \ge z_{\alpha/2} \big\} \right) \approx 1 - (1 - \alpha)^K \quad \text{(upper bound)}, \qquad \lim_{K \to \infty} \alpha_{\text{inflated}} = 1.0

    Because the sequence of test statistics Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_K forms a correlated stochastic process, repeated evaluations against an unadjusted threshold cause Type I error to inflate rapidly with the number of looks K.

  2. O'Brien-Fleming Alpha Spending Boundary

    c(t) = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{t}}, \qquad \alpha^*(t) = 2 \left( 1 - \Phi\left(\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{t}}\right) \right), \quad t = \frac{n}{N_{\max}} \in (0, 1]

    The O'Brien-Fleming boundary scales inversely with the square root of the information fraction t. Early in the experiment (small t), the required critical z-score is very high, guarding against premature false rejections while spending minimal alpha.

  3. Mixture Sequential Probability Ratio Test (mSPRT)

    \Lambda_n = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{f(x_i; \theta)}{f(x_i; 0)} \, dH(\theta), \qquad P_{H_0}\left( \exists n \ge 1 : \Lambda_n \ge \frac{1}{\alpha} \right) \le \alpha

    By mixing likelihood ratios over a prior distribution H(\theta) (typically Gaussian), \Lambda_n is a non-negative martingale under H_0 with expectation 1. Ville's inequality guarantees that the probability of ever exceeding 1/\alpha at ANY stopping time is bounded by \alpha.

04 Key takeaways

  • Repeatedly checking p-values on accumulating data inflates Type I error rates from 5% to 30% or more.
  • The peeking problem occurs because the difference in sample means executes a random walk that frequently touches significance thresholds by chance.
  • Group Sequential Designs (O'Brien-Fleming) adjust critical boundaries across looks, requiring extreme evidence early on to preserve overall alpha.
  • Martingale-based tests (mSPRT) provide always-valid p-values, allowing continuous real-time monitoring and stopping with strict mathematical guarantees.

05 In code

python
"""
The Peeking Problem in A/B Testing vs O'Brien-Fleming Sequential Testing
Demonstrates false positive rate inflation under naive monitoring
and how sequential boundaries restore nominal alpha = 0.05.
"""

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

np.random.seed(42)
num_simulations = 2000
sample_size_max = 500
num_looks = 10  # Peek every 50 observations
look_points = np.linspace(50, sample_size_max, num_looks, dtype=int)

# Simulate A/A tests (True effect = 0.0, both groups N(0, 1))
naive_false_positives = 0
fixed_horizon_false_positives = 0
obrien_fleming_false_positives = 0

z_alpha_fixed = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)  # 1.96

for _ in range(num_simulations):
    # Generate A and B observations
    a = np.random.normal(0, 1, sample_size_max)
    b = np.random.normal(0, 1, sample_size_max)
    
    # 1. Fixed Horizon Test (Only evaluate at the very end n = sample_size_max)
    z_final = (np.mean(b) - np.mean(a)) / np.sqrt(2.0 / sample_size_max)
    if abs(z_final) >= z_alpha_fixed:
        fixed_horizon_false_positives += 1
        
    # 2. Naive Peeking (Check at every look using unadjusted z >= 1.96)
    naive_stopped = False
    of_stopped = False
    
    for look_idx, n in enumerate(look_points):
        # Current z-statistic
        sub_a = a[:n]
        sub_b = b[:n]
        z_k = (np.mean(sub_b) - np.mean(sub_a)) / np.sqrt(2.0 / n)
        
        # Check Naive Threshold
        if not naive_stopped and abs(z_k) >= z_alpha_fixed:
            naive_false_positives += 1
            naive_stopped = True
            
        # Check O'Brien-Fleming Threshold
        # t = information fraction n / N_max
        t_frac = n / sample_size_max
        c_t = z_alpha_fixed / np.sqrt(t_frac)
        
        if not of_stopped and abs(z_k) >= c_t:
            obrien_fleming_false_positives += 1
            of_stopped = True

rate_fixed = (fixed_horizon_false_positives / num_simulations) * 100
rate_naive = (naive_false_positives / num_simulations) * 100
rate_of = (obrien_fleming_false_positives / num_simulations) * 100

print(f"=== A/A Simulation Results ({num_simulations} simulated experiments, Nominal Alpha = 5.0%) ===")
print(f"1. Fixed Horizon (Check once at end):      {rate_fixed:5.2f}% False Positives (Controlled!)")
print(f"2. Naive Daily Peeking (Check every look): {rate_naive:5.2f}% False Positives (Dramatically Inflated!)")
print(f"3. O'Brien-Fleming Sequential Boundary:    {rate_of:5.2f}% False Positives (Controlled at ~5%!)")
print("\nO'Brien-Fleming Critical Z-Score Trajectory:")
for n in look_points:
    t = n / sample_size_max
    c = z_alpha_fixed / np.sqrt(t)
    p_thresh = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(c))
    print(f"  n = {n:3d} (t={t:.2f}) -> Required z >= {c:.2f} (p < {p_thresh:.5f})")

Common pitfalls

  • Stopping an experiment early for significance while using fixed-horizon p-values: This guarantees an elevated false positive rate and inflates estimated effect sizes (the winner's curse).
  • Extending an experiment because it was 'almost significant': Post-hoc sample extension is peeking in reverse; it also severely inflates false positive rates.
  • Failing to define N_max upfront in sequential testing: Group sequential boundaries depend on the maximum planned sample size N_max; without it, information fractions cannot be calculated.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does checking an A/B test daily and stopping as soon as p < 0.05 inflate the False Positive Rate?

Why: Continuous peeking creates multiple testing across time. Even when the null hypothesis is true, random Brownian fluctuations will cross the unadjusted z=1.96 boundary at some point with high probability.

Q2How does the O'Brien-Fleming boundary prevent false positive inflation during early looks?

Why: O'Brien-Fleming sets critical threshold c(t) = z_{\alpha/2} / \sqrt{t}, spending negligible alpha during early looks and preserving the majority of the alpha budget for the final evaluation.

Q3What mathematical tool allows mSPRT to compute 'always-valid' p-values for continuous monitoring?

Why: The likelihood ratio in mSPRT is a non-negative martingale under H_0. Ville's inequality bounds the probability of the supremum over all time steps, guaranteeing strict Type I error control regardless of when peeking occurs.
