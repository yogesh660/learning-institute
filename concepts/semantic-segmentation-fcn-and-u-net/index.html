Semantic segmentation: FCN and U-Net

Semantic segmentation assigns a categorical semantic label to every individual pixel in an image, generating dense spatial masks for medical diagnosis, autonomous driving drivable-area detection, and satellite terrain mapping. Jonathan Long et al.'s Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) established the deep segmentation paradigm by converting dense classification layers into $1 \times 1$ convolutions and introducing transposed convolutions for learnable spatial upsampling. Olaf Ronneberger et al.'s U-Net introduced a symmetric contracting encoder and expansive decoder architecture connected by direct skip pathways that concatenate high-resolution shallow spatial details with deep semantic context, resolving the fundamental tension between spatial localization and semantic abstraction.

01 The idea

Bounding boxes are useful, but for many safety-critical vision tasks, a rectangle is too coarse. An autonomous car navigating a narrow city street cannot simply know that an obstacle is roughly bounded by a $2 \times 3$ metre box; it needs to know the exact curving boundary of the asphalt kerb down to the single pixel. A surgical robot cutting a tumour cannot risk a bounding box estimate; it must delineate diseased tissue margins with sub-millimetre precision. Semantic segmentation delivers this dense per-pixel understanding, bridging high-level semantic recognition with low-level spatial geometry.

From image classification to dense per-pixel prediction: Standard CNN classifiers (such as VGG or ResNet) downsample input images through successive pooling and strided convolutions, collapsing spatial dimensions into a single $1 \times 1 \times C$ embedding vector connected to a fully connected layer. While this destroys spatial coordinates to achieve translation invariance for classification, segmentation requires translation equivariance: if an object shifts five pixels to the left, its output mask must also shift five pixels to the left. In 2015, Long et al. showed that any fully connected layer can be viewed as a $1 \times 1$ convolution covering the entire receptive field. Replacing fully connected layers with convolutions yields a Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) capable of processing arbitrary input resolutions and outputting 2D spatial heatmap logits.

The upsampling challenge: Transposed convolutions: A deep convolutional backbone downsamples an image by a factor of 32 ($H/32 \times W/32$). To produce a dense mask matching the input resolution $H \times W$, feature maps must be upsampled. While bilinear interpolation can resize matrices, it cannot learn data-dependent reconstruction filters. Transposed convolutions (also known as fractionally strided convolutions or deconvolution) reverse the forward convolution stride: they expand feature dimensions by interleaving zeros between activations and applying learnable convolution kernels, allowing the network to learn optimal spatial reconstruction filters during backpropagation.

The spatial localization dilemma: Deep layers in a CNN possess expansive receptive fields capable of recognizing complex semantics ("this region is a human lung"), but their coarse spatial resolution blurs boundaries and obliterates fine anatomical details. Conversely, shallow layers possess fine spatial resolution capable of pinpointing crisp boundaries, but lack semantic depth (they perceive only raw edges and colour gradients). How can a network be both semantically wise and spatially precise?

U-Net's symmetric skip connection architecture: Ronneberger et al. answered this in 1995 with the U-Net architecture. U-Net is structured as a symmetric U-shaped topology: a contracting path (encoder) that captures contextual semantic representations through repeated convolutions and max-pooling, paired with an expansive path (decoder) that restores spatial resolution through repeated transposed convolutions. Crucially, U-Net introduces horizontal skip connections: feature maps from the encoder are copied and concatenated channel-wise directly into the corresponding decoder tier. The decoder therefore receives both high-resolution spatial boundary cues from the encoder and rich semantic representations from deep bottleneck layers, synthesizing sharp, pixel-accurate segmentation boundaries.

In everyday terms

Imagine an architect tasked with restoring an intricate, centuries-old cathedral mosaic that has suffered minor earthquake damage. The encoder path is like an art historian stepping backward across the cathedral plaza: with each step back, the individual mosaic tiles blur together, but the historian grasps the grand thematic composition—understanding that this section depicts a saint and that section depicts an archway. If the historian tries to repaint the mosaic from memory from fifty metres away, the boundaries will be blurry and misshapen. U-Net's skip connection is like a master craftsman on scaffolding who takes high-resolution Polaroid snapshots of the individual tile grout lines before stepping back. As the historian directs the restoration from afar (semantic decoder), the craftsman injects the exact high-resolution Polaroid photographs of the original tile boundaries (encoder skip connections) directly into the plastering stage. The resulting restoration captures both grand artistic intent and razor-sharp tile alignment.

03 The maths

  1. Pixel-wise cross-entropy loss with class weights

    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{c=1}^K w_c\, y_{i, c} \log(p_{i, c}), \quad p_{i, c} = \frac{\exp(z_{i, c})}{\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(z_{i, k})}

    Here $N = H \times W$ is total pixels, $K$ is the number of semantic classes, $y_{i, c} \in \{0, 1\}$ is ground-truth indicator, $p_{i, c}$ is the predicted softmax probability for pixel $i$ and class $c$, and $w_c$ is an inverse frequency weight to prevent common background pixels from overpowering rare foreground categories.

  2. Sørensen-Dice coefficient and Soft Dice Loss

    \text{Dice} = \frac{2 \sum_{i=1}^N p_i y_i + \epsilon}{\sum_{i=1}^N p_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N y_i^2 + \epsilon}, \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \text{Dice}

    The Dice loss directly mirrors the continuous Intersection over Union metric. By operating on probability outputs $p_i \in [0, 1]$, Dice loss is differentiable and remains robust under extreme class imbalance (e.g. when a brain lesion occupies only $0.1\%$ of total image pixels). Constant $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero.

  3. Transposed convolution spatial dimension mapping

    O = (I - 1) \cdot s - 2p + k + p_{\text{out}}

    Here $I$ is input dimension, $s$ is stride, $p$ is padding, $k$ is kernel size, and $p_{\text{out}}$ is optional output padding. For standard $2\times$ upsampling with $k=2, s=2, p=0$, the output dimension doubles exactly: $O = (I - 1) \cdot 2 + 2 = 2I$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Semantic segmentation outputs dense categorical labels for every pixel in an image, preserving spatial geometry.
  • Fully Convolutional Networks (FCN) replace dense linear layers with $1 \times 1$ convolutions to process arbitrary input dimensions.
  • U-Net's symmetric encoder-decoder architecture pairs contracting context extraction with expansive spatial resolution recovery.
  • Encoder-decoder skip connections concatenate shallow spatial boundary details with deep semantic representations, resolving spatial blurring.
  • Combining Cross-Entropy with Soft Dice Loss ensures robust training on segmentation tasks with severe foreground-background class imbalance.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Simulate a minimal U-Net style encoder-decoder forward pass with skip connection
np.random.seed(42)

# Input image tensor: batch=1, channels=1, height=16, width=16
H, W = 16, 16
x = np.random.randn(1, 1, H, W).astype(np.float32)

# 1. Encoder Stage: 3x3 conv with padding, ReLU, and 2x2 max pooling
# Filter weights: conv1 (1 in -> 4 out), stride=1, pad=1
w_conv1 = np.random.randn(4, 1, 3, 3).astype(np.float32) * 0.1

def conv2d_same(inp, weights):
    B, Cin, iH, iW = inp.shape
    Cout, _, kH, kW = weights.shape
    pad = kH // 2
    padded = np.pad(inp, ((0,0), (0,0), (pad,pad), (pad,pad)), mode='constant')
    out = np.zeros((B, Cout, iH, iW), dtype=np.float32)
    for b in range(B):
        for c_out in range(Cout):
            for r in range(iH):
                for c in range(iW):
                    patch = padded[b, :, r:r+kH, c:c+kW]
                    out[b, c_out, r, c] = np.sum(patch * weights[c_out])
    return out

# Forward encoder conv
feat_enc = np.maximum(0.0, conv2d_same(x, w_conv1))  # shape (1, 4, 16, 16) - SKIP CONNECTION SOURCE

# Max pooling 2x2 (stride 2) -> downsamples to 8x8
def maxpool2d(inp):
    B, C, iH, iW = inp.shape
    out = np.zeros((B, C, iH // 2, iW // 2), dtype=np.float32)
    for r in range(iH // 2):
        for c in range(iW // 2):
            patch = inp[:, :, r*2:r*2+2, c*2:c*2+2]
            out[:, :, r, c] = np.max(patch, axis=(2, 3))
    return out

bottleneck = maxpool2d(feat_enc)  # shape (1, 4, 8, 8)
print(f"Input image shape:            {x.shape}")
print(f"Encoder feature (skip source): {feat_enc.shape}")
print(f"Bottleneck feature shape:     {bottleneck.shape}")

# 2. Decoder Stage: 2x upsampling (nearest neighbour / bilinear)
def upsample2x(inp):
    return np.repeat(np.repeat(inp, 2, axis=2), 2, axis=3)

feat_up = upsample2x(bottleneck)  # shape (1, 4, 16, 16)

# 3. Skip connection concatenation along channel axis
# Concatenate encoder feature (4 channels) + upsampled decoder feature (4 channels) = 8 channels
feat_concat = np.concatenate([feat_enc, feat_up], axis=1)  # shape (1, 8, 16, 16)
print(f"Upsampled decoder shape:      {feat_up.shape}")
print(f"Concatenated skip map shape:  {feat_concat.shape}")

# 4. Final 1x1 conv to produce binary segmentation mask (2 classes: background / foreground)
w_out = np.random.randn(2, 8, 1, 1).astype(np.float32) * 0.1
logits = conv2d_same(feat_concat, w_out)  # shape (1, 2, 16, 16)

# Softmax probabilities over classes
exp_logits = np.exp(logits - np.max(logits, axis=1, keepdims=True))
probs = exp_logits / np.sum(exp_logits, axis=1, keepdims=True)
predicted_mask = np.argmax(probs, axis=1)[0]

print(f"Output probability map shape: {probs.shape}")
print(f"Final segmentation mask shape: {predicted_mask.shape} (values 0 or 1)")
print("Verified: U-Net encoder-decoder skip connection executed successfully!")

Common pitfalls

  • Checkerboard artifacts from transposed convolutions: Transposed convolutions with kernel size not divisible by stride (e.g. $k=3, s=2$) cause uneven overlap during upsampling, producing visible checkerboard artifacts; using bilinear upsampling followed by a standard $3 \times 3$ convolution avoids this.
  • Severe foreground-background class imbalance: In medical imaging, background pixels often exceed $99\%$ of the image; training with unweighted cross-entropy causes the network to predict all pixels as background, achieving $99\%$ accuracy with $0\%$ utility.
  • Spatial dimension mismatch at skip connections: Successive downsampling on odd-numbered image dimensions (e.g. $125 \to 62 \to 31$) leads to rounding issues during $2\times$ upsampling ($31 \times 2 = 62 \neq 125$); ensure input dimensions are multiples of $2^D$ (where $D$ is network depth) or use reflection padding.
  • Confusing semantic segmentation with instance segmentation: Semantic segmentation labels all pixels belonging to a class with the same integer ID without distinguishing separate object instances (e.g. two overlapping sheep are merged into a single mask).
  • Overfitting on small medical datasets: Training deep U-Nets on limited patient cohorts causes rapid memorisation; heavy data augmentation (elastic deformations, random rotations, intensity jitter) is essential.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary function of the horizontal skip connections in the U-Net architecture?

Why: Repeated max-pooling downsamples spatial dimensions, capturing high-level context but destroying precise boundary locations. Skip connections route early, high-resolution spatial feature maps directly to the decoder, restoring sharp boundary localisation.

Q2How does a Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) differ from a traditional CNN image classifier (like VGG or ResNet)?

Why: By converting dense linear layers into $1 \times 1$ spatial convolutions, FCN eliminates fixed-input-size constraints. It computes representations across 2D spatial feature grids, outputting dense per-pixel class logits.

Q3Why is Soft Dice Loss often combined with Cross-Entropy when training semantic segmentation models on medical images?

Why: In medical scans, lesions or tumours often occupy less than $1\%$ of pixels. Cross-entropy can be dominated by easy background predictions; Dice Loss evaluates relative intersection overlap, maintaining strong gradient signals even for diminutive anatomical targets.

Q4What visual artifact frequently appears when using transposed convolutions with kernel sizes that are not multiples of the stride (e.g. $k=3, s=2$)?

Why: When kernel size is not divisible by stride, transposed convolution filters overlap unevenly across neighbouring pixels, depositing more energy onto certain output coordinates and creating a characteristic high-frequency checkerboard pattern.
