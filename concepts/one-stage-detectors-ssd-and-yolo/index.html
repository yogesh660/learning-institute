One-stage detectors: SSD and YOLO

While two-stage detectors (such as Faster R-CNN) separate region proposal generation from bounding box classification, one-stage detectors frame object detection as a unified, single-pass dense regression problem directly from raw pixels to bounding boxes and class probabilities. Joseph Redmon's YOLO (You Only Look Once) pioneered high-frame-rate real-time detection on a spatial grid, while Wei Liu's SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) introduced multi-scale feature pyramid heads with pre-defined multi-aspect anchor boxes. Modern one-stage detectors combine multi-scale Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN), anchor-free regression, and Tsung-Yi Lin's Focal Loss to conquer severe foreground-background class imbalance and match two-stage accuracy at blazing inference speeds.

01 The idea

Early deep object detection architectures were complex, multi-stage pipelines: first, a Region Proposal Network (RPN) hypothesised thousands of candidate object regions; next, ROI-pooling cropped each proposal; and finally, fully connected networks classified each crop and refined coordinates. This two-pass process achieved high accuracy but crawled at 5 to 7 frames per second—far too slow for autonomous driving, drone navigation, or robotics. In 2015, YOLO shattered this complexity by asking a provocative question: why not treat detection as a single regression problem, passing the image through a convolutional network once and reading off all detections simultaneously at 45 frames per second?

The unified grid paradigm (YOLOv1): Joseph Redmon framed detection as a single neural forward pass. YOLO divides the input image into an $S \times S$ grid (e.g. $7 \times 7$). If an object's centre falls within a specific grid cell, that cell is responsible for detecting it. Each cell predicts $B$ bounding box offsets $(x, y, w, h)$, an objectness confidence score $\operatorname{Pr}(\text{Object}) \cdot \operatorname{IoU}$, and conditional class probabilities $\operatorname{Pr}(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object})$. By processing the entire image holistically in one shot, YOLO encodes global contextual awareness, drastically reducing background false positive detections compared to sliding-window or region-proposal approaches.

Multi-scale detection heads and anchor boxes (SSD): A key limitation of early YOLO was poor localization on small objects: a single coarse $7 \times 7$ grid cannot resolve multiple tiny birds clustered together. In 2016, SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) resolved this by attaching detection heads to multiple intermediate feature maps of varying spatial resolutions throughout the convolutional backbone. Deep low-resolution layers ($10 \times 10$) detect large objects using expansive receptive fields, while shallow high-resolution layers ($38 \times 38$) detect tiny objects. Furthermore, SSD placed pre-defined "anchor boxes" (or default priors) of various aspect ratios ($1:1, 1:2, 2:1, 1:3, 3:1$) at each grid location, predicting relative offset residuals $(\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta w, \Delta h)$ rather than absolute coordinates.

The extreme foreground-background class imbalance problem: While two-stage detectors filter out 99% of empty background candidates during the initial RPN proposal stage, a one-stage detector evaluates hundreds of thousands of candidate locations across its spatial pyramid simultaneously. Over $99.9\%$ of these dense anchor locations contain empty background. In standard cross-entropy loss, the overwhelming accumulation of easy background negative gradients drowns out the rare positive target gradients, crippling detector training.

Focal Loss and the modern one-stage convergence: In 2017, Lin et al. introduced RetinaNet and Focal Loss: $\operatorname{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t (1 - p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)$. By inserting the modulating factor $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ (with focusing parameter $\gamma = 2$), the loss of well-classified easy examples ($p_t \ge 0.9$) is down-weighted by a factor of $100\times$, focusing gradient updates predominantly on hard, ambiguous object candidates. Coupled with Path Aggregation Feature Pyramids (PANet) and anchor-free centre-point regression, modern one-stage detectors (such as YOLOv8 and YOLOv10) achieve both real-time execution (>100 FPS) and state-of-the-art mAP, rendering two-stage detectors obsolete for most production computer vision applications.

In everyday terms

Think of inspecting a crowded international airport baggage terminal for suspicious luggage. A two-stage detector (Faster R-CNN) is like having two separate shifts of security officers: first, an initial team walks the conveyor belt, sticking yellow post-it notes onto any bag that looks vaguely interesting; then, a second specialised inspection team individually picks up each tagged bag, scans it with an X-ray machine, and classifies its contents. This is thorough, but it creates huge lines and slow baggage throughput. A one-stage detector (YOLO) is like mounting automated high-speed ceiling scanners along the conveyor belt: as bags glide past at full speed, the scanner grid reads barcodes, estimates suitcase dimensions, and classifies contents in a single continuous camera sweep. Focal Loss is like programming the ceiling scanner to ignore millions of obvious nylon black roller bags, alerting security personnel exclusively when unusual, complex, or ambiguous cargo appears.

03 The maths

  1. YOLO bounding box coordinate parameterisation

    b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x, \quad b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y, \quad b_w = p_w e^{t_w}, \quad b_h = p_h e^{t_h}

    Here $(c_x, c_y)$ is the top-left offset of the grid cell, $(p_w, p_h)$ are anchor box prior dimensions, $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid constraining box centres to remain within their assigned cell, and $(b_x, b_y, b_w, b_h)$ are absolute predicted coordinates normalised by grid size.

  2. Focal Loss for addressing class imbalance

    \operatorname{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t \left(1 - p_t\right)^\gamma \log(p_t), \quad p_t = \begin{cases} p & \text{if } y = 1 \\ 1 - p & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

    Here $p \in [0, 1]$ is the model's estimated probability for the ground-truth class, $\alpha_t$ balances positive versus negative examples, and $\gamma \ge 0$ is the tunable focusing parameter (typically $\gamma = 2.0$). As $p_t \to 1$ for easy background examples, the modulating factor $(1 - p_t)^\gamma \to 0$, preventing background gradients from dominating the loss.

  3. Complete Intersection over Union (CIoU) loss

    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}} = 1 - \operatorname{IoU} + \frac{\rho^2(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{b}_{\text{gt}})}{c^2} + \alpha\, v, \quad v = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\left(\arctan\frac{w_{\text{gt}}}{h_{\text{gt}}} - \arctan\frac{w}{h}\right)^2

    CIoU loss incorporates three geometric factors: overlap area ($\operatorname{IoU}$), normalised central point Euclidean distance $\rho^2 / c^2$ (where $c$ is the diagonal length of the smallest enclosing box), and aspect ratio consistency $v$, driving smooth gradient updates even when candidate boxes have zero initial spatial overlap.

04 Key takeaways

  • One-stage detectors frame object detection as a single-pass regression problem, eliminating separate region proposal stages.
  • YOLO divides images into spatial grid cells, predicting bounding boxes and class probabilities simultaneously at high frame rates.
  • SSD introduces multi-scale feature pyramids and multi-aspect anchor boxes to detect both small and large objects effectively.
  • Focal Loss down-weights easy background negative examples by $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$, solving the extreme class imbalance inherent in dense single-stage detection.
  • Modern one-stage detectors employ Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN/PANet), anchor-free heads, and CIoU loss to rival two-stage accuracy with real-time throughput.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Simulate a YOLO-style one-stage detection head decoding pass
np.random.seed(42)

# Input parameters: 13x13 spatial grid, 3 anchor boxes per cell, 3 classes
grid_h, grid_w = 13, 13
num_anchors = 3
num_classes = 3

# Pre-defined anchor prior dimensions (width, height in grid units)
anchors = np.array([
    [1.5, 2.0],  # Tall anchor (e.g. pedestrian)
    [3.0, 3.0],  # Square anchor
    [5.0, 2.5]   # Wide anchor (e.g. car)
], dtype=np.float32)

# Raw network output logits for one image: shape (13, 13, num_anchors, 4 + 1 + num_classes)
# Channels: [tx, ty, tw, th, objectness_logit, class_logit_0, class_logit_1, class_logit_2]
num_attribs = 4 + 1 + num_classes
raw_preds = np.random.randn(grid_h, grid_w, num_anchors, num_attribs).astype(np.float32)

# 1. Sigmoid activation helper
def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))

# 2. Decode bounding box coordinates and probabilities
# Construct grid coordinate offsets
grid_y, grid_x = np.meshgrid(np.arange(grid_h), np.arange(grid_w), indexing='ij')
grid_x = grid_x[:, :, np.newaxis]  # shape (13, 13, 1)
grid_y = grid_y[:, :, np.newaxis]

# Extract predicted terms
tx = raw_preds[..., 0]
ty = raw_preds[..., 1]
tw = raw_preds[..., 2]
th = raw_preds[..., 3]
obj_logits = raw_preds[..., 4]
class_logits = raw_preds[..., 5:]

# Decode coordinates: bx = sigmoid(tx) + cx, by = sigmoid(ty) + cy
bx = sigmoid(tx) + grid_x
by = sigmoid(ty) + grid_y

# Decode dimensions: bw = pw * exp(tw), bh = ph * exp(th)
anchor_w = anchors[:, 0].reshape(1, 1, num_anchors)
anchor_h = anchors[:, 1].reshape(1, 1, num_anchors)
bw = anchor_w * np.exp(np.clip(tw, -5.0, 5.0))
bh = anchor_h * np.exp(np.clip(th, -5.0, 5.0))

# Decode confidence and probabilities
confidence = sigmoid(obj_logits)
class_probs = sigmoid(class_logits)

# Convert center format (bx, by, bw, bh) to corner format (x1, y1, x2, y2)
x1 = bx - bw / 2.0
y1 = by - bh / 2.0
x2 = bx + bw / 2.0
y2 = by + bh / 2.0

# 3. Flatten and filter candidates
total_proposals = grid_h * grid_w * num_anchors
flat_x1 = x1.flatten()
flat_y1 = y1.flatten()
flat_x2 = x2.flatten()
flat_y2 = y2.flatten()
flat_conf = confidence.flatten()
flat_classes = np.argmax(class_probs.reshape(-1, num_classes), axis=-1)

# Pre-NMS confidence filtering (confidence >= 0.85)
keep_mask = flat_conf >= 0.85
kept_count = np.sum(keep_mask)

print(f"Total dense spatial anchor proposals: {total_proposals:,}")
print(f"Proposals surviving confidence >= 0.85: {kept_count}")
print(f"Confidence score of top candidate:     {np.max(flat_conf):.4f}")
print(f"Predicted box 0 (corner format):       [{flat_x1[0]:.2f}, {flat_y1[0]:.2f}, {flat_x2[0]:.2f}, {flat_y2[0]:.2f}]")
print("Verified: One-stage detection head successfully decodes multi-scale bounding boxes!")

Common pitfalls

  • Unconstrained coordinate regression: Directly predicting unconstrained unbounded offsets $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ causes gradient instability; using sigmoid activations $\sigma(t_x) + c_x$ restricts box centres to their local cell, ensuring stable convergence.
  • Ignoring small-object feature degradation: Detecting small objects on deep feature maps fails because aggressive striding shrinks small targets below 1 pixel; predictions for small objects must be extracted from shallow high-resolution pyramid layers.
  • Over-relying on hand-crafted anchor shapes: Hard-coding anchor aspect ratios without inspecting target dataset distributions degrades IoU matching; anchor dimensions should be tailored to dataset statistics via k-means clustering or replaced with anchor-free heads.
  • Training one-stage detectors with standard cross-entropy without Focal Loss: Using standard unmodulated cross-entropy on dense single-stage grids causes background negatives to swamp positive signals, leading to non-converging or degenerate models.
  • Excessive NMS compute overhead: Feeding hundreds of thousands of raw predictions directly into NMS causes latency bottlenecks; always apply pre-filtering with a confidence threshold ($p \ge 0.05$) to cull 95% of candidates prior to NMS.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the defining architectural distinction between a one-stage detector (e.g. YOLO, SSD) and a two-stage detector (e.g. Faster R-CNN)?

Why: Two-stage detectors generate candidate proposals first using a Region Proposal Network (RPN) before classifying each crop. One-stage detectors treat detection as a direct unified regression problem across a spatial grid in one forward pass.

Q2Why does SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector) attach detection heads to multiple intermediate layers throughout its convolutional backbone?

Why: Shallow layers retain fine spatial resolution necessary to detect small targets, whereas deep layers possess the wide receptive fields required to comprehend and bound large objects. Multi-scale heads enable scale-invariant detection.

Q3How does Focal Loss resolve the severe foreground-background class imbalance problem encountered in dense one-stage detectors?

Why: In dense single-stage detection, over 99% of anchors are easy background negatives. Focal Loss dynamically scales the loss by $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$; for easy background examples where $p_t \approx 0.99$, the loss is reduced by $10,000\times$, focusing gradient updates on hard positive targets.

Q4In YOLO coordinate decoding, why are bounding box centre offsets passed through a sigmoid function ($\sigma(t_x) + c_x$)?

Why: Unconstrained linear predictions can place the box centre anywhere across the image, causing chaotic gradient fluctuations. The sigmoid function bounds offsets strictly to $[0, 1]$, anchoring the box centre firmly within its designated cell.
