01 The idea

When two or more explanatory variables in a multiple regression model are strongly correlated with each other, ordinary least squares faces multicollinearity. While multicollinearity does not reduce the model's overall predictive capacity, it inflates coefficient variances dramatically, turning regression estimates erratic, unstable, and uninterpretable. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) quantifies how much parameter variance is magnified by linear dependencies among predictors.

Imagine an athletic trainer building a regression model to predict sprinting speed using two features: left leg length and right leg length. Naturally, an athlete's left leg and right leg lengths are almost perfectly correlated ($r = 0.957$).

When both variables enter the regression simultaneously, least squares attempts to answer: "What is the effect of lengthening the left leg while keeping the right leg length strictly fixed?" Because this scenario is physically impossible in the data, the algorithm cannot partition credit between the two limbs. The model might assign $+15.0$ to the left leg and $-14.2$ to the right leg. The overall model predicts sprint speed reasonably well, but the standard errors on both coefficients balloon dramatically. Both $t$-statistics drop to near zero, giving non-significant p-values.

An analyst inspecting the p-values might naively conclude that neither leg matters for sprinting! This is the essence of multicollinearity. It does not mean the features lack predictive power; it means the features share redundant information, making it impossible to isolate individual effects. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) measures how much the variance of an estimated slope is inflated compared to when predictors are orthogonal. A VIF of 1 means zero collinearity; a VIF of 11.77 means variance is inflated nearly twelve-fold. Dropping one redundant leg from the model instantly collapses VIF and restores stable, significant estimates.

In everyday terms

Multicollinearity is like two singers harmonizing into the exact same microphone: the audience hears a rich sound, but the sound engineer cannot determine which singer was singing louder.

02 Step by step

  1. Examine the pairwise correlation matrix of all predictors to identify obvious bivariate correlations ($|r| > 0.8$).
  2. For each predictor $X_j$, fit an auxiliary OLS regression predicting $X_j$ from all remaining $p - 1$ predictors.
  3. Extract the auxiliary determination coefficient $R_j^2$ from the auxiliary regression.
  4. Compute the Variance Inflation Factor: $\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$.
  5. Evaluate VIF against diagnostic thresholds: $\text{VIF} = 1$ is orthogonal, $\text{VIF} > 5$ warrants caution, and $\text{VIF} > 10$ indicates severe, harmful multicollinearity.
  6. Remediate collinearity: remove redundant features, combine correlated features via dimensionality reduction, or apply ridge regularisation.

03 The maths

  1. Variance Inflation Factor Formula

    $$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{(n - 1)s_j^2} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{(n - 1)s_j^2} \cdot \text{VIF}_j$$

    Here $\sigma^2$ is the residual variance, $s_j^2$ is the sample variance of predictor $X_j$, and $R_j^2$ is the coefficient of determination obtained by regressing $X_j$ on all other predictors. $\text{VIF}_j$ directly measures the factor by which the variance of $\hat{\beta}_j$ is multiplied due to correlation with other explanatory variables.

  2. Tolerance and Condition Number

    $$\text{Tolerance}_j = \frac{1}{\text{VIF}_j} = 1 - R_j^2, \qquad \kappa(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}) = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}}$$

    Tolerance expresses the fraction of variance in $X_j$ that is unique and independent of all other predictors; a tolerance below $0.10$ signifies $\text{VIF} > 10$. The condition number $\kappa$ evaluates the ratio of maximum to minimum eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$; $\kappa > 30$ indicates severe ill-conditioning and numerical instability in matrix inversion.

  3. Regularisation as a Collinearity Remedy

    $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = \left(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$$

    When predictors are collinear, $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is nearly singular with eigenvalues near zero, causing $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ to explode. Ridge regression adds a positive constant $\lambda$ along the diagonal, bounding eigenvalues away from zero and dramatically contracting parameter variance.

04 Key takeaways

  • Multicollinearity does not bias OLS slope estimates or reduce overall $R^2$; it inflates standard errors and makes individual coefficients erratic and uninterpretable.
  • High pairwise correlation signals collinearity, but multi-variable collinearity can also emerge from linear combinations of three or more variables with low pairwise correlations.
  • VIF quantifies the degree of variance inflation; $\text{VIF} > 10$ is the standard empirical threshold denoting severe collinearity.
  • Regularisation techniques like Ridge regression directly resolve multicollinearity by trading small bias for large variance reduction.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100

# Collinear features x1 and x2 (r ~ 0.968) and independent feature x3
x1 = rng.normal(loc=10.0, scale=2.0, size=n)
x2 = x1 + rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=0.5, size=n)
x3 = rng.normal(loc=5.0, scale=1.5, size=n)

X_all = np.column_stack([x1, x2, x3])
vifs = []

# Compute VIF for each predictor via auxiliary regressions
for j in range(3):
    y_aux = X_all[:, j]
    X_others = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), np.delete(X_all, j, axis=1)])
    b_aux = np.linalg.inv(X_others.T @ X_others) @ X_others.T @ y_aux
    res_aux = y_aux - X_others @ b_aux
    r2_j = 1.0 - np.sum(res_aux**2) / np.sum((y_aux - np.mean(y_aux))**2)
    vifs.append(1.0 / (1.0 - r2_j))

r_12, _ = stats.pearsonr(x1, x2)
print(f"Pairwise correlation r(x1, x2): {r_12:.3f}")
print(f"VIF x1: {vifs[0]:.2f} (severe collinearity)")
print(f"VIF x2: {vifs[1]:.2f} (severe collinearity)")
print(f"VIF x3: {vifs[2]:.2f} (orthogonal)")

Common pitfalls

  • Dropping important control variables from a causal model without verifying whether their omission creates omitted variable bias.
  • Relying exclusively on pairwise correlation matrices, failing to detect multi-variable collinearity where $X_1 \approx X_2 + X_3$.
  • Believing that multicollinearity degrades out-of-sample prediction accuracy when test data follows the same joint predictor distribution.
  • Confusing multicollinearity (linear dependence among features) with high correlation between features and the target label.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary consequence of multicollinearity in an ordinary least squares regression?

Why: Multicollinearity does not bias estimates or reduce $R^2$, but inflates coefficient variances, leading to wide confidence intervals and non-significant t-tests.

Q2If an auxiliary regression of predictor $X_j$ on all other predictors yields $R_j^2 = 0.90$, what is the Variance Inflation Factor $\text{VIF}_j$?

Why: $\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.90} = \frac{1}{0.10} = 10.0$.

Q3Why does Ridge regression effectively resolve numerical problems caused by severe multicollinearity?

Why: Adding $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ guarantees that the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}$ are at least $\lambda > 0$, preventing matrix singularity and shrinking inflated variances.

Q4Why is inspecting only the pairwise correlation matrix insufficient to rule out multicollinearity?

Why: Three or more variables can be almost perfectly collinear ($X_1 \approx X_2 + X_3$) even when each individual pairwise correlation is modest (e.g. $r \approx 0.5$). VIF detects this multi-variable dependence.
