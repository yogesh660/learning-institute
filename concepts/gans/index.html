Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Pitting two neural networks against each other in a zero-sum minimax game: a generator creates synthetic counterfeits while a discriminator learns to detect them.

01 The idea

Rather than relying on hand-crafted pixel reconstruction metrics that encourage blurry outputs, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs, Goodfellow et al. 2014) cast generative modelling as an adversarial game. A generator learns to synthesize realistic data from random noise, while a discriminator acts as an adaptive loss function, continually learning to distinguish genuine training examples from synthetic fakes.

Consider an art counterfeiter and an art detective: - The **Generator** ($G$) is the counterfeiter: it accepts random Gaussian noise $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and attempts to paint a convincing fake artwork $G(z)$. - The **Discriminator** ($D$) is the detective: it receives an image $x$ (which is either an authentic museum masterpiece or a counterfeit from the generator) and outputs a probability score $D(x) \in [0, 1]$ indicating whether the image is authentic. At the beginning, both networks are unskilled: the generator outputs random static noise, and the discriminator guesses at chance levels.

Training proceeds as a competitive two-player alternating game: 1. **Train the Discriminator**: Present it with a batch of authentic images (labelled 1) and a batch of generator fakes (labelled 0). Update discriminator weights to maximise classification accuracy. 2. **Train the Generator**: Feed noise through the generator, pass the generated fakes to the discriminator, and update generator weights to fool the discriminator into classifying fakes as authentic ($D(G(z)) \to 1$). As the discriminator learns to detect increasingly subtle visual flaws, the generator is forced to eliminate those flaws, producing sharper textures, higher fidelity, and convincing spatial details.

At theoretical equilibrium (the **Nash equilibrium**), the generator captures the true underlying data distribution ($p_g = p_{\text{data}}$), and the discriminator can do no better than guessing with probability $D(x) = 0.5$ for every candidate image. Because the generator is guided by a learned discriminator rather than an inflexible pixel-wise distance metric like Mean Squared Error, GANs avoid the blurriness typical of standard autoencoders and produce crisp, photorealistic imagery.

In everyday terms

Like a currency counterfeiter and a bank teller: as the counterfeiter refines their printing techniques, the teller learns new counterfeit-detection checks, perpetually driving both to higher levels of mastery.

02 Step by step

  1. Design dual architectures: construct Generator $G_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{d_z} \to \mathbb{R}^D$ and Discriminator $D_\phi: \mathbb{R}^D \to [0, 1]$ using convolutional or dense layers with LeakyReLU activations.
  2. Train the discriminator on real and fake batches: freeze generator parameters, evaluate $D(x_{\text{real}})$ and $D(G(z))$, and update discriminator weights using binary cross-entropy loss.
  3. Train the generator with non-saturating loss: freeze discriminator parameters, sample fresh noise $z$, and update generator weights to maximise $\log D(G(z))$ (minimising $-\log D(G(z))$).
  4. Alternate training updates: execute one or more discriminator steps per generator step to ensure the discriminator provides informative gradients.
  5. Monitor adversarial equilibrium: verify that discriminator loss does not collapse to zero (which eliminates generator gradients) or diverge uncontrollably.

03 The maths

  1. The minimax zero-sum game objective

    $$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big]$$

    $D(x)$ is the discriminator's predicted probability that $x$ is real, and $G(z)$ is the generator's synthesized sample from noise prior $p_z$. The discriminator seeks to maximise $V(D, G)$ by correctly classifying real and fake inputs; the generator seeks to minimise it by producing fakes that drive $D(G(z)) \to 1$.

  2. Optimal discriminator and Jensen-Shannon Divergence

    $$D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} \implies V(D^*, G) = -\log(4) + 2 \cdot D_{\text{JS}}(p_{\text{data}} \parallel p_g)$$

    For any fixed generator $G$, the optimal discriminator is the density ratio of real data to combined data. When substituted into the objective, minimising $V(D^*, G)$ is mathematically equivalent to minimising the Jensen-Shannon divergence between real data distribution $p_{\text{data}}$ and generated distribution $p_g$.

  3. Non-saturating generator loss (practical implementation)

    $$\mathcal{L}_G = -\mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\big[\log D(G(z))\big] \quad \text{instead of} \quad \mathcal{L}_G^{\text{orig}} = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\big[\log(1 - D(G(z)))\big]$$

    Early in training when the generator is weak, the discriminator easily identifies fakes ($D(G(z)) \approx 0$). In this regime, the original minimax loss $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$ has flat gradients. Maximising $\log D(G(z))$ provides steep, non-zero gradients early on, dramatically accelerating generator learning.

04 Key takeaways

  • GANs replace hand-crafted loss functions with an adaptive discriminator network that dynamically discovers the criteria for realism.
  • The non-saturating generator loss ($-\log D(G(z))$) overcomes vanishing gradients early in training when the discriminator easily outmatches the generator.
  • Theoretical Nash equilibrium is reached when the generator reproduces the true data distribution ($p_g = p_{\text{data}}$) and the discriminator predicts $0.5$ for all samples.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Simulate one step of GAN training on 1D Gaussian target N(4.0, 0.5^2)
np.random.seed(42)
N = 20
w_g, b_g = 1.0, 1.0  # Generator: G(z) = w_g * z + b_g
w_d, b_d = 0.5, 0.0  # Discriminator: D(x) = sigmoid(w_d * x + b_d)

# 1. Evaluate Discriminator on real data vs generated fakes
z_noise = np.random.randn(N)
x_fake = w_g * z_noise + b_g
x_real = np.random.normal(4.0, 0.5, size=N)

d_real = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(w_d * x_real + b_d)))
d_fake = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(w_d * x_fake + b_d)))

loss_d = -float(np.mean(np.log(d_real + 1e-8) + np.log(1.0 - d_fake + 1e-8)))

# 2. Evaluate Generator using non-saturating loss: -log(D(G(z)))
z_fresh = np.random.randn(N)
x_gen = w_g * z_fresh + b_g
d_gen = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(w_d * x_gen + b_d)))
loss_g = -float(np.mean(np.log(d_gen + 1e-8)))

print(f"Discriminator loss on mixed batch: {loss_d:.4f}")
print(f"Generator non-saturating loss:     {loss_g:.4f}")
print(f"Avg discriminator score on real data: {float(np.mean(d_real)):.4f}")
print(f"Avg discriminator score on fake data: {float(np.mean(d_fake)):.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Mode collapse: the generator discovers a narrow subset of convincing samples (or a single class) that reliably fools the discriminator, ignoring the rest of the target distribution.
  • Discriminator overpowering: if the discriminator learns too fast and achieves 100% accuracy, generator gradients vanish to zero, freezing training.
  • Using training loss curves to judge visual quality: GAN loss curves fluctuate and do not correlate monotonically with sample fidelity; use metrics like FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) instead.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is "mode collapse" in Generative Adversarial Networks?

Why: Mode collapse occurs when the generator finds a small region of the data space that consistently fools the discriminator, repeatedly producing that single pattern rather than learning the entire multimodal distribution.

Q2Why is the non-saturating generator loss $-\log D(G(z))$ preferred over the original minimax formulation $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$?

Why: Early in training, $D(G(z)) \approx 0$. The slope of $\log(1 - D(G(z)))$ is near zero in this regime, causing vanishing gradients. In contrast, $-\log D(G(z))$ has a steep slope near zero, providing large initial gradient updates.

Q3What is the theoretical Nash equilibrium state of the ideal GAN minimax game?

Why: At Nash equilibrium, the generator perfectly replicates the true data distribution ($p_g = p_{\text{data}}$), leaving the discriminator unable to distinguish real from fake and forcing its optimal prediction to be chance ($D(x) = 0.5$).

Q4Why are training loss curves often uninformative for assessing the quality of a GAN?

Why: In a zero-sum game, as the generator gets better, the discriminator also adapts. Both losses can remain roughly constant even as sample realism improves dramatically, necessitating sample-based metrics like Fréchet Inception Distance (FID).
