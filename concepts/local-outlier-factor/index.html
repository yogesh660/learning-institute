Local Outlier Factor

Density-based anomaly detection that quantifies how isolated a point is relative to the varying local densities of its surrounding $k$-nearest neighbours.

01 The idea

Global anomaly detection algorithms assume that anomalies always sit far away from all other data points according to a single distance threshold. In datasets containing multiple clusters of wildly differing densities, this global assumption collapses: points on the fringe of a sparse cluster look like anomalies, while outliers near a dense cluster remain undetected. Local Outlier Factor (LOF) solves this by computing a ratio of local reachability densities, evaluating each point's degree of isolation purely relative to its immediate neighbourhood.

Imagine comparing population densities in two different regions: central Tokyo and rural Scotland. In central Tokyo, houses sit centimetres apart; if a new house were built 50 metres from its closest neighbour, that house would be an extraordinary anomaly. In the Scottish Highlands, houses routinely sit two kilometres apart; a home separated by 50 metres from its neighbour is not an anomaly at all, but rather part of a tightly clustered village. A global distance threshold would either brand every home in Scotland an anomaly, or miss the isolated house in Tokyo completely.

Local Outlier Factor resolves this by measuring relative density rather than absolute distance. For any candidate point $p$, LOF first finds its $k$-nearest neighbours and calculates its local reachability density ($\operatorname{lrd}$)—the reciprocal of the average distance required to reach $p$ from its neighbours. If $p$ is located in the middle of a dense cluster, its reachability density is high. If $p$ sits in a sparse cloud, its density is low.

The critical breakthrough is the LOF score itself, which divides the average local reachability density of $p$'s neighbours by $p$'s own local density. If $p$ has roughly the same density as its neighbours, this ratio is approximately 1, identifying $p$ as an inlier regardless of whether the cluster is dense or sparse. If $p$ is isolated—meaning its neighbours enjoy high density while $p$ sits out in the cold—the ratio jumps significantly above 1 (typically 1.5 or higher). This relative formulation makes LOF uniquely adept at uncovering local anomalies across complex, multi-density manifolds.

In everyday terms

Judging whether an ambient sound is unusually loud not by measuring absolute decibels on an open highway, but by comparing the volume to the quiet whisper of the immediate library room you are sitting in.

02 Step by step

  1. For every point $p$ in the dataset, determine its $k$-distance $d_k(p)$ (the distance to its $k$-th nearest neighbour) and identify its neighbourhood set $N_k(p)$.
  2. For each neighbour $o \in N_k(p)$, compute the reachability distance of $p$ from $o$: $\text{reach-dist}_k(p, o) = \max\\{d_k(o), d(p, o)\}$, which stabilises density estimates within dense clusters.
  3. Calculate the local reachability density $\operatorname{lrd}_k(p)$ as the inverse of the average reachability distance from $p$ to all points in $N_k(p)$.
  4. Compute the Local Outlier Factor $\operatorname{LOF}_k(p)$ by taking the average ratio between the neighbours' local reachability densities $\operatorname{lrd}_k(o)$ and $p$'s own density $\operatorname{lrd}_k(p)$.
  5. Flag observations with $\operatorname{LOF}_k(p) \gg 1$ as anomalies; points with $\operatorname{LOF}_k(p) \approx 1$ are confirmed inliers.

03 The maths

  1. k-distance and reachability distance

    $$\text{reach-dist}_k(p, o) = \max\big\\{d_k(o), \, d(p, o)\big}$$

    where $d(p, o)$ is Euclidean distance and $d_k(o)$ is the distance from neighbour $o$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbour. If $p$ is very close to $o$, the reachability distance is floored at $d_k(o)$, preventing tiny denominator fluctuations from destabilising density estimates.

  2. Local reachability density

    $$\operatorname{lrd}_k(p) = \frac{|N_k(p)|}{\sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \text{reach-dist}_k(p, o)}$$

    $\operatorname{lrd}_k(p)$ quantifies the number of neighbours per unit reachability distance. Dense regions produce small reachability distances and large $\operatorname{lrd}$, while sparse regions produce small $\operatorname{lrd}$.

  3. Local Outlier Factor score

    $$\operatorname{LOF}_k(p) = \frac{1}{|N_k(p)|} \sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \frac{\operatorname{lrd}_k(o)}{\operatorname{lrd}_k(p)}$$

    When $p$ is deep inside a cluster, its density matches its neighbours' ($\operatorname{lrd}_k(o) \approx \operatorname{lrd}_k(p)$), yielding $\operatorname{LOF} \approx 1$. When $p$ is an outlier, its density is much lower than its neighbours' ($\operatorname{lrd}_k(p) \ll \operatorname{lrd}_k(o)$), driving $\operatorname{LOF} \gg 1$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Detects anomalies based on relative local density rather than global Euclidean distance cutoffs.
  • Successfully identifies outliers in datasets containing multiple clusters of vastly different densities.
  • Reachability distance $\max\\{d_k(o), d(p, o)\\}$ stabilises calculations within dense clusters.
  • Points with $\operatorname{LOF} \approx 1$ are normal inliers; scores substantially greater than 1 indicate local outliers.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Synthetic clusters with different densities plus one outlier
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
c1 = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (30, 2))
c2 = rng.normal(5, 1.5, (30, 2))
outlier = np.array([[1.5, 1.5]])
X = np.vstack([c1, c2, outlier])
n, k = len(X), 5

# Pairwise distances and k-nearest neighbours
dist = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :], axis=-1)
sorted_idx = np.argsort(dist, axis=1)
nbr_idx = sorted_idx[:, 1:k+1]
k_dist = dist[np.arange(n), sorted_idx[:, k]]

# Reachability distance and local reachability density (lrd)
reach_dist = np.maximum(dist, k_dist[None, :])
lrd = k / np.sum([reach_dist[i, nbr_idx[i]] for i in range(n)], axis=1)

# Local Outlier Factor (LOF) score
lof = np.array([np.mean(lrd[nbr_idx[i]] / lrd[i]) for i in range(n)])

print(f"Dense cluster inlier LOF: {lof[0]:.3f}")
print(f"Sparse cluster inlier LOF: {lof[30]:.3f}")
print(f"Outlier LOF: {lof[-1]:.3f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Quadratic computational scaling: Finding exact $k$-nearest neighbours across all pairs requires $O(n^2)$ distance calculations, which becomes computationally prohibitive on millions of rows without spatial tree indexing (like $k$-d trees).
  • Selection of $k$: If $k$ is chosen smaller than the size of an outlier cluster, the outliers form a mutual neighbourhood and mask each other. If $k$ is too large, it dilutes micro-density contrasts.
  • High-dimensional distance concentration: In spaces with hundreds of dimensions, Euclidean distances concentrate and become equidistant, eroding the contrast between local and distant densities.
  • Lack of an absolute score threshold: Unlike a calibrated probability, there is no universal cutoff for LOF; a score of 1.3 might be an extreme outlier in one dataset and a normal border point in another.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Local Outlier Factor use relative local density rather than absolute distance to the nearest cluster centre?

Why: In datasets with heterogeneous densities, points in a legitimate sparse cluster have large pairwise distances. Global metrics mistake them for outliers, whereas LOF's relative density ratio normalises for local cluster spread.

Q2What is the expected Local Outlier Factor ($\operatorname{LOF}$) score for a data point nestled securely deep inside a cluster?

Why: Deep inside a cluster, a point and all of its neighbours have virtually identical local reachability densities ($\operatorname{lrd}(o) \approx \operatorname{lrd}(p)$), so the ratio averages to approximately 1.0.

Q3What role does the reachability distance $\text{reach-dist}_k(p, o) = \max\\{d_k(o), d(p, o)\\}$ play in LOF?

Why: By replacing tiny distances between identical or near-duplicate points with $d_k(o)$, reachability distance prevents near-zero divisions and stabilises local density estimates.

Q4What is a primary limitation of standard Local Outlier Factor when scaling to high-dimensional datasets?

Why: As dimensionality increases, Euclidean distances concentrate around a common mean (the curse of dimensionality), causing relative density ratios to flatten toward 1 and degrading outlier discrimination.
