Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

Standard autoencoders leave unconstrained voids in latent space that cannot generate new data. Variational autoencoders enforce a smooth Gaussian prior via probabilistic mapping and the reparameterisation trick.

01 The idea

While standard autoencoders compress data effectively, their latent spaces are riddled with discontinuities: sampling random vectors produces garbled outputs. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs, Kingma & Welling 2013) transform autoencoders into principled directed probabilistic generative models by mapping inputs to probability distributions and optimising the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO).

In a deterministic autoencoder, image $A$ maps to point $z = (2.0, 3.0)$ and image $B$ maps to $z = (-4.0, 1.0)$. The optimiser has no reason to smooth the space between them. If you pick a point halfway between them at $(-1.0, 2.0)$, the decoder has never trained on that coordinate and outputs unrecognizable static. The latent space is a constellation of isolated islands surrounded by empty voids.

A **Variational Autoencoder** replaces fixed points with probability distributions. Instead of outputting a single vector $z$, the encoder outputs two vectors: a mean $\mu(x)$ and a log-variance $\log \sigma^2(x)$. The latent representation is then sampled from the Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$. Two competing forces govern the objective: 1. **Reconstruction Loss**: Compels the decoder to reconstruct $x$ accurately from sampled codes $z$. 2. **Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence**: Penalises the encoder distribution for deviating from a standard normal prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. The KL penalty acts like an elastic spring: it pulls all means $\mu$ towards zero and forces variances $\sigma^2$ towards one. This prevents clusters from scattering across isolated regions, turning the latent space into a smooth, continuous manifold where interpolating between two points morphs seamlessly from one valid sample to another.

The central algorithmic hurdle is backpropagation: you cannot pass gradients through a stochastic sampling operation ($z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$). The **reparameterisation trick** solves this by isolating the randomness into an independent noise variable $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and computing $z$ deterministically: $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$. Gradients now flow freely into $\mu$ and $\sigma$ via standard autodiff, enabling end-to-end training.

In everyday terms

Like mapping cities onto an elastic sphere: without constraints, you can scatter cities into disconnected corners of space; the KL penalty pulls all coordinates into an orderly, continuous spherical terrain where every coordinate represents realistic geography.

02 Step by step

  1. Construct the probabilistic encoder: build a network that maps input $x$ to two parallel vectors: mean $\mu_\phi(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and log-variance $\log \sigma^2_\phi(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
  2. Execute the reparameterisation trick: sample standard Gaussian noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and calculate latent sample $z = \mu + \exp(0.5 \log \sigma^2) \odot \epsilon$.
  3. Construct the generative decoder: build a network $g_\theta(z)$ that reconstructs original data parameters from sampled latent vector $z$.
  4. Evaluate the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO): compute total loss as the sum of reconstruction error and the closed-form analytical KL divergence between $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
  5. Backpropagate and train: update encoder and decoder parameters end-to-end with AdamW.
  6. Synthesise novel data: discard the encoder, sample fresh noise $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and pass it through the decoder to generate novel, realistic observations.

03 The maths

  1. Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) objective

    $$\log p_\theta(x) \ge \operatorname{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x|z)\big] - D_{\text{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z|x) \parallel p(z)\big)$$

    $\log p_\theta(x)$ is the marginal log-likelihood of the data. The first term measures reconstruction quality under the variational posterior $q_\phi(z|x)$; the second term is the KL divergence penalising deviation from the prior $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Maximising the ELBO guarantees that true data likelihood increases.

  2. Closed-form KL divergence for diagonal Gaussians

    $$D_{\text{KL}}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)) \parallel \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^d \left( 1 + \log(\sigma_j^2) - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2 \right)$$

    $d$ is latent dimensionality, $\mu_j$ is the predicted mean, and $\sigma_j^2$ is the predicted variance of component $j$. This analytical formula computes exact KL divergence in a single vectorised pass without Monte Carlo integration. If $\mu_j = 0$ and $\sigma_j^2 = 1$, the penalty evaluates to $0$.

  3. The reparameterisation trick

    $$z = \mu(x) + \sigma(x) \odot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \implies \frac{\partial z}{\partial \mu} = I, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial \sigma} = \operatorname{diag}(\epsilon)$$

    By rewriting the stochastic variable $z$ as a deterministic transformation of parameters and independent noise $\epsilon$, gradients can backpropagate through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ without encountering non-differentiable sampling nodes.

04 Key takeaways

  • The reparameterisation trick ($z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$) permits gradient backpropagation through stochastic sampling by moving randomness into an external noise input.
  • The KL divergence term regularises the latent space into an uninterrupted Gaussian manifold, eliminating dead zones and enabling smooth interpolation.
  • Unlike classical autoencoders, VAEs are true generative models: sampling $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and passing it to the decoder synthesises coherent novel samples.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Reparameterisation trick and analytical KL divergence
np.random.seed(42)
N, d = 2, 2  # 2 samples, 2 latent dimensions

# Encoder outputs: mean and log-variance
mu = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [1.5, -2.0]])
log_var = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [-0.5, 0.8]])
std = np.exp(0.5 * log_var)

# 1. Reparameterisation trick: z = mu + std * epsilon
eps = np.random.randn(N, d)
z = mu + std * eps

# 2. Analytical KL divergence against standard Gaussian N(0, I)
kl_divergence = -0.5 * np.sum(1.0 + log_var - mu**2 - np.exp(log_var), axis=1)

print("Sample 0 (ideal prior: mu=0, logvar=0):")
print(f"  Latent sample z: {np.round(z[0], 4).tolist()}")
print(f"  KL divergence:   {kl_divergence[0]:.4f} (zero penalty)")

print("Sample 1 (displaced mean, scaled variance):")
print(f"  Latent sample z: {np.round(z[1], 4).tolist()}")
print(f"  KL divergence:   {kl_divergence[1]:.4f} (regularisation penalty applied)")

Common pitfalls

  • Posterior collapse: when paired with powerful autoregressive decoders, the decoder learns to generate data unconditionally, ignoring $z$ completely while setting $D_{\text{KL}} \to 0$.
  • Sampling directly using non-differentiable random generators inside the forward pass, which severs the computational graph and stops the encoder from updating.
  • Over-weighting the KL penalty early in training without KL annealing, forcing all representations to zero and producing blurry, featureless reconstructions.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the fundamental purpose of the reparameterisation trick in Variational Autoencoders?

Why: Direct stochastic sampling ($z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$) has no defined gradient with respect to distribution parameters. Formulating $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$ isolates randomness into $\epsilon$, making $z$ fully differentiable with respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

Q2What failure mode occurs when a VAE experiences "posterior collapse"?

Why: Posterior collapse happens when an expressive decoder reconstructs data without using latent information, causing the variational posterior $q(z|x)$ to collapse to the uninformative prior $p(z)$ ($D_{\text{KL}} \to 0$).

Q3In the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), what role does the KL divergence term play?

Why: The KL divergence acts as a regulariser that pulls encoded representations toward a zero-centred unit Gaussian distribution, preventing disconnected clusters and guaranteeing that random sampling yields realistic data.

Q4How does generation of new samples occur in a trained VAE?

Why: Because the KL divergence trained the latent space to approximate $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, sampling $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and evaluating $g_\theta(z)$ synthesises novel, realistic data without needing an input image.
