Wasserstein (earth mover's) distance

The Wasserstein distance (also known as the Earth Mover's Distance) measures the minimum work required to transform one probability distribution into another, defined as mass multiplied by physical ground distance. Unlike Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence, which collapse to infinite or constant values when probability distributions have non-overlapping supports, the Wasserstein distance provides a continuous, meaningful, and differentiable metric that reflects geometry in the underlying metric space. This geometric property makes the Wasserstein-1 metric fundamental to Optimal Transport, generative adversarial networks (WGAN), and domain adaptation.

01 The idea

Suppose you have two discrete point mass distributions: P has all its probability mass at position x=0, and Q_1 has all its mass at x=1, while Q_2 has all its mass at x=100. Standard information-theoretic metrics like KL divergence or Total Variation see no difference between these two scenarios: in both cases, the distributions do not overlap, yielding maximal divergence. But clearly Q_1 is vastly closer to P than Q_2 is! The Wasserstein distance fixes this fatal flaw by factoring in the physical metric distance that probability mass must travel.

The Dirt Pile Analogy (Earth Mover's Distance): Imagine distribution P is a pile of dirt spread across the ground, and distribution Q is an excavation hole of identical volume. The Wasserstein-1 distance W_1(P, Q) is the minimum mechanical work (mass multiplied by transport distance) required to shovel and transport the dirt from pile P until it fills hole Q.

The Overlap Failure of KL and JS Divergences: In high-dimensional spaces (e.g. natural images), real data concentrates on low-dimensional manifolds. Two different manifolds in 1000-dimensional space almost never intersect. When supports do not overlap, KL divergence is infinite and JS divergence is constant log 2. Their gradients vanish everywhere, making neural network training impossible. The Wasserstein distance remains smooth, linear, and continuous regardless of overlap!

Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality: Computing optimal transport directly requires solving a linear programming problem over all joint couplings \gamma(x, y). The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality reveals a magical alternative: W_1(P, Q) equals the maximum expectation difference sup_{||f||_L <= 1} [E_P[f(x)] - E_Q[f(y)]] over all 1-Lipschitz functions! This is the exact objective optimised by the WGAN critic.

In everyday terms

Imagine moving furniture between two apartments. KL divergence is like a strict landlord who only checks whether a sofa is sitting in the exact same centimetre in both apartments: if it is moved by even one inch, they declare the apartments 100% completely different. The Wasserstein distance is the moving company invoice: it charges you based on the physical weight of the sofa multiplied by how many miles the lorry had to drive to deliver it.

03 The maths

  1. Primal Wasserstein-p Distance Formulation

    W_p(P, Q) = \left( \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \gamma}\left[ d(x, y)^p \right] \right)^{1/p}

    Here \Pi(P, Q) denotes the collection of all joint probability distributions (couplings) \gamma(x, y) having marginals P and Q respectively. For p=1, W_1 is the standard Earth Mover's Distance.

  2. One-Dimensional Closed-Form via Cumulative Distribution Functions

    W_1(P, Q) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \big| F_P(t) - F_Q(t) \big| \, dt = \int_0^1 \big| F_P^{-1}(u) - F_Q^{-1}(u) \big| \, du

    In one dimension, optimal transport has a closed-form identity: the minimum cost equals the L_1 distance between the cumulative distribution functions F_P and F_Q, or equivalently, the integral of quantile function differences.

  3. Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality

    W_1(P, Q) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim Q}[f(y)] \right)

    The dual theorem reformulates the infimum over joint couplings into a supremum over 1-Lipschitz continuous functions \|f\|_L \le 1. This enables deep neural networks (such as WGAN critics) to approximate the Wasserstein distance without combinatorial optimisation.

04 Key takeaways

  • The Wasserstein distance measures the minimal work (mass * distance) required to morph one probability distribution into another.
  • Unlike KL and JS divergences, the Wasserstein metric reflects the underlying geometry of metric space and remains continuous even when supports are completely disjoint.
  • In one dimension, W_1 has an exact closed-form solution equal to the integrated absolute difference between cumulative distribution functions.
  • The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality converts the primal coupling problem into a supremum over 1-Lipschitz continuous critic functions, powering WGAN architectures.

05 In code

python
"""
Wasserstein (Earth Mover's) Distance vs Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence
Demonstrates 1D optimal transport, CDF integration, and linear gradient
properties under non-overlapping support translation.
"""

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def wasserstein_1d(samples_p: np.ndarray, samples_q: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """
    Compute 1D Wasserstein-1 distance via sorted quantile differences.
    O(N log N) closed-form optimal transport.
    """
    p_sorted = np.sort(samples_p)
    q_sorted = np.sort(samples_q)
    # If sample sizes match, W_1 is simply mean absolute difference between sorted arrays
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(p_sorted - q_sorted)))

# Generate two 1D Gaussian distributions
# P: N(0, 1), Q: N(delta, 1) as delta moves from 0 to 10
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 5000
p_samples = np.random.normal(loc=0.0, scale=1.0, size=n_samples)

print("=== Demonstration: Wasserstein Distance vs Translation Delta ===")
print("True analytical W_1 between N(0, 1) and N(delta, 1) is exactly |delta|.\n")

deltas = [0.0, 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, 10.0]

for delta in deltas:
    q_samples = np.random.normal(loc=delta, scale=1.0, size=n_samples)
    w1_dist = wasserstein_1d(p_samples, q_samples)
    
    # Analytical KL divergence between N(0, 1) and N(delta, 1): KL = delta^2 / 2
    kl_div = 0.5 * (delta ** 2)
    
    print(f"Shift delta = {delta:4.1f} | Empirical W_1 = {w1_dist:6.3f} (True: {delta:4.1f}) | KL Divergence = {kl_div:6.2f}")

# Discrete Optimal Transport via Linear Programming Demonstration
print("\n=== Discrete Earth Mover's Distance on a Grid ===")
# Pile P at positions [0, 1, 2] with masses [0.5, 0.5, 0.0]
# Hole Q at positions [0, 1, 2] with masses [0.0, 0.5, 0.5]
p_mass = np.array([0.5, 0.5, 0.0])
q_mass = np.array([0.0, 0.5, 0.5])
# Distance matrix between bins: |i - j|
cost_matrix = np.array([
    [0.0, 1.0, 2.0],
    [1.0, 0.0, 1.0],
    [2.0, 1.0, 0.0]
])

# Optimal plan: keep 0.5 at pos 1, move 0.5 from pos 0 to pos 2 (cost = 0.5 * 2 = 1.0)
# Closed-form 1D discrete CDF:
cdf_p = np.cumsum(p_mass)
cdf_q = np.cumsum(q_mass)
discrete_w1 = np.sum(np.abs(cdf_p - cdf_q))

print(f"Distribution P masses: {p_mass}")
print(f"Distribution Q masses: {q_mass}")
print(f"Discrete 1D Wasserstein Cost: {discrete_w1:.3f} units of work")

Common pitfalls

  • High computational complexity in multiple dimensions: Solving multi-dimensional discrete optimal transport scales cubically O(N^3) without Sinkhorn entropy regularisation.
  • Violating the Lipschitz constraint in dual estimation: If the critic network in WGAN fails to maintain ||f||_L <= 1, the objective ceases to approximate the true Wasserstein distance.
  • Assuming Wasserstein distance is dimensionless: W_1 inherits the physical units of the underlying sample space (e.g. metres, seconds, pixels), unlike unitless KL divergence.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does the Wasserstein distance provide superior optimisation gradients compared to Jensen-Shannon divergence when training generative models?

Why: When distributions have disjoint supports, JS divergence saturates at log(2) with zero gradient. The Wasserstein distance grows linearly with physical separation distance, providing clean, non-vanishing gradients everywhere.

Q2In one dimension, what is the closed-form formulation for the Wasserstein-1 distance between two distributions P and Q?

Why: For 1D distributions, optimal transport preserves monotonic quantile order, meaning W_1 is exactly equal to the area between the two cumulative distribution curves.

Q3What condition must the critic function f(x) satisfy in the Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual formulation of the Wasserstein-1 distance?

Why: The Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem proves that W_1(P, Q) is the supremum of E_P[f(x)] - E_Q[f(x)] over the family of 1-Lipschitz continuous functions.
