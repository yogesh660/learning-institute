Stop words and n-grams

Stop words are ubiquitous grammatical function words (such as "the", "is", "at", "which") that provide syntactic scaffolding in prose but offer minimal domain-specific topical discrimination in information retrieval and document classification. N-grams capture local multi-word context by forming contiguous tuples of $n$ consecutive tokens (unigrams, bigrams, trigrams), upgrading bag-of-words models into context-sensitive phrase extractors capable of distinguishing "not guilty" from "guilty". Deciding when to filter stop words and engineering word or character n-gram vocabularies balances vector sparsity against semantic nuance.

01 The idea

If you tally the most frequent words in Shakespeare's plays, a corporate contract, and a medical textbook, the top of all three lists looks identical: "the", "and", "of", "to", "a", "in". These function words bind sentences together grammatically, but they reveal almost nothing about whether the document discusses Elizabethan tragedy, corporate liability, or cardiovascular surgery. Conversely, treating each word as an isolated island discards crucial phrasing: "hot dog" is not merely heat plus a canine, and "not bad" is positive praise despite containing a negative word. Navigating stop words and constructing n-grams provides the bridge from isolated word counts to contextual phrase representations.

Zipf's Law and the function word explosion: George Kingsley Zipf observed that the frequency of any word in a natural language corpus is inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table: $f(w) \propto 1 / r^\alpha$ (where $\alpha \approx 1$). The top 50 to 100 most frequent words typically account for $40\%$ to $50\%$ of all word occurrences in a document. In tasks like topic modelling, search indexing, or author classification, these high-frequency function words inflate vector dimensionality, consume memory, and add noise without aiding class separation. Identifying and filtering stop words strips away this high-frequency noise floor.

The peril of aggressive stop word removal: While removing stop words helps simple bag-of-words document search, indiscriminate filtering can destroy meaning. Consider sentiment analysis: stripping the stop word "not" transforms the movie review "The movie was not entertaining" into "movie entertaining"—inverting the sentiment from negative to positive. In question answering, filtering "to be or not to be" eliminates the entire query. Modern deep language models (such as BERT and GPT) never remove stop words because self-attention relies heavily on prepositions and pronouns to resolve syntactic dependencies.

Capturing local context with N-grams: A unigram is a single isolated token ($n=1$). A bigram ($n=2$) groups two consecutive tokens ("machine learning", "deep learning"). A trigram ($n=3$) groups three ("natural language processing"). Moving from unigrams to n-grams allows statistical models to capture negation ("not good"), compound nouns ("New York City"), and domain idioms. Higher-order n-grams provide richer semantic context, but cause the vocabulary size to explode combinatorially.

Character-level N-grams for robust morphology: N-grams can also be constructed at the subword character level rather than the word level. For example, the 3-character n-grams (character trigrams) of "" are "". Character n-grams are immune to out-of-vocabulary errors, capture morphological roots across inflections ("run", "running", "runner"), and withstand spelling mistakes and OCR typos (such as "definately" matching "definitely"), forming the secret weapon behind FastText and modern subword tokenisers.

In everyday terms

Think of assembling a jigsaw puzzle of a landscape. Stop words are like the thousands of plain blue sky pieces that look virtually identical: they make up half the box, take up huge table space, and tell you very little about whether the puzzle depicts Paris or the Grand Canyon. If you are indexing the puzzle in an inventory catalogue, sweeping the blue sky pieces aside (stop word filtering) lets you focus immediately on the distinctive landmark pieces (the Eiffel Tower or canyon cliffs). Single unigram words are like individual disconnected puzzle tabs: you hold a piece with a brown paw, another with red fabric, another with a wheel. Bigrams and trigrams are like clicking two or three interlocking pieces together: suddenly, you don't just have an isolated paw and wheel; you see a dog sitting in a bicycle basket. You capture the scene's composition without needing the entire thousand-piece puzzle.

03 The maths

  1. Zipf's Law frequency distribution

    f(r) = \frac{C}{r^\alpha} \approx \frac{C}{r}, \quad \log f(r) = \log C - \alpha \log r

    Here $r$ is the frequency rank of a word (where $r=1$ is the most frequent word, e.g. "the"), $f(r)$ is its empirical frequency, and $\alpha \approx 1$. A log-log plot of word frequency versus rank forms a straight line with slope $-1$, proving that a minuscule fraction of words dominates total corpus volume.

  2. N-gram token count and sliding window formulation

    \text{N-grams}(W, n) = \left\{ (w_i, w_{i+1}, \dots, w_{i+n-1}) \;\middle|\; 1 \le i \le |W| - n + 1 \right\}

    A document of length $T = |W|$ tokens yields exactly $T - n + 1$ contiguous n-grams. Adding $n-1$ boundary padding symbols at the start and end yields exactly $T + n - 1$ padded n-grams, preserving transition probabilities for initial and terminal words.

  3. Maximum likelihood bigram transition probability

    P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\operatorname{Count}(w_{i-1}, w_i)}{\operatorname{Count}(w_{i-1})}

    In classical n-gram language modelling, the conditional probability of word $w_i$ given preceding word $w_{i-1}$ is evaluated as the joint bigram count divided by the unigram count of the conditioning prefix.

04 Key takeaways

  • Stop words are high-frequency grammatical function words that carry minimal topical discrimination in bag-of-words search.
  • Zipf's Law demonstrates that the top 100 most frequent words account for approximately half of all tokens in natural language corpora.
  • Aggressive stop word removal destroys negation and subtle syntax, making it unsuitable for sentiment analysis and modern language models.
  • N-grams capture contiguous local word context, enabling models to differentiate between distinct phrases like "not guilty" and "guilty".
  • Character n-grams capture morphological variations and typos, providing robust representations for out-of-vocabulary words.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

# Corpus of documents
documents = [
    "Machine learning is not magic, it is computer science.",
    "Deep learning is a subset of machine learning.",
    "Not bad at all, this machine learning model works well!"
]

# 1. Custom stop words list (preserving negation words: 'not')
standard_stop_words = {"is", "it", "a", "of", "at", "all", "this"}

def simple_tokenize(doc):
    # Lowercase and split on non-alphanumeric characters
    import re
    return [tok for tok in re.split(r'\W+', doc.lower()) if tok]

# Tokenise all documents
tokenized_docs = [simple_tokenize(d) for d in documents]

# 2. Filter stop words while retaining negations
filtered_docs = [
    [w for w in doc if w not in standard_stop_words]
    for doc in tokenized_docs
]

print("Document 1 before stop word filtering:")
print(tokenized_docs[0])
print("\nDocument 1 after stop word filtering (notice 'not' preserved):")
print(filtered_docs[0])

# 3. Generate Word-Level Bigrams and Trigrams
def generate_ngrams(tokens, n=2):
    return [tuple(tokens[i:i+n]) for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1)]

bigrams_doc1 = generate_ngrams(filtered_docs[0], n=2)
print(f"\nWord Bigrams (n=2) for Document 1:")
for bg in bigrams_doc1:
    print(f"  {' '.join(bg)}")

# 4. Character-level N-Grams for Subword Morphology
def generate_char_ngrams(word, n=3):
    padded = f"<{word}>"
    return [padded[i:i+n] for i in range(len(padded) - n + 1)]

sample_word = "learning"
char_trigrams = generate_char_ngrams(sample_word, n=3)
print(f"\nCharacter Trigrams (n=3) for '{sample_word}':")
print(char_trigrams)

# 5. Bigram Frequency Distribution across Corpus
all_bigrams = [bg for doc in filtered_docs for bg in generate_ngrams(doc, n=2)]
bigram_counts = Counter(all_bigrams)

print("\nMost frequent bigrams across corpus:")
for bg, count in bigram_counts.most_common(3):
    print(f"  '{' '.join(bg)}': {count} occurrences")
print("Verified: Word and character n-gram extraction executed successfully!")

Common pitfalls

  • Stripping negation words in sentiment tasks: Blindly removing words like "not", "no", and "never" reverses sentiment polarities and ruins sentiment classifiers.
  • Unconstrained N-gram vocabulary explosion: Generating unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams on large corpora without min_df thresholds inflates vocabulary to millions of sparse columns, causing GPU memory exhaustion.
  • Applying stop word lists to modern Transformer models: Removing stop words before feeding text to BERT, RoBERTa, or GPT cripples self-attention, as transformers rely on prepositions and pronouns to parse sentence syntax.
  • Ignoring casing when matching stop words: If stop words are lowercased ("the") but document tokens retain capitalization ("The"), stop word filtering fails on sentence-initial words unless casing is normalised first.
  • Over-filtering rare domain terminology: Using automated frequency thresholds without domain review can inadvertently discard rare medical, legal, or technical acronyms that carry the highest topical information.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Zipf's Law motivate the filtering of stop words in classical information retrieval and topic modelling?

Why: Zipf's Law states that word frequency decays inversely with rank. The top 50 words ("the", "of", "and") appear in virtually every document regardless of topic, diluting term vectors without offering discrimination between categories.

Q2Under what natural language processing task can indiscriminate stop word removal cause severe model degradation?

Why: Negation words ("not", "neither", "hardly") are often included in naive stop word lists. Stripping them completely reverses the sentiment of a sentence, turning praise into condemnation or vice versa.

Q3What critical advantage do character-level n-grams offer over word-level n-grams in text embeddings (as seen in FastText)?

Why: If an unseen word or typo appears at test time (e.g. "definately"), word models fail (out-of-vocabulary). Character n-grams decompose the word into overlapping fragments ("def", "efi", "fin") that match known subwords, producing a robust embedding.

Q4Why do modern Transformer architectures (such as BERT and GPT) avoid removing stop words during pre-training and fine-tuning?

Why: Transformers model natural language holistically: function words provide essential structural clues for pronoun coreference, syntactic dependency parsing, and semantic role assignment. Deleting them destroys linguistic context.
