Sampling and Standard Error

Distinguishing population variability from estimation uncertainty: standard deviation measures individual spread, standard error measures how much sample statistics fluctuate.

01 The idea

Statistical inference hinges on distinguishing between the spread of individual observations and the uncertainty of a summary statistic. While standard deviation quantifies the dispersion of the raw population, standard error measures the variability of an estimator across hypothetical repeated samples.

Suppose you want to know the average income in a city of 1,000,000 residents. Asking every resident is prohibitively expensive, so you draw a random sample of 100 people. Some make £25,000, some £60,000, and a few make £150,000. The standard deviation of your sample—say £20,000—describes the broad diversity of incomes among individual citizens. It will not shrink if you survey 1,000 people instead; people's salaries remain just as diverse.

Now consider the sample mean income, which turns out to be £45,000. If your colleague surveys a completely different random sample of 100 residents, they might find a mean of £43,200. A third sample might yield £47,100. The spread of these hypothetical sample averages is the standard error (SE). Because averages are far more stable than individual measurements, the standard error for $n = 100$ is much smaller than the standard deviation: roughly $£20,000 / \sqrt{100} = £2,000$.

Crucially, while population standard deviation stays constant as sample size grows, standard error shrinks steadily at rate $1/\sqrt{n}$. If you expand your survey to 10,000 residents, your sample standard deviation remains £20,000, but your standard error plunges to $£20,000 / \sqrt{10,000} = £200$. Standard error directly measures the precision of your estimate.

In everyday terms

Standard deviation is the size of the waves in the ocean; standard error is the slight bobbing of a massive cargo ship resting on those waves.

02 Step by step

  1. Identify the population parameter of interest (e.g. population mean $\mu$ or proportion $p$).
  2. Draw a representative random sample of size $n$ using independent, identically distributed sampling.
  3. Compute the sample statistic (the point estimate), such as the sample mean $\bar{x}$ or sample proportion $\hat{p}$.
  4. Compute the sample standard deviation $s$ as a measure of raw observational scatter.
  5. Calculate the estimated standard error of the estimator: $\operatorname{SE}(\bar{x}) = s / \sqrt{n}$.
  6. Use the standard error to construct confidence intervals or evaluate statistical significance in hypothesis testing.

03 The maths

  1. Standard Deviation vs. Standard Error

    $$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

    The standard deviation $\sigma$ describes the inherent variability of individual observations in the population. The standard error $\operatorname{SE}(\bar{X})$ is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the sample mean $\bar{X}$. When the population $\sigma$ is unknown, we substitute the sample standard deviation $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$.

  2. Standard Error of a Proportion

    $$\operatorname{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}$$

    For binary outcomes (Bernoulli trials with probability of success $p$), the variance is $p(1-p)$. The standard error of the sample proportion $\hat{p}$ peaks at $p = 0.5$ and decays symmetrically toward the boundaries 0 and 1.

  3. Finite Population Correction (FPC)

    $$\operatorname{SE}_{\text{finite}}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}}$$

    When sampling without replacement from a finite population of size $N$, observations are not strictly independent. The finite population correction factor $\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$ shrinks the standard error toward zero as the sample size $n$ approaches the full population size $N$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Standard deviation describes data spread; standard error describes estimator precision.
  • Standard error always decreases as sample size increases, scaling strictly with $1/\sqrt{n}$.
  • Reporting an estimate without its standard error gives an illusion of certainty without context.
  • In linear regression, every coefficient $\beta_j$ has a dedicated standard error used to compute $t$-statistics.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
true_mu, true_sigma = 50.0, 12.0

sample_sizes = [25, 100, 400, 1600]
print("True Population SD:", true_sigma)

for n in sample_sizes:
    # Draw 1,000 independent samples of size n
    samples = rng.normal(loc=true_mu, scale=true_sigma, size=(1000, n))
    sample_means = samples.mean(axis=1)
    
    single_sample = samples[0]
    s = np.std(single_sample, ddof=1)
    estimated_se = s / np.sqrt(n)
    empirical_se = np.std(sample_means, ddof=1)
    
    print(f"n={n:4d} | Sample SD: {s:.2f} | Est SE: {estimated_se:.3f} | Empirical SE: {empirical_se:.3f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Reporting standard deviation when asked for estimation uncertainty, or reporting standard error to describe population diversity.
  • Assuming non-random convenience samples can be cured with standard error formulas; selection bias invalidates standard error entirely.
  • Forgetting the $1/\sqrt{n}$ diminishing returns; doubling precision requires a fourfold increase in data.
  • Omitting Bessel's correction ($n-1$) when computing sample standard deviation on small samples.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the primary conceptual difference between standard deviation and standard error?

Why: Standard deviation describes the spread of individual observations in a population or sample. Standard error describes the spread of the sampling distribution of an estimator (e.g. sample mean).

Q2If a sample of 64 observations has a sample standard deviation of $s = 16$, what is the estimated standard error of the mean?

Why: $\operatorname{SE}(\bar{x}) = s / \sqrt{n} = 16 / \sqrt{64} = 16 / 8 = 2.0$.

Q3What happens to the population standard deviation $\sigma$ and the standard error of the mean $\operatorname{SE}(\bar{X})$ as the sample size $n$ grows arbitrarily large?

Why: The population standard deviation $\sigma$ is a fixed physical attribute of the population. The standard error $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$ measures sampling uncertainty and converges to 0 as $n \to \infty$.

Q4For a binary classification test set of $n = 400$ instances with an observed accuracy of $\hat{p} = 0.80$, what is the estimated standard error of the accuracy estimate?

Why: $\operatorname{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) / n} = \sqrt{(0.80 \times 0.20) / 400} = \sqrt{0.16 / 400} = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$ (or 2 percentage points).

Q5At what true population proportion $p$ is the standard error $\operatorname{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ at its maximum for any fixed sample size $n$?

Why: The Bernoulli variance $p(1 - p)$ is a quadratic curve peaking at $p = 0.50$ (where $p(1 - p) = 0.25$). As $p$ approaches 0 or 1, the variance and standard error shrink toward zero.

Q6When sampling without replacement from a finite population of size $N$, which factor is applied to adjust the standard error of the sample mean?

Why: The Finite Population Correction factor $\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$ accounts for the reduction in sampling variability when a sizable fraction of the finite population is sampled without replacement.

Q7Study A surveys 100 participants and reports $\operatorname{SE} = 6.0$. If Study B samples the same population and desires a standard error of $\operatorname{SE} = 1.5$, how many participants must Study B recruit?

Why: Standard error shrinks as $1/\sqrt{n}$. To cut the standard error by a factor of $6.0 / 1.5 = 4$, the sample size must increase by a factor of $4^2 = 16$. Thus $n = 100 \times 16 = 1{,}600$.

Q8In Python, evaluating s / np.sqrt(len(data)) where s = np.std(data, ddof=1) computes:

Why: Dividing the sample standard deviation by $\sqrt{n}$ calculates the standard error of the mean. Using ddof=1 ensures the sample variance estimator is unbiased.

Q9An online survey collects 250,000 voluntary responses and calculates a tiny standard error of $\pm 0.1\%$. Why can this precision be deceptive?

Why: Standard error only reflects random sampling fluctuations under probability sampling. It cannot diagnose or fix systematic sampling bias (e.g. self-selection), which undermines the estimate regardless of sample size.

Q10A company has $N = 500$ employees. A random audit of $n = 100$ employees without replacement yields $s = 20\text{ hours}$. Using the Finite Population Correction $\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}$, what is the corrected standard error? (Given: $\sqrt{400/499} \approx 0.895$).

Why: The unadjusted standard error is $s / \sqrt{n} = 20 / 10 = 2.0\text{ hours}$. Multiplying by the FPC factor $\sqrt{400/499} \approx 0.8953$ gives $2.0 \times 0.8953 \approx 1.79\text{ hours}$.

Q11An A/B test has two independent groups: Group A ($n_A = 100, s_A^2 = 300$) and Group B ($n_B = 100, s_B^2 = 100$). What is the standard error of the difference between the two sample means, $\operatorname{SE}(\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B)$?

Why: For independent samples, variances of the sample means sum: $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B) = \frac{s_A^2}{n_A} + \frac{s_B^2}{n_B} = \frac{300}{100} + \frac{100}{100} = 3 + 1 = 4$. Taking the square root gives $\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{4} = 2.0$.

Q12A model achieves test accuracy $\hat{p} = 0.90$. To ensure the standard error of this accuracy estimate is at most 0.015 (1.5 percentage points), what is the minimum test sample size $n$?

Why: Setting $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n} \le 0.015$ gives $(0.90 \times 0.10) / n \le 0.015^2 = 0.000225$. Thus $n \ge 0.09 / 0.000225 = 400$.
