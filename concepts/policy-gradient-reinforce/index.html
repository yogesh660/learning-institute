Policy gradient: REINFORCE

Policy gradient methods directly parameterise and optimise a stochastic policy without requiring an intermediate value function or environmental transition dynamics. Grounded in the Policy Gradient Theorem, REINFORCE (Monte Carlo policy gradient) uses sampled episode returns to adjust policy parameters, increasing the probability of actions that yield high cumulative rewards. By introducing baseline subtraction to reduce variance without introducing bias, policy gradients enable stable learning in high-dimensional and continuous action spaces.

01 The idea

In complex robotics and video games, mapping every possible state to a Q-table or finding the argmax over millions of continuous actuator voltages is practically impossible. What if, instead of estimating values and acting greedily, we parameterised the policy directly as a neural network that outputs action probabilities? Policy gradient methods optimise the policy directly: if an action led to great returns, increase its probability; if it led to disaster, decrease its probability.

Model-free control without transition dynamics: Traditional dynamic programming requires knowing the transition probability tensor P(s' | s, a). In the real world, physics are complex and unmodelled. The remarkable magic of the Policy Gradient Theorem is that the gradient of expected cumulative return with respect to policy parameters theta does NOT depend on the gradient of environmental transition dynamics: nabla_theta J(theta) = E [ nabla_theta log pi_theta(a | s) * Q(s, a) ]. We can optimise the policy purely by sampling trajectories.

The log-derivative trick and the intuition of REINFORCE: The term nabla_theta log pi_theta(a | s) = (nabla_theta pi_theta(a | s)) / pi_theta(a | s) acts as a normalising score function. Multiplying by return G_t scales the parameter update: actions followed by large positive returns receive positive gradient steps (increasing their likelihood in that state), while actions followed by negative or sub-optimal returns have their likelihood suppressed.

The curse of high variance and baseline subtraction: Because REINFORCE samples complete Monte Carlo returns G_t = sum_{k=0}^inf gamma^k R_{t+k+1}, random environmental noise cascades over long episodes, causing massive variance in gradient estimates. Subtracting a state-dependent baseline b(s)—typically an estimate of state value V(s)—drastically reduces variance. Crucially, because sum_a nabla_theta pi(a | s) * b(s) = b(s) * nabla (1) = 0, baseline subtraction introduces zero mathematical bias!

In everyday terms

Think of learning to shoot basketball free-throws with your eyes closed, guided only by a coach's whistle. You adjust your shooting posture (policy parameters theta) and take shots (sample actions). If the ball swishes through the hoop, the coach shouts praise (high return G_t), and your brain reinforces the neuromuscular motor commands you just fired (positive gradient update). If the ball clangs off the rim, the coach expresses disappointment, and your brain dampens those specific muscle firings. Over thousands of shots, your shooting posture converges to near-perfection without you ever calculating the differential equations of aerodynamic ballistics.

03 The maths

  1. The Policy Gradient Theorem (Sutton et al., 1999)

    \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^T \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \, Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t, a_t) \right]

    The expected gradient of performance $J(\theta)$ depends strictly on the gradient of the log-policy weighted by action values, completely bypassing the unknown transition dynamics $\nabla_\theta P(s' \mid s, a)$ of the environment.

  2. REINFORCE parameter update with baseline subtraction

    \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^T \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \left( G_t - b(s_t) \right)

    Here $G_t = \sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k-t} R_{k+1}$ is the empirical return-to-go. Subtracting baseline $b(s_t)$ preserves an unbiased expectation ($\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \ln \pi \cdot b] = 0$) while drastically reducing variance.

  3. Softmax policy score function derivative

    \nabla_\theta \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \mathbf{\phi}(s, a) - \sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} \pi_\theta(a' \mid s) \mathbf{\phi}(s, a')

    For a linear softmax policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s) \propto \exp(\theta^T \mathbf{\phi}(s, a))$, the score function gradient is the difference between the observed feature vector and the expected feature vector under current policy probabilities.

04 Key takeaways

  • Policy gradient methods directly parameterise and optimise the policy pi_theta(a | s) using gradient ascent.
  • The Policy Gradient Theorem enables model-free learning without requiring knowledge of environmental transition dynamics.
  • REINFORCE uses full Monte Carlo returns G_t as unbiased estimators of action values Q(s, a).
  • Subtracting a state-dependent baseline b(s) reduces gradient variance without introducing mathematical bias.
  • Policy gradients naturally handle continuous action spaces, stochastic policies, and partial observability.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

class REINFORCESimulator:
    """
    Simulation of the REINFORCE (Monte Carlo Policy Gradient) algorithm
    with softmax policy parameterization and baseline subtraction.
    """
    def __init__(self, num_states=2, num_actions=2, lr=0.1, gamma=0.95):
        self.num_states = num_states
        self.num_actions = num_actions
        self.lr = lr
        self.gamma = gamma
        # Policy parameter weights: (num_states x num_actions)
        self.theta = np.zeros((num_states, num_actions))

    def get_action_probs(self, state):
        logits = self.theta[state]
        exp_logits = np.exp(logits - np.max(logits))
        return exp_logits / np.sum(exp_logits)

    def sample_action(self, state):
        probs = self.get_action_probs(state)
        return np.random.choice(self.num_actions, p=probs)

    def compute_returns_to_go(self, rewards):
        """Compute discounted returns G_t = sum_k gamma^k R_{t+k+1} backwards."""
        T = len(rewards)
        returns = np.zeros(T)
        G = 0.0
        for t in reversed(range(T)):
            G = rewards[t] + self.gamma * G
            returns[t] = G
        return returns

    def update_policy(self, states, actions, rewards, use_baseline=True):
        returns = self.compute_returns_to_go(rewards)
        baseline = np.mean(returns) if use_baseline else 0.0
        advantages = returns - baseline
        
        # Accumulate gradients
        grad = np.zeros_like(self.theta)
        for t in range(len(states)):
            s = states[t]
            a = actions[t]
            adv = advantages[t]
            probs = self.get_action_probs(s)
            
            # nabla_theta log pi(a|s) for softmax: e_a - probs
            dlog_pi = -probs
            dlog_pi[a] += 1.0
            
            grad[s] += dlog_pi * adv
            
        # Gradient ascent update
        self.theta += self.lr * grad

# Simulated training on a 2-state MDP:
# State 0: Action 1 is rewarded (+10), Action 0 is penalized (-1)
np.random.seed(42)
agent = REINFORCESimulator(num_states=2, num_actions=2, lr=0.05, gamma=0.90)

print("Initial Action Probabilities in State 0:", agent.get_action_probs(0))

# Simulate 50 training episodes
for ep in range(50):
    states, actions, rewards = [], [], []
    s = 0
    # Single-step episode
    a = agent.sample_action(s)
    r = 10.0 if a == 1 else -1.0
    
    states.append(s)
    actions.append(a)
    rewards.append(r)
    
    agent.update_policy(states, actions, rewards, use_baseline=True)

print("\nTrained Action Probabilities in State 0 (after REINFORCE):")
final_probs = agent.get_action_probs(0)
print(f"  P(Action 0 [Penalized]): {final_probs[0]:.4f}")
print(f"  P(Action 1 [Rewarded]):  {final_probs[1]:.4f}")
print("Notice how policy gradient successfully shifted probability mass to Action 1!")

Common pitfalls

  • High variance of Monte Carlo returns: Because REINFORCE evaluates full trajectories, returns fluctuate wildly based on downstream randomness. Baseline subtraction and actor-critic methods are essential to prevent slow or unstable convergence.
  • Sample inefficiency: REINFORCE is an on-policy algorithm: every parameter update requires generating fresh trajectory samples from the updated policy. Discarding past data limits sample efficiency.
  • Premature policy collapse / entropy collapse: If the policy becomes deterministic too early in training (probabilities collapse to 1.0 on sub-optimal actions), exploration ceases and the agent becomes permanently stuck in local optima. Add an entropy bonus to encourage exploration.
  • Learning rate sensitivity: Unlike supervised learning where mistakes can be corrected by subsequent batches, a bad policy gradient step can destroy policy performance, making it impossible to sample good trajectories again. Keep learning rates small.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does the Policy Gradient Theorem represent a profound breakthrough for model-free reinforcement learning?

Why: In real-world environments, transition physics P(s' | s, a) are unknown. The Policy Gradient Theorem proves that nabla_theta J(theta) depends only on the gradient of the known policy nabla log pi_theta and sampled returns, enabling model-free learning.

Q2Why does subtracting a state-dependent baseline b(s) from Monte Carlo returns reduce variance without introducing bias?

Why: Mathematically, E[nabla log pi(a|s) * b(s)] = sum_a nabla pi(a|s) * b(s) = b(s) * nabla (1) = 0. Because the expected baseline gradient is zero, subtracting b(s) does not change the expected gradient, but drastically reduces sample variance.

Q3What is a major practical drawback of the REINFORCE algorithm compared to Actor-Critic methods?

Why: Because REINFORCE samples full-episode returns, stochastic environmental noise over long trajectories causes extreme gradient variance. Actor-Critic methods resolve this by bootstrapping value estimates at each step.
