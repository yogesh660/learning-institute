01 The idea

Collaborative filtering generates personalised recommendations without requiring item metadata, tags, or content descriptions. By mining historical ratings or interaction matrices, it identifies akin taste profiles (user-based) or co-rated items (item-based) to predict unseen preferences through neighbourhood aggregation.

Imagine visiting a vast library containing 100,000 novels. Rather than categorising books by genres or author biographies, you look at borrowers who read the same five eclectic titles you adored last month. When you find three other readers whose checkout logs overlap with yours 90% of the time, you can confidently read whatever novel they picked next. You did not need to know whether the book is a sci-fi thriller or historical non-fiction; their shared reading behaviour acts as the collective filter.

This is user-based collaborative filtering (UBCF). We build a matrix where rows are users and columns are items. To predict how Alice will rate a new film, we compute pairwise similarity scores between Alice and all other users using their co-rated items. We select Alice's nearest neighbours (the $k$ highest-scoring users) and calculate a weighted average of their ratings. Crucially, because Alice might be a stingy rater whose average score is 2.5 stars while Bob awards 5 stars to everything, we mean-centre ratings before averaging so personal rating baselines do not corrupt predictions.

When users dramatically outnumber items—such as millions of shoppers purchasing from an inventory of 20,000 products—computing user similarities becomes computationally prohibitive and volatile. Item-based collaborative filtering (IBCF) flips the perspective: it computes similarities between pairs of items based on how users rated them concurrently. Because item relationships reflect enduring characteristics rather than fleeting user moods, item similarity matrices can be precomputed offline, updated incrementally, and produce explainable recommendations ("Because you watched Inception...").

In everyday terms

Asking your culinary soulmate what they ordered off a foreign menu you cannot read, rather than attempting to decipher the ingredient list yourself.

02 Step by step

  1. Construct the user-item interaction matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, where observed cells record explicit ratings or implicit actions, and unobserved pairs form the sparse missing majority.
  2. Compute user mean ratings $\bar{r}_u$ across observed items to calculate mean-centred ratings $r_{u, i} - \bar{r}_u$, isolating genuine deviations from individual baseline leniency.
  3. Calculate pairwise item similarities (for IBCF) or user similarities (for UBCF) using adjusted cosine or Pearson correlation over mutually evaluated entries.
  4. For a target user and unrated item, filter candidate neighbours to the top $k$ entities exhibiting positive similarity ($s > 0$).
  5. Aggregate the neighbourhood ratings via a similarity-weighted sum added back to the user baseline, predicting the target interaction score.

03 The maths

  1. Pearson correlation similarity for user-based CF

    $$s(u, v) = \frac{\sum_{i \in I_{uv}} (r_{u, i} - \bar{r}_u)(r_{v, i} - \bar{r}_v)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i \in I_{uv}} (r_{u, i} - \bar{r}_u)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i \in I_{uv}} (r_{v, i} - \bar{r}_v)^2}}$$

    Here $I_{uv} = I_u \cap I_v$ represents the subset of items evaluated by both user $u$ and user $v$. Subtracting user-specific means $\bar{r}_u$ and $\bar{r}_v$ centres each user's rating distribution around zero, preventing differences in personal rating scales from distorting the cosine angle.

  2. Adjusted cosine similarity for item-based CF

    $$s(i, j) = \frac{\sum_{u \in U_{ij}} (r_{u, i} - \bar{r}_u)(r_{u, j} - \bar{r}_u)}{\sqrt{\sum_{u \in U_{ij}} (r_{u, i} - \bar{r}_u)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{u \in U_{ij}} (r_{u, j} - \bar{r}_u)^2}}$$

    In item-based filtering, $U_{ij}$ is the set of users who rated both item $i$ and item $j$. Unlike standard cosine similarity between item column vectors, adjusted cosine subtracts the respective user mean $\bar{r}_u$ from each interaction. This normalises across user rating tendencies before evaluating item co-occurrence.

  3. Rating prediction via normalised neighbourhood aggregation

    $$\hat{r}_{u, i} = \bar{r}_u + \frac{\sum_{j \in N_u(i)} s(i, j) \big(r_{u, j} - \bar{r}_u\big)}{\sum_{j \in N_u(i)} |s(i, j)|}$$

    The predicted rating $\hat{r}_{u, i}$ starts from user $u$'s mean rating $\bar{r}_u$. It then adds the similarity-weighted offset from the top-$k$ nearest items $j \in N_u(i)$ that user $u$ has already rated. Dividing by the sum of absolute similarities ensures the aggregated deviation respects the original rating scale.

04 Key takeaways

  • Domain-free discovery: collaborative filtering requires no content metadata, discovering unexpected serendipitous connections across item categories.
  • Item-based stability: item-item similarities remain far more stable over time than user-user similarities, allowing large-scale precomputation and caching.
  • Mean-centring necessity: subtracting user averages removes individual bias, ensuring that harsh critics and generous raters contribute on equal terms.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

R = np.array([[5, 4, 1, 0], [4, 5, 1, 2], [1, 2, 5, 4], [2, 1, 4, 5], [5, 5, 2, 1]], dtype=float)
means = np.array([row[row > 0].mean() for row in R])
R_c = np.where(R > 0, R - means[:, None], 0.0)

m, n = R.shape
sim = np.eye(n)
for i in range(n):
    for j in range(i + 1, n):
        co = (R[:, i] > 0) & (R[:, j] > 0)
        if np.sum(co) >= 2:
            ui, uj = R_c[co, i], R_c[co, j]
            d = np.sqrt(np.sum(ui**2)) * np.sqrt(np.sum(uj**2))
            sim[i, j] = sim[j, i] = np.sum(ui * uj) / d if d > 1e-9 else 0.0

target_u, target_i, k = 0, 3, 2
rated = np.where(R[target_u] > 0)[0]
top_k = rated[np.argsort(sim[target_i, rated])[::-1][:k]]
w = sim[target_i, top_k]

pred = means[target_u] + np.sum(w * R_c[target_u, top_k]) / np.sum(np.abs(w))
print(f"User 0 mean rating: {means[target_u]:.2f}")
print(f"Item similarities to Item 3: {np.round(sim[target_i], 3)}")
print(f"Predicted rating for User 0 on Item 3: {pred:.2f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Cold-start vulnerability: completely new users without interaction history or new items with zero ratings cannot be recommended via collaborative signals alone.
  • Extreme sparsity: commercial catalogues typically exhibit $>99\%$ sparsity; two random users rarely co-rate items, collapsing the intersection size $|I_{uv}|$.
  • Popularity echo-chambers: universally popular blockbuster items dominate co-occurrence graphs, crowding out niche long-tail items unless explicit inverse-frequency penalties are applied.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why is adjusted cosine similarity preferred over standard cosine similarity in item-based collaborative filtering?

Why: Different users possess distinct internal rating scales (e.g. some hand out 5s easily, others reserve 5 for masterpieces). Adjusted cosine subtracts the user mean $\bar{r}_u$ from each rating, standardising ratings across users before computing item vector similarities.

Q2In a production system with 50,000,000 registered users and 100,000 catalogue products, why is item-based collaborative filtering (IBCF) favoured over user-based (UBCF)?

Why: Item similarity matrices are smaller ($10^5 \times 10^5$ vs $10^7 \times 10^7$) and item-item correlations remain stable over time, allowing item similarities to be precomputed and cached offline for sub-millisecond retrieval.

Q3What is the primary manifestation of the "cold-start" problem in pure collaborative filtering?

Why: Collaborative filtering relies exclusively on past behavioural overlap. When a brand-new user joins or a new item is added, they have zero co-ratings, preventing neighbourhood models from calculating valid similarity scores.
