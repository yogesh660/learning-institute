01 The idea

Every photograph captured by a digital sensor is a central perspective projection of a 3D world onto a 2D plane. To enable robotic navigation, autonomous driving, or augmented reality, a vision system must invert this projection: converting 2D pixel coordinates back into calibrated 3D geometric rays. Camera calibration mathematically estimates the camera's optical centre, focal length, physical sensor orientation, and lens distortion parameters.

The ideal Pinhole Camera Model projects a 3D point $\mathbf{P}_w = (X_w, Y_w, Z_w)$ onto a 2D image plane via central perspective: $x = f \frac{X}{Z}, y = f \frac{Y}{Z}$, where $f$ is the focal length.

In homogeneous projective geometry, this mapping is expressed as a compact $3 \times 4$ projection matrix $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{K} [\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}]$.

The Intrinsic Matrix $\mathbf{K}$ represents internal optical properties: focal lengths in pixel units $(f_x, f_y)$ and the principal point (optical centre) $(c_x, c_y)$. The Extrinsic Matrix $[\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}]$ represents the camera's rigid 3D pose in the world: a $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and a $3 \times 1$ translation vector $\mathbf{t}$.

Real physical lenses introduce optical distortion: Radial distortion (barrel or pincushion distortion caused by lens curvature, modelled with coefficients $k_1, k_2, k_3$) and Tangential distortion (caused by the lens sensor not being perfectly parallel, modelled with $p_1, p_2$). Zhang's calibration method observes a flat checkerboard from multiple angles to solve for all intrinsic, extrinsic, and distortion parameters closed-form via planar homographies.

In everyday terms

Imagine a painter sitting behind a clear glass window, painting the landscape outside by tracing exactly where light rays passing through a tiny peephole (the pinhole) hit the glass sheet. If the glass is tilted (extrinsic rotation), shifted (extrinsic translation), or if the glass pane is curved like a funhouse mirror (radial lens distortion), the tracing will warp unless the painter uses a calibration matrix to mathematically un-distort the perspective.

02 Step by step

  1. Capture planar calibration pattern: Photograph a rigid checkerboard or circle grid from 10–20 diverse angles, distances, and orientations.
  2. Detect sub-pixel corner coordinates: Locate interior checkerboard corner vertices to sub-pixel accuracy using gradient saddle point refinement.
  3. Compute planar homographies: For each view, estimate the $3 \times 3$ homography matrix $\mathbf{H}$ mapping known planar checkerboard coordinates to detected image pixels.
  4. Extract intrinsic camera matrix: Use orthogonality constraints across multiple homographies to solve the linear closed-form system for intrinsic parameters $\mathbf{K}$.
  5. Estimate lens distortion coefficients: Fit radial distortion polynomial ($k_1, k_2$) and tangential parameters ($p_1, p_2$) via non-linear Levenberg-Marquardt optimisation.
  6. Rectify and undistort: Remap incoming raw camera frames to undistorted rectilinear images prior to downstream 3D triangulation.

03 The maths

  1. Full Perspective Camera Projection Matrix

    s \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{K} \text{ (Intrinsics)}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} & t_x \\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} & t_y \\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} & t_z \end{bmatrix}}_{[\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}] \text{ (Extrinsics)}} \begin{bmatrix} X_w \\ Y_w \\ Z_w \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}

    Projects homogeneous 3D world coordinates $(X_w, Y_w, Z_w, 1)^T$ to homogeneous 2D pixel coordinates $(u, v, 1)^T$ up to arbitrary scale $s$.

  2. Radial Lens Distortion Polynomial Model

    \begin{aligned} x_{\mathrm{distorted}} &= x (1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) \\ y_{\mathrm{distorted}} &= y (1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) \end{aligned}, \quad r^2 = x^2 + y^2

    Models barrel distortion ($k_1 < 0$) and pincushion distortion ($k_1 > 0$) as an even-power radial polynomial expansion away from the optical centre.

  3. Planar Homography Constraint (Zhang Formulation)

    \mathbf{h}_1^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{h}_2 = 0, \quad \mathbf{h}_1^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{h}_1 = \mathbf{h}_2^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{h}_2, \quad \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{K}^{-T} \mathbf{K}^{-1}

    Exploits the orthonormality of rotation column vectors $\mathbf{r}_1$ and $\mathbf{r}_2$ to solve for absolute conic image $\mathbf{B}$ and extract matrix $\mathbf{K}$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Camera calibration maps 3D physical world space to 2D discrete digital pixel arrays.
  • Intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$ models internal optics: focal lengths $(f_x, f_y)$ and principal point $(c_x, c_y)$.
  • Extrinsic matrix $[\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}]$ models the 6-DOF camera pose in the world coordinate frame.
  • Radial and tangential distortion parameters rectify lens curvature before geometric triangulation.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

def project_3d_to_pixel(point_3d_world: np.ndarray, K: np.ndarray, R: np.ndarray, t: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Project a 3D world coordinate to 2D image pixel coordinates."""
    # 1. Transform to camera frame: P_cam = R * P_world + t
    point_cam = R @ point_3d_world + t
    
    # 2. Perspective division by depth Z
    z = point_cam[2]
    if z <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Point lies behind the camera!")
    normalised_coord = np.array([point_cam[0] / z, point_cam[1] / z, 1.0])
    
    # 3. Apply intrinsic matrix K
    pixel_homo = K @ normalised_coord
    return pixel_homo[:2]

# Define camera intrinsics: focal length 800px, optical centre at (320, 240)
K = np.array([
    [800.0,   0.0, 320.0],
    [  0.0, 800.0, 240.0],
    [  0.0,   0.0,   1.0]
])

# Camera located at (0, 0, 0) looking straight down +Z axis
R = np.eye(3)
t = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.0])

# 3D object located at (X=1.0m, Y=0.5m, Z=4.0m)
P_world = np.array([1.0, 0.5, 4.0])

pixel = project_3d_to_pixel(P_world, K, R, t)
print(f"3D World Point {P_world} -> 2D Image Pixel: (u={pixel[0]:.1f}, v={pixel[1]:.1f})")
# u = 800 * (1/4) + 320 = 520.0, v = 800 * (0.5/4) + 240 = 340.0

Common pitfalls

  • Assuming square pixels ($f_x = f_y$): Some digital sensors have non-square pixel aspect ratios; assuming $f_x = f_y$ introduces subtle aspect ratio errors in 3D reconstruction.
  • Neglecting lens distortion on wide-angle cameras: Using linear projection equations on fisheye or wide-angle lenses causes massive errors near image margins (up to 50 pixels).
  • Calibrating with coplanar camera views: Capturing checkerboard images from only parallel frontal perspectives fails to constrain focal length; tilt the board by at least $30^\circ$–$45^\circ$.

06 Check yourself

Q1What is the role of the camera Intrinsic Matrix $\mathbf{K}$ in geometric computer vision?

Why: The intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$ models the internal geometric optical properties that transform rays in camera space into pixel coordinates.

Q2What causes "radial distortion" in optical camera lenses?

Why: Physical glass lenses are spherical approximations. Rays entering near lens perimeters bend unevenly, warping straight lines into curved arcs.

Q3Why does Zhang's checkerboard calibration method require photographing the pattern from multiple tilted angles?

Why: Frontal parallel views create degenerate homography systems where focal length cannot be disentangled from distance. Tilting provides non-coplanar optical constraints.
