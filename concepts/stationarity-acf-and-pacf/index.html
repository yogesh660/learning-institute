Stationarity, Autocorrelation and Partial Autocorrelation

Weak stationarity ensures that mean, variance, and autocovariance remain invariant over time, while the ACF and PACF reveal the memory structure of temporal data.

01 The idea

Statistical forecasting models rely on stationarity: historical patterns must behave consistently into the future. Autocorrelation (ACF) measures total linear association between observations at varying lags, whereas partial autocorrelation (PACF) isolates direct linear relationships by removing the confounding influence of intervening lags.

Imagine forecasting daily electricity demand. If demand grows steadily by 5% each year as the population expands, yesterday's mean is an unreliable baseline for tomorrow. Similarly, if winter demand swings by 50 GW while spring fluctuations rarely exceed 10 GW, the variance depends on the calendar. A non-stationary process is a moving target whose statistical parameters shift underneath your model, generating spurious correlations and unstable predictions.

Weak (second-order or covariance) stationarity imposes three conditions: a constant mean $\mu$, a constant finite variance $\sigma^2$, and an autocovariance $\gamma(k)$ that depends solely on the time separation $k = |t - s|$, not on the absolute calendar date $t$. When raw data drifts or displays changing volatility, transformations such as logarithmic scaling and differencing ($\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$) stabilise the moments, converting persistent wanderings into stationary fluctuations around zero.

Once a series is stationary, we map its temporal memory using autocorrelation functions. The ACF at lag 2 measures the raw correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-2}$. However, $y_t$ correlates with $y_{t-1}$, and $y_{t-1}$ correlates with $y_{t-2}$, creating an indirect echo through the intermediate period. The PACF strips away this linear chain of mediation, measuring only the residual connection between $y_t$ and $y_{t-2}$ once $y_{t-1}$ is controlled for. For a pure autoregressive process AR(1), the ACF decays geometrically while the PACF cuts off cleanly after lag 1.

In everyday terms

ACF is like the total reverberation echoing down a corridor, while PACF isolates the distinct sound bouncing directly off a specific far wall after silencing echoes from every doorway in between.

02 Step by step

  1. Plot the raw sequence against time to inspect for deterministic trends, seasonal cycles, or widening variance envelopes.
  2. Calculate rolling window metrics (such as a 30-period rolling mean and standard deviation) to visually inspect parameter constancy across historical segments.
  3. Execute formal unit-root hypothesis tests, such as the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test, to assess whether stochastic trending requires first-order differencing.
  4. Apply variance-stabilising transformations (e.g. natural logarithms) and differencing ($\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$) until the series exhibits mean-reverting behaviour.
  5. Compute sample ACF and PACF coefficients up to lag $K$, plotting them alongside 95% Bartlett confidence bounds ($\pm 1.96 / \sqrt{n}$) for white noise.
  6. Diagnose model signatures: an AR($p$) process displays decaying ACF and sharp PACF cut-off at lag $p$, whereas an MA($q$) process displays sharp ACF cut-off at lag $q$ and decaying PACF.

03 The maths

  1. Weak (covariance) stationarity

    $$\mathbb{E}[y_t] = \mu, \quad \operatorname{Var}(y_t) = \sigma^2 < \infty, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-k}) = \gamma(k) \quad \forall t, k$$

    A process is weakly stationary if its first moment (mean $\mu$) and second moments (variance $\sigma^2$ and autocovariance $\gamma(k)$) are finite and invariant to time shifts $t$. Strict stationarity requires all joint probability distributions to remain identical under time shifts, but weak stationarity suffices for linear time-series modelling.

  2. Autocorrelation function (ACF)

    $$\rho(k) = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)} = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^n (y_t - \bar{y})(y_{t-k} - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^n (y_t - \bar{y})^2}$$

    $\rho(k)$ measures the linear correlation between observations separated by $k$ periods. Under the null hypothesis of Gaussian white noise, sample autocorrelations are asymptotically normal with variance $1/n$, yielding Bartlett 95% confidence bands of $\pm 1.96 / \sqrt{n}$.

  3. Partial autocorrelation function (PACF)

    $$\phi_{kk} = \operatorname{Corr}\big(y_t - \hat{y}_t^{(k-1)}, \; y_{t-k} - \hat{y}_{t-k}^{(k-1)}\big)$$

    $\phi_{kk}$ represents the partial correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$ after projecting both onto the linear subspace spanned by intermediate lags $\{y_{t-1}, \dots, y_{t-k+1}\}$. In an AR($p$) process, $\phi_{kk} = 0$ for all $k > p$, providing an exact diagnostic cut-off.

04 Key takeaways

  • Non-stationary series produce spurious regressions: two completely independent drifting series frequently exhibit inflated $R^2$ values and misleadingly tiny p-values.
  • ACF captures total serial correlation across time including cascaded intermediaries, while PACF isolates the direct conditional correlation at lag $k$.
  • Bartlett bounds ($\pm 1.96 / \sqrt{n}$) establish the threshold beyond which sample autocorrelations reflect genuine signal rather than sampling noise.
  • Differencing removes stochastic polynomial trends, but excessive over-differencing adds artificial negative autocorrelation and needlessly inflates forecast variance.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n = 300
eps = np.random.normal(0, 1.0, n)
y = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    y[t] = 0.7 * y[t-1] + eps[t]

# Sample moments
y_dm = y - np.mean(y)
gamma0 = np.dot(y_dm, y_dm) / n
acf = [np.dot(y_dm[k:], y_dm[:-k]) / (n * gamma0) for k in range(1, 4)]

# PACF: lag 1 equals ACF lag 1; lag 2 via OLS conditioning on lag 1
pacf1 = acf[0]
X2 = np.column_stack([y[1:-1], y[:-2]])
y2 = y[2:]
beta2 = np.linalg.lstsq(X2 - X2.mean(axis=0), y2 - y2.mean(), rcond=None)[0]
pacf2 = beta2[1]
bound_95 = 1.96 / np.sqrt(n)

print("Sample mean:", round(float(np.mean(y)), 3))
print("Sample var:", round(float(np.var(y)), 3))
print("ACF lags 1..3:", [round(float(x), 3) for x in acf])
print("PACF lag 1:", round(float(pacf1), 3), "lag 2:", round(float(pacf2), 3))
print("Bartlett 95% bound:", round(float(bound_95), 3))

Common pitfalls

  • Evaluating ACF and PACF on un-differenced trending data: strong trends cause sample ACF to decay linearly and sluggishly, obscuring the true autoregressive structure.
  • Over-differencing stationary data: differencing a stationary white noise series creates a non-invertible MA(1) process with $\theta = 1$, introducing artificial negative correlation.
  • Confusing weak stationarity with strict stationarity: weak stationarity concerns only the first two moments, whereas strict stationarity requires invariance of the entire joint distribution.
  • Neglecting seasonal non-stationarity: seasonal peaks occurring every $s$ periods violate autocovariance invariance and require seasonal differencing ($y_t - y_{t-s}$).

06 Check yourself

Q1Which condition is strictly required for a time series to be weakly (covariance) stationary?

Why: Weak stationarity requires constant mean, constant finite variance, and autocovariances that depend solely on the lag difference $k = |t - s|$.

Q2If a stationary series is generated by an AR(1) process $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$ with $0 < \phi < 1$, what behaviour do ACF and PACF display?

Why: In an AR(1) model, ACF displays geometric decay $\rho(k) = \phi^k$, whereas PACF isolates the direct lag effect and drops to zero for all lags $k \ge 2$.

Q3Why does PACF differ from ACF at lag 2 for an autoregressive process?

Why: ACF at lag 2 incorporates both the direct relationship and indirect transmission through $y_{t-1}$; PACF eliminates the intermediate linear mediation of $y_{t-1}$.

Q4What is the risk of over-differencing a series that is already stationary?

Why: Differencing white noise $y_t = \epsilon_t$ yields $\Delta y_t = \epsilon_t - \epsilon_{t-1}$, introducing an artificial MA(1) structure with $\rho(1) = -0.5$ and inflating prediction variance.
