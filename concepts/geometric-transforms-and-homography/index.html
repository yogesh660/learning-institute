Geometric transforms and homography

Planar projective transformations (homographies) map points between different camera perspectives of a planar scene or across views captured by a camera rotating purely around its optical centre. Governed by a $3 \times 3$ invertible matrix $H$ with 8 degrees of freedom in homogeneous coordinates ($\mathbf{x}' \sim H \mathbf{x}$), homographies generalise translation, Euclidean rigid-body motion, similarity, and affine transformations. Estimating $H$ from four or more point correspondences via the Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) algorithm forms the mathematical engine for panoramic image stitching, document perspective rectification, and augmented reality planar tracking.

01 The idea

When you capture an angled photograph of a whiteboard or a receipt on a table, the rectangular surface appears distorted into an arbitrary quadrilateral: parallel lines converge toward vanishing points, and right angles shear. Yet because the physical surface is flat, that distortion is not arbitrary. A single linear transformation in projective space—a homography—completely maps any point on the world plane directly to your sensor plane. Understanding the geometric hierarchy of 2D transforms and solving for homographies from point matches is the gateway from 2D pixel processing to 3D spatial vision.

The geometric transformation hierarchy: 2D planar transformations form a strict nesting hierarchy based on preserved geometric invariants. Rigid (Euclidean) transforms preserve Euclidean distances and angles (3 degrees of freedom: 2 translation, 1 rotation). Similarity transforms add uniform scaling, preserving angles and length ratios (4 degrees of freedom). Affine transforms allow independent axis scaling and shear, mapping parallel lines to parallel lines but distorting angles (6 degrees of freedom). Projective transformations (homographies) represent the most general linear mapping in homogeneous coordinates: parallel lines intersect at vanishing lines, lengths and angles warp freely, but straight lines remain straight (8 degrees of freedom).

Projective geometry and homogeneous coordinates: In standard Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$, a projective warp is non-linear due to perspective division by depth: $x' = \frac{h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}}$. By introducing homogeneous coordinates $\mathbf{x} = [x, y, 1]^T$ and $\mathbf{x}' = [x', y', 1]^T$, this non-linear perspective mapping becomes a strictly linear matrix multiplication $\mathbf{x}' \sim H \mathbf{x}$, where $\sim$ denotes equality up to an arbitrary non-zero global scale factor $\lambda$.

When does a homography hold: A $3 \times 3$ homography accurately maps pixels between two cameras under two physical conditions: (1) all observed 3D points lie on a common flat physical plane in the scene (such as a floor, facade, or table surface), or (2) the camera rotates purely around its optical centre with zero baseline translation ($C_1 = C_2$). The second condition is why panoramic mobile apps can stitch 360-degree vistas of mountains and landscapes without needing 3D depth reconstructions.

Solving for $H$ via Direct Linear Transformation (DLT): Because each 2D point correspondence $(x, y) \leftrightarrow (x', y')$ provides two independent linear constraints on $H$, a minimum of four non-collinear point pairs are required to constrain the 8 degrees of freedom of $H$ (up to scale). Rather than solving non-linear ratios directly, DLT exploits the vector cross-product identity $\mathbf{x}' \times (H \mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$, rearranging the point constraints into a homogeneous linear system $A \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}$, where $A$ is an $2N \times 9$ coefficient matrix. The optimal solution in the presence of noise is the right singular vector of $A$ associated with its smallest singular value, computed via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).

In everyday terms

Imagine holding a flat glass slide painted with a square grid in front of an overhead projector. If you slide the slide left or right, the projected shadow translates (Euclidean motion). If you spin the slide like a steering wheel, the projection rotates (Euclidean). If you move the projector closer or further, the shadow expands or contracts uniformly (similarity). If you pull the projector screen at an oblique angle, the shadow stretches into a parallelogram (affine transform). But if you tilt the glass slide itself relative to the light beam, the light rays travel different distances before striking the wall: the square grid warps into a trapezoid where parallel grid lines converge toward a horizon. That trapezoidal shadow is a homography. Reversing the homography is like positioning a virtual projector to un-tilt the shadow back into a perfect square.

03 The maths

  1. Planar projective transformation in homogeneous coordinates

    \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = H \mathbf{x}

    The matrix $H \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ has 9 entries but only 8 independent degrees of freedom due to the arbitrary scale factor: multiplying $H$ by any non-zero scalar $c \neq 0$ leaves projected inhomogeneous coordinates $(x' = \frac{h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}}, y' = \frac{h_{21}x + h_{22}y + h_{23}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}})$ completely unchanged.

  2. Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) cross-product constraint

    \mathbf{x}'_i \times (H \mathbf{x}_i) = \mathbf{0} \implies \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0}^T & -w'_i \mathbf{x}_i^T & y'_i \mathbf{x}_i^T \\ w'_i \mathbf{x}_i^T & \mathbf{0}^T & -x'_i \mathbf{x}_i^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{h}_1 \\ \mathbf{h}_2 \\ \mathbf{h}_3 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}

    Because the cross product of two collinear vectors is zero, expanding the vector product yields three equations, of which two are linearly independent. For $N$ point correspondences, stacking rows yields the $2N \times 9$ matrix $A$, solved via $\min_{\|\mathbf{h}\|=1} \|A \mathbf{h}\|^2$.

  3. Hartley coordinate normalisation transform

    T = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\bar{d}} & 0 & -\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\bar{x}}{\bar{d}} \\ 0 & \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\bar{d}} & -\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\bar{y}}{\bar{d}} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \bar{d} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sqrt{(x_i - \bar{x})^2 + (y_i - \bar{y})^2}

    Translating coordinates so their centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ lies at the origin and scaling average distance from the origin to $\sqrt{2}$ bounds the numerical entries of $A$, balancing condition numbers and preventing numerical overflow in SVD solvers.

04 Key takeaways

  • A homography is an invertible $3 \times 3$ projective matrix with 8 degrees of freedom relating two planar perspective views.
  • Homographies hold whenever scenes are planar OR whenever the camera rotates purely around its optical centre with zero translation.
  • Four non-collinear point correspondences are the minimum necessary to solve for $H$ (each pair provides 2 independent linear constraints).
  • The Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) algorithm transforms the non-linear perspective mapping into an algebraic null-space problem $A\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{0}$, solved via SVD.
  • Hartley coordinate normalisation (zero centroid, average radius $\sqrt{2}$) is essential to prevent numerical instability during algebraic estimation.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np

# Estimate a 3x3 homography matrix from 4 point correspondences via DLT
# Source coordinates (e.g. tilted document corners in an image)
pts_src = np.array([
    [100.0, 150.0],
    [400.0, 120.0],
    [380.0, 480.0],
    [80.0,  450.0]
], dtype=np.float32)

# Target coordinates (e.g. rectified rectangular document 300x400)
pts_dst = np.array([
    [0.0,   0.0],
    [300.0, 0.0],
    [300.0, 400.0],
    [0.0,   400.0]
], dtype=np.float32)

def compute_homography_dlt(src, dst):
    N = src.shape[0]
    A = []
    
    for i in range(N):
        x, y = src[i, 0], src[i, 1]
        u, v = dst[i, 0], dst[i, 1]
        
        # Two linear equations per point correspondence from cross product: x' x (H x) = 0
        A.append([-x, -y, -1.0,  0.0,  0.0,  0.0, u*x, u*y, u])
        A.append([ 0.0,  0.0,  0.0, -x, -y, -1.0, v*x, v*y, v])
        
    A = np.array(A, dtype=np.float64)
    
    # Solve A h = 0 via Singular Value Decomposition
    # Optimal solution h is the singular vector corresponding to smallest singular value
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
    H = Vt[-1].reshape(3, 3)
    
    # Normalise so bottom-right element H[2, 2] == 1.0
    return H / H[2, 2]

H = compute_homography_dlt(pts_src, pts_dst)
print("Estimated 3x3 Homography Matrix H:")
print(np.round(H, 4))

# Verify forward projection on the 4 source points
ones = np.ones((4, 1), dtype=np.float32)
pts_src_homo = np.hstack([pts_src, ones])  # shape (4, 3)

# Project points: x' ~ H @ x
projected_homo = (H @ pts_src_homo.T).T
# Perform perspective division by w'
projected_cart = projected_homo[:, :2] / projected_homo[:, 2:3]

reprojection_error = np.linalg.norm(projected_cart - pts_dst, axis=1)
print("\nTarget coordinates:")
print(pts_dst)
print("\nReprojected coordinates:")
print(np.round(projected_cart, 2))
print(f"\nMax reprojection error: {np.max(reprojection_error):.2e} pixels")
assert np.max(reprojection_error) < 1e-3, "Homography DLT estimation error!"
print("Homography DLT algorithm successfully verified!")

Common pitfalls

  • Applying homography to 3D scenes with translation: If the camera translates in a non-planar 3D scene, parallax causes points at different depths to shift at different rates; a single homography cannot model parallax and produces heavy double-vision ghosting artifacts.
  • Collinear point sets: If three of the four selected calibration points lie along a single straight line, the DLT constraint matrix becomes rank-deficient, yielding an invalid or degenerate homography matrix.
  • Omitting Hartley coordinate normalisation: Solving DLT directly on raw pixel coordinates ($x, y \approx 1000$) produces condition numbers on the order of $10^8$, corrupting the SVD solution with severe floating-point rounding errors.
  • Inverting perspective division: Forgetting to divide by the homogeneous coordinate $w' = h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33}$ when mapping back to Cartesian space produces unscaled projective coordinates that misplace warped points.
  • Forward warping holes: Iterating through source image pixels and scattering them to target coordinates $(x', y') = H(x, y)$ leaves empty unpainted pixel gaps ("holes") in the target image due to rounding; always use inverse warping (iterating over target pixels and pulling from $H^{-1}$).

06 Check yourself

Q1Under what physical camera conditions is a $3 \times 3$ planar homography mathematically valid to register images?

Why: A homography models perspective projections between planes. It holds strictly if all scene points lie on a single 3D plane, or if the camera rotates purely about its optical centre (eliminating depth parallax).

Q2How many non-collinear point correspondences are required as a mathematical minimum to estimate an 8-degree-of-freedom homography matrix?

Why: Matrix $H$ has 9 elements with 8 degrees of freedom up to scale. Each 2D point correspondence provides two equations ($x'$ and $y'$), requiring at least $8 / 2 = 4$ non-collinear pairs.

Q3Why does the Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) algorithm require coordinate normalisation (Hartley normalisation) prior to running SVD?

Why: Without normalisation, matrix $A$ contains entries varying from $1$ to $10^6$, leading to numerical instability and poor SVD solutions. Translating coordinates to zero mean and scaling average radius to $\sqrt{2}$ balances condition numbers.

Q4When warping an image using an estimated homography $H$, why is inverse warping preferred over forward warping?

Why: Forward mapping maps source pixels to floating-point target coordinates, which round into clustered integer coordinates and leave vacant unpainted gaps ("holes"). Inverse mapping visits each target pixel and pulls values using bilinear interpolation.
