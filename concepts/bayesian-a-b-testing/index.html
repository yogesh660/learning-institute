Bayesian A/B testing

Bayesian A/B testing reformulates online experimentation from binary null-hypothesis significance testing (p-values) into direct posterior probability assessment. Instead of asking "Is there a statistically significant difference under the assumption that the variants are identical?", Bayesian testing answers the intuitive questions business stakeholders actually care about: "What is the probability that Variant B is better than Variant A?" (P(B > A)) and "What is the expected financial loss if I choose Variant B and I am wrong?" (Expected Loss / Bayes Risk). This framework naturally integrates historical priors and supports continuous decision-making without multiple testing penalties.

01 The idea

Ask an executive what a p-value of 0.03 means, and they will almost certainly say: "There is a 97% chance Variant B is better than Variant A." In classical frequentist statistics, this interpretation is completely false: a p-value only measures how improbable the observed data would be if the two variants were identical. Why use a framework whose core output is routinely misunderstood? Bayesian A/B testing computes the exact quantity decision-makers desire: the direct probability of superiority and the monetary risk of making the wrong decision.

Probability of Superiority (P(B > A)): By placing prior distributions on conversion rates \theta_A ~ Beta(\alpha_A, \beta_A) and \theta_B ~ Beta(\alpha_B, \beta_B), observed customer conversions update these into posterior distributions. We can directly compute the probability that variant B has a higher conversion rate than variant A by integrating over the joint posterior: P(\theta_B > \theta_A | D).

Expected Loss (Bayes Risk) as the Decision Threshold: Knowing P(B > A) = 90% is not enough. If B is better, it might beat A by only 0.01%; but if A is better, B might destroy conversion rates by 50%! Expected Loss computes the expected financial cost of choosing variant B when A is actually better: E[Loss | Choose B] = \int_{\theta_A > \theta_B} (\theta_A - \theta_B) p(\theta_A, \theta_B | D) d\theta_A d\theta_B. When Expected Loss drops below a business threshold \epsilon (e.g. 0.001 lift), you can safely ship Variant B with guaranteed bounded risk.

Natural Peeking and Continuous Stopping: In Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution at step n is an exact reflection of all evidence accumulated up to step n. Updating posteriors as data streams in does not corrupt the posterior. You can evaluate Expected Loss continuously and stop whenever uncertainty is sufficiently small.

In everyday terms

Imagine deciding between two routes to the airport. A frequentist test assumes both routes take identical time, collects 50 test drives, and computes whether the observed difference is rare enough to reject equality. A Bayesian driver combines traffic reports (priors) with live GPS data (observations) to output: "Route B has an 88% chance of being faster, and if you choose Route B and it turns out to be slower, you will lose on average only 45 seconds." That expected loss lets the driver make a rational, risk-calibrated choice instantly.

03 The maths

  1. Beta-Binomial Conjugate Posterior Updating

    \theta_A \mid (y_A, n_A) \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + y_A, \; \beta_0 + n_A - y_A), \qquad \theta_B \mid (y_B, n_B) \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0 + y_B, \; \beta_0 + n_B - y_B)

    The Beta distribution is conjugate to the Binomial likelihood. The posterior parameters represent effective pseudo-counts of successes and failures combining prior beliefs with observed experimental results.

  2. Probability of Superiority Formulation

    P(\theta_B > \theta_A \mid \mathcal{D}) = \int_0^1 f(\theta_B \mid \mathcal{D}_B) \left[ \int_0^{\theta_B} f(\theta_A \mid \mathcal{D}_A) \, d\theta_A \right] d\theta_B

    Because Variant A and Variant B are sampled independently, their joint posterior distribution factorises as p(\theta_A, \theta_B \mid \mathcal{D}) = p(\theta_A \mid \mathcal{D}_A) \, p(\theta_B \mid \mathcal{D}_B). The probability that B beats A is the volume of the joint distribution above the diagonal \theta_B > \theta_A.

  3. Expected Loss (Bayes Risk)

    \mathcal{L}_B = \mathbb{E}\Big[\max(0, \, \theta_A - \theta_B) \;\Big|\; \mathcal{D}\Big], \qquad \mathcal{L}_A = \mathbb{E}\Big[\max(0, \, \theta_B - \theta_A) \;\Big|\; \mathcal{D}\Big]

    Expected Loss calculates the expected penalty incurred if you choose a given variant and that variant is suboptimal. Unlike p-values which only measure evidence against equality, Expected Loss directly measures economic risk.

04 Key takeaways

  • Bayesian A/B testing computes direct posterior probabilities of superiority: P(\theta_B > \theta_A | \mathcal{D}).
  • Expected Loss (Bayes Risk) measures the economic cost of making the wrong decision, providing an actionable stopping criterion.
  • Historical priors easily incorporate baseline domain knowledge, anchoring small-sample experiments against extreme flukes.
  • Because posteriors represent cumulative updated evidence, Bayesian testing allows continuous monitoring without multiple testing inflation.

05 In code

python
"""
Bayesian A/B Testing Framework
Demonstrating Beta-Binomial posterior updating, Probability of Superiority,
Expected Loss (Bayes Risk), and threshold stopping rules.
"""

import numpy as np

# Experiment Setup:
# Variant A (Control): 1,000 visitors, 120 conversions (12.0%)
# Variant B (Treatment): 1,000 visitors, 145 conversions (14.5%)
visitors_a, conv_a = 1000, 120
visitors_b, conv_b = 1000, 145

# Prior: Weakly informative Beta(alpha=10, beta=90) -> Prior Mean = 10%
alpha_prior = 10.0
beta_prior = 90.0

# Posterior Beta parameters
alpha_post_a = alpha_prior + conv_a
beta_post_a = beta_prior + (visitors_a - conv_a)

alpha_post_b = alpha_prior + conv_b
beta_post_b = beta_prior + (visitors_b - conv_b)

# Monte Carlo Sampling from Posteriors
num_draws = 100000
draws_a = np.random.beta(alpha_post_a, beta_post_a, size=num_draws)
draws_b = np.random.beta(alpha_post_b, beta_post_b, size=num_draws)

# 1. Probability of Superiority P(B > A)
prob_b_better = np.mean(draws_b > draws_a)

# 2. Relative Lift Distribution: (B - A) / A
relative_lift = (draws_b - draws_a) / draws_a
expected_lift = np.mean(relative_lift)
lift_ci_lower = np.percentile(relative_lift, 2.5)
lift_ci_upper = np.percentile(relative_lift, 97.5)

# 3. Expected Loss (Bayes Risk)
# Loss if we choose B: max(0, A - B)
loss_choose_b = np.mean(np.maximum(0, draws_a - draws_b))
# Loss if we choose A: max(0, B - A)
loss_choose_a = np.mean(np.maximum(0, draws_b - draws_a))

# Business Decision Rule: Stop and ship B if Expected Loss <= 0.001 (0.1% conversion rate)
risk_threshold = 0.0010
ship_b = loss_choose_b <= risk_threshold

print(f"=== Bayesian A/B Test Analysis ===")
print(f"Variant A: {conv_a}/{visitors_a} ({conv_a/visitors_a:.1%}) -> Posterior Mean: {np.mean(draws_a):.3f}")
print(f"Variant B: {conv_b}/{visitors_b} ({conv_b/visitors_b:.1%}) -> Posterior Mean: {np.mean(draws_b):.3f}")

print(f"\nProbability Variant B is Superior: P(B > A) = {prob_b_better:.1%}")
print(f"Expected Relative Lift: {expected_lift:.1%} [95% Credible Interval: {lift_ci_lower:.1%}, {lift_ci_upper:.1%}]")

print(f"\nExpected Loss if choosing B: {loss_choose_b:.5f} ({loss_choose_b * 100:.3f}% conversion rate)")
print(f"Expected Loss if choosing A: {loss_choose_a:.5f} ({loss_choose_a * 100:.3f}% conversion rate)")

print(f"\nDecision against Risk Threshold (epsilon = {risk_threshold}):")
if ship_b:
    print(f"  SHIP VARIANT B: Expected risk ({loss_choose_b:.5f}) is within acceptable threshold ({risk_threshold})!")
else:
    print(f"  CONTINUE EXPERIMENT: Expected risk ({loss_choose_b:.5f}) exceeds threshold.")

Common pitfalls

  • Relying solely on Probability of Superiority without checking Expected Loss: A variant might have P(B > A) = 95% but possess a heavy left tail that risks catastrophic financial losses if wrong.
  • Using overly strong subjective priors without validation: An aggressive prior requires substantial experimental evidence to overturn, potentially masking real treatment gains.
  • Confusing credible intervals with confidence intervals: A 95% Bayesian credible interval means there is a 95% probability the true parameter lies within the bounds; a frequentist confidence interval does not carry this interpretation.

06 Check yourself

Q1In Bayesian A/B testing, what does 'Expected Loss' measure?

Why: Expected Loss calculates E[max(0, \theta_A - \theta_B)]. It quantifies the expected margin of regret if you choose variant B and variant A was actually better.

Q2Why is continuous monitoring permissible in Bayesian A/B testing without the classical 'peeking problem'?

Why: In Bayesian inference, probability is a measure of belief given data. The posterior distribution after n observations is identical whether you inspected it 100 times or zero times prior to step n.

Q3What conjugate prior family is used to model conversion rates in binary A/B testing?

Why: The Beta distribution is conjugate to the Binomial likelihood: updating Beta(\alpha, \beta) with y successes and n-y failures produces Beta(\alpha + y, \beta + n - y).
