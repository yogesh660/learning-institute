Prompting and in-context learning

Prompting and In-Context Learning (ICL) are the defining operational paradigms of modern Large Language Models (LLMs). Rather than updating neural network parameters via gradient descent, in-context learning enables models to infer novel tasks, adapt outputs, and execute complex reasoning purely by conditioning on natural language instructions and demonstration exemplars supplied in the input context window. Methods like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Self-Consistency unlock multi-step cognitive reasoning without parameter modifications.

01 The idea

To teach a machine learning model to classify legal contracts in 2018, you needed to annotate thousands of documents and execute hours of backpropagation on a GPU cluster. Today, you simply prepend three example contracts and their corresponding classifications into the prompt of a Large Language Model. The model immediately mimics the pattern, generalising to unseen contracts on the fly. How does an autoregressive next-token predictor perform task adaptation without updating a single weight?

The Prompting Continuum: From Zero-Shot to Few-Shot: - Zero-Shot Prompting: Providing explicit task instructions directly ("Classify the sentiment of the following review: ..."). Relies entirely on pre-trained semantic knowledge. - Few-Shot Prompting: Prepending k demonstration pairs (Input: X, Output: Y) before the test query. Demonstrations communicate expected formatting, stylistic tone, edge cases, and output schema. - Chain-of-Thought (CoT): Encouraging the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps ("Let's think step by step") before outputting the final answer. This decomposes complex multi-hop problems into simple linear steps.

Mechanisms of In-Context Learning: Why does ICL work? Mechanistic interpretability research (such as Anthropic's induction heads) reveals that Transformer attention layers form specialized circuits that perform pattern completion: 1. Induction Heads: Detect previous occurrences of tokens and copy subsequent tokens, effectively performing algorithmic copying and associative retrieval. 2. Implicit Meta-Gradients: Mathematical analysis shows that forward passes through self-attention layers implement an implicit form of meta-gradient descent, optimising temporary internal representation states within the context window without updating persistent weights.

Prompt Sensitivity and Biases: ICL is notoriously sensitive to superficial prompt variations: - Recency Bias: Models disproportionately favor repeating classes or patterns demonstrated in the final few-shot example. - Format Bias: Inconsistent delimiters or whitespace significantly alter output quality. - Hallucinated Reasoning: Models may produce fluent rationales that sound convincing but contain factual errors (rationalization rather than reasoning).

In everyday terms

Think of an experienced human actor preparing for an audition. Traditional fine-tuning is like sending the actor to drama school for four years to permanently rewire their personality. In-context learning is handing the actor a three-page script backstage and saying: "You are playing a 19th-century British detective; here are two lines of dialogue to show your accent and cadence; now walk onto stage and deliver the scene." The actor does not permanently change who they are; they temporarily inhabit the persona defined by the prompt script.

03 The maths

  1. In-Context Learning conditional probability formulation

    P(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}) = \prod_{i=1}^{|y|} P(y_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{|x|}, y_1, \dots, y_{i-1}, \, \mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}})

    Here $\mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_k, y_k)\}$ represents the set of $k$ few-shot demonstration exemplars provided in the prompt context window. The model generates output $y$ by conditioning on both the query $x$ and demonstration history $\mathcal{D}_{\text{prompt}}$.

  2. Self-Consistency majority voting consensus

    \hat{y} = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}\left( \operatorname{extract}(r_i) = c \right)

    Here $r_1, \dots, r_N$ are independent stochastic reasoning chains sampled from the model at non-zero temperature, and $\operatorname{extract}(r_i)$ extracts the final categorical answer from reasoning chain $r_i$. The consensus answer $\hat{y}$ is selected via modal majority vote.

  3. Attention-based induction head operation

    \operatorname{Attn}(A \dots B \dots A \to B): \quad Q_i \approx K_{j+1} \implies \text{Copy token } B

    Induction heads detect recurring prefix patterns ($A$) and shift attention to attend to the token that followed $A$ in previous occurrences ($B$), enabling autoregressive copying and sequence continuation in-context.

04 Key takeaways

  • In-Context Learning (ICL) enables Large Language Models to adapt to new tasks purely by conditioning on prompt context without weight updates.
  • Few-shot prompting provides input-output demonstration pairs that establish task formatting, tone, and decision boundaries.
  • Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting elicits step-by-step reasoning tokens, dramatically boosting performance on arithmetic and logic tasks.
  • Self-consistency aggregates multiple sampled reasoning paths via majority voting to filter out isolated reasoning errors.
  • ICL is governed by specialized attention circuits (such as induction heads) that perform algorithmic copying and implicit gradient descent.

05 In code

python
from collections import Counter

class FewShotPromptBuilder:
    """
    Structured prompt builder supporting zero-shot, few-shot,
    and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompt construction.
    """
    def __init__(self, system_instruction):
        self.system = system_instruction
        self.examples = []

    def add_example(self, input_text, output_text, reasoning=None):
        self.examples.append({
            "input": input_text,
            "output": output_text,
            "reasoning": reasoning
        })

    def build_prompt(self, test_input, cot=False):
        prompt_parts = [f"System: {self.system}\n"]
        
        # Add few-shot exemplars
        for i, ex in enumerate(self.examples, 1):
            prompt_parts.append(f"Example {i}:")
            prompt_parts.append(f"Input: {ex['input']}")
            if cot and ex['reasoning']:
                prompt_parts.append(f"Reasoning: {ex['reasoning']}")
            prompt_parts.append(f"Output: {ex['output']}\n")
            
        # Add test query
        prompt_parts.append("Test Query:")
        prompt_parts.append(f"Input: {test_input}")
        if cot:
            prompt_parts.append("Reasoning: Let's think step by step.")
        prompt_parts.append("Output:")
        
        return "\n".join(prompt_parts)

def self_consistency_vote(sampled_answers):
    """Aggregate multiple sampled outputs via majority voting."""
    counts = Counter(sampled_answers)
    winner, top_votes = counts.most_common(1)[0]
    confidence = top_votes / len(sampled_answers)
    return winner, confidence, counts

# Build Math Word Problem Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought Prompt
system_msg = "You are an expert mathematical assistant. Solve word problems with clear reasoning."
builder = FewShotPromptBuilder(system_msg)

builder.add_example(
    input_text="A bakery had 24 cupcakes. They sold 3/4 of them, then baked 10 more. How many cupcakes do they have now?",
    reasoning="They started with 24 cupcakes. Sold 3/4 of 24 = 18 cupcakes. Remaining: 24 - 18 = 6. Then baked 10 more: 6 + 10 = 16.",
    output="16"
)

test_problem = "A farmer has 40 sheep. 1/4 of them run away, and then 5 sheep are returned. How many sheep are there now?"
formatted_prompt = builder.build_prompt(test_problem, cot=True)

print("Generated Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought Prompt:")
print("-" * 65)
print(formatted_prompt)
print("-" * 65)

# Simulate Self-Consistency Voting across 5 sampled reasoning paths
mock_sampled_outputs = ["35", "35", "30", "35", "35"]
winner, conf, vote_breakdown = self_consistency_vote(mock_sampled_outputs)

print(f"\nSelf-Consistency Aggregation across {len(mock_sampled_outputs)} sampled paths:")
print(f"  Vote Breakdown: {dict(vote_breakdown)}")
print(f"  Consensus Answer: '{winner}' (Confidence: {conf * 100:.1f}%)")

Common pitfalls

  • Class imbalance in few-shot demonstrations: If your five-shot prompt contains four positive examples and one negative example, the model exhibits strong majority class bias, predicting positive regardless of input text. Maintain balanced demonstration distributions.
  • Recency bias in exemplar ordering: The final example in a prompt exerts disproportionate influence on the generated output format and classification decision. Permute example orders to check stability.
  • Assuming Chain-of-Thought guarantees factual correctness: A model can produce a beautifully structured, logically coherent chain of reasoning that is based on a completely fabricated premise. Always ground reasoning in verifiable retrieval context.
  • Exceeding context token limits: Stuffing too many long demonstrations into the prompt degrades attention resolution and risks truncation; 3 to 5 concise, well-chosen exemplars consistently outperform 20 verbose examples.

06 Check yourself

Q1How does In-Context Learning (ICL) in Large Language Models achieve task adaptation without updating model parameters?

Why: In-context learning is entirely gradient-free at inference time. The model relies on internal attention circuits developed during pre-training to recognize the structure of input-output demonstrations and continue the pattern.

Q2Why does Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting substantially improve performance on complex multi-step reasoning tasks?

Why: In autoregressive Transformers, each output token represents a fixed budget of computation. Forcing an immediate final answer requires solving complex math in a single forward pass. CoT spreads the calculation over intermediate reasoning tokens.

Q3What is the purpose of the Self-Consistency decoding strategy in prompt engineering?

Why: Wang et al. showed that correct reasoning paths tend to converge on the same correct answer even if the intermediate wording differs, whereas erroneous reasoning produces divergent answers. Majority voting across diverse paths boosts accuracy.
