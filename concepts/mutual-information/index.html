01 The idea

While Pearson correlation measures strictly linear associations and fails on curved dependencies, Mutual Information (MI) quantifies all forms of statistical dependence—linear, non-linear, and non-monotonic. Grounded in information theory, MI measures how much uncertainty about one random variable is eliminated by knowing the value of another. It serves as a foundational technique for non-linear feature selection, decision tree splitting, and representation learning.

Suppose you analyse the relationship between outdoor temperature $X$ and electricity demand $Y$. On freezing winter days and scorching summer days, electricity demand surges to power heating and air conditioning, while on mild spring days demand is low. The relationship forms a parabolic U-shape ($Y \approx X^2$).

If you compute Pearson's linear correlation coefficient $r$, the positive slope on the hot side cancels out the negative slope on the cold side, yielding $r \approx -0.058$ ($p = 0.193$). An analyst relying solely on linear correlation would mistakenly conclude that temperature has zero statistical relationship with electricity demand!

Mutual information avoids this blind spot entirely. It does not look for straight lines; it compares the joint distribution $P(X, Y)$ against the product of the marginals $P(X)P(Y)$ (what the joint distribution would look like if $X$ and $Y$ were completely independent). When temperature strongly dictates electricity demand, knowing $X$ removes substantial uncertainty from $Y$, yielding a high mutual information of $1.722$ bits. If two variables are truly statistically independent, $P(X, Y) = P(X)P(Y)$ everywhere, and mutual information drops to identically $0.0$ bits.

In everyday terms

Pearson correlation is like a rigid wooden ruler that can only measure straight paths; mutual information is like a flexible measuring tape that wraps around every curve and bend to measure true connection.

02 Step by step

  1. Estimate the joint probability distribution $P(X, Y)$ and compute individual marginal distributions $P(X)$ and $P(Y)$.
  2. For continuous variables, discretise features into two-dimensional grid bins or employ $k$-nearest neighbours estimators.
  3. Compute the individual Shannon marginal entropies $H(X)$ and $H(Y)$.
  4. Compute the joint entropy $H(X, Y)$ or the conditional entropy $H(Y \mid X)$.
  5. Calculate mutual information: $I(X; Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$.
  6. Rank candidate explanatory features by mutual information with the target label to perform non-linear feature selection.

03 The maths

  1. Mutual Information Definition via KL Divergence

    $$I(X; Y) = D_{\text{KL}}\left(P(X, Y) \parallel P(X)P(Y)\right) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(x, y) \log_2\left(\frac{P(x, y)}{P(x)P(y)}\right)$$

    Here $P(x, y)$ is the joint probability mass function, and $P(x)P(y)$ is the product of independent marginal distributions. Mutual information measures the Kullback–Leibler divergence between joint reality and the independent null baseline. By Gibbs' inequality, $I(X; Y) \ge 0$, with equality holding if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are statistically independent.

  2. Entropy Decomposition and Symmetry

    $$I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)$$

    Here $H(Y)$ is the initial uncertainty in $Y$, and $H(Y \mid X)$ is the remaining uncertainty in $Y$ after observing $X$. Their difference represents the information gain about $Y$ provided by $X$. Because entropy Venn diagrams are symmetric, $I(X; Y) = I(Y; X)$.

  3. Continuous Mutual Information and Gaussian Equivalence

    $$I(X; Y) = -\frac{1}{2} \ln\left(1 - \rho^2\right) \quad \text{for jointly Gaussian variables}$$

    When $X$ and $Y$ follow a bivariate normal distribution with correlation coefficient $\rho$, mutual information is an exact monotonic function of $\rho^2$. For non-Gaussian variables, $I(X; Y) \ge -\frac{1}{2}\ln(1 - \rho^2)$, capturing higher-order non-linear associations that linear correlation misses.

04 Key takeaways

  • Mutual information detects all forms of statistical association, including non-linear, non-monotonic, and periodic relationships.
  • $I(X; Y)$ is strictly non-negative and symmetric: $I(X; Y) = I(Y; X) \ge 0$, with $I(X; Y) = 0$ if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
  • In decision trees, the information gain splitting criterion is mathematically identical to the mutual information between the candidate split feature and the target class.
  • Unlike Pearson correlation, mutual information is invariant under any invertible, monotonic transformation of the variables.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 500

# Parabolic non-linear relationship: Y = X^2 + noise
x = rng.uniform(-3.0, 3.0, size=n)
y = x**2 + rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=0.5, size=n)

# 1. Pearson linear correlation (fails on non-linear curves)
r_val, p_r = stats.pearsonr(x, y)

# 2. Discrete mutual information via 2D binning (Shannon formula in bits)
bins = 12
hist_2d, _, _ = np.histogram2d(x, y, bins=bins)
p_xy = hist_2d / np.sum(hist_2d)
p_x = np.sum(p_xy, axis=1, keepdims=True)
p_y = np.sum(p_xy, axis=0, keepdims=True)

# I(X; Y) = sum P(x,y) * log2(P(x,y) / (P(x)*P(y)))
nonzero = p_xy > 0
mi_bits = np.sum(p_xy[nonzero] * np.log2(p_xy[nonzero] / (p_x @ p_y)[nonzero]))

print(f"Pearson correlation r: {r_val:.3f} (p-value: {p_r:.3f})")
print(f"Mutual Information:    {mi_bits:.3f} bits")

Common pitfalls

  • Naive binning on continuous data: too few bins underestimates true dependence; too many bins overestimates MI due to finite-sample sampling noise.
  • Expecting an upper bound of 1.0: unnormalised mutual information is bounded by $\min(H(X), H(Y))$; use normalised mutual information (NMI) if a $[0, 1]$ metric is required.
  • Feature redundancy in greedy selection: picking top features by individual MI can select highly collinear duplicates; use minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR).
  • High computational cost: estimating high-dimensional joint mutual information suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Pearson's correlation coefficient evaluate to approximately zero on a perfect parabolic relationship ($Y = X^2$ on $[-3, 3]$)?

Why: Pearson correlation evaluates strictly linear slope. In symmetric U-shaped curves, positive and negative slopes cancel out, yielding $r \approx 0$ despite complete deterministic dependence.

Q2Under what condition is the mutual information $I(X; Y)$ strictly equal to zero?

Why: By definition, $I(X; Y) = D_{\text{KL}}(P(X, Y) \parallel P(X)P(Y))$. The divergence is zero if and only if $P(X, Y) = P(X)P(Y)$, which is the exact definition of statistical independence.

Q3In decision tree induction algorithms like ID3 and C4.5, what information-theoretic quantity corresponds to "information gain"?

Why: Information gain is defined as $H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$, which is mathematically identical to the mutual information $I(X; Y)$.

Q4What is the theoretical upper bound on the mutual information $I(X; Y)$ between two discrete random variables?

Why: Because knowing $X$ cannot eliminate more uncertainty than exists in $X$ itself or in $Y$, $I(X; Y) \le \min(H(X), H(Y))$.
