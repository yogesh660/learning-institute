01 The idea

A GMM says each point was generated by one of $K$ Gaussians, chosen with probabilities $\pi_k$. The EM algorithm alternates between estimating which Gaussian each point probably came from (E-step) and refitting each Gaussian to its weighted points (M-step).

It is k-means with three upgrades: clusters can be elliptical and tilted (full covariance), different sizes, and assignments are probabilities rather than hard labels. In fact k-means is a GMM with equal, spherical covariances shrunk to zero.

Because it is a proper probability model, you can compare different $K$ values with BIC, compute the likelihood of new points (useful for anomaly detection), and sample new data.

In everyday terms

Explaining a crowd photo as several overlapping groups, each with its own centre and spread, and saying how likely each person belongs to each group.

02 How it works

  1. Initialise means (often with k-means), covariances and weights.
  2. E-step: compute responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$, the probability point $i$ came from component $k$.
  3. M-step: update $\pi_k$, $\mu_k$, $\Sigma_k$ as responsibility-weighted estimates.
  4. Repeat until the log-likelihood stops improving; choose $K$ by BIC.

  1. Use "elongated" blobs: GMM tilts its ellipses, k-means cannot.
  2. Switch covariance to spherical: it starts behaving like soft k-means.

04 The maths

  1. Mixture density

    $$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x;\,\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$

    Weights $\pi_k$ sum to 1.

  2. E-step

    $$\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}$$

    Bayes’ rule for "which component produced this point".

  3. M-step

    $$N_k = \sum_i\gamma_{ik},\quad \pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n},\quad \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i,\quad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)(x_i - \mu_k)^\top$$

    Weighted versions of the usual mean and covariance. Each EM iteration never lowers the likelihood.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • Soft, probabilistic assignments.
  • Elliptical clusters of different sizes.
  • Principled model selection with BIC; density estimation for free.

Limitations

  • Assumes Gaussian components.
  • Local optima; sensitive to initialisation.
  • Full covariances need many parameters in high dimensions.

Reach for it when

Overlapping elliptical clusters, density estimation, anomaly scoring.

Think twice when

Non-convex shapes or very high-dimensional data with few rows.

07 In code

python
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

df = pd.read_csv("datasets/raw/mall_customers.csv")
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[["Annual_Income_k", "Spending_Score"]])
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

bics = {k: GaussianMixture(k, covariance_type="full", n_init=5, random_state=0).fit(X).bic(X) for k in range(1, 9)}
best_k = min(bics, key=bics.get)
gmm = GaussianMixture(best_k, covariance_type="full", n_init=5, random_state=0).fit(X)
print("best k by BIC:", best_k, "soft memberships:", gmm.predict_proba(X[:3]).round(2))

Common pitfalls

  • Using full covariances with many features and few rows: singular matrices.
  • Comparing log-likelihoods across different K without a penalty such as BIC.

09 Check yourself

Q1What does the E-step compute?

Why: It fills in the hidden "which component" variable with probabilities.

Q2k-means is a special case of a GMM with:

Why: As the shared variance goes to zero, responsibilities become 0/1 and the M-step becomes the mean update.

Q3In scikit-learn's GaussianMixture, which covariance_type setting allows each cluster to have its own independent oriented ellipse with arbitrary tilt and aspect ratio?

Why: full allows each Gaussian component to estimate its own unconstrained covariance matrix $\Sigma_k$, accommodating tilted, elliptical clusters of differing orientations and widths.

Q4Why is the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) or Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) preferred over raw training log-likelihood when choosing the number of components $K$ in a GMM?

Why: Just like $R^2$ in regression or inertia in $k$-means, adding more parameters and Gaussian components monotonically increases the training log-likelihood. BIC penalises model complexity (number of free parameters), identifying the optimal trade-off where extra components cease to justify their parameter cost.

Q5A 2-component GMM has mixture weights $\pi_1 = 0.60$ and $\pi_2 = 0.40$. At a specific observation $x$, the Gaussian probability density evaluations are $\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_1, \Sigma_1) = 0.20$ and $\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_2, \Sigma_2) = 0.30$. In the E-step, what is the responsibility $\gamma_{1}(x)$ (the posterior probability that $x$ belongs to component 1)?

Why: By Bayes' rule: $\gamma_1(x) = \frac{\pi_1 \mathcal{N}_1}{\pi_1 \mathcal{N}_1 + \pi_2 \mathcal{N}_2} = \frac{0.60 \times 0.20}{(0.60 \times 0.20) + (0.40 \times 0.30)} = \frac{0.12}{0.12 + 0.12} = \frac{0.12}{0.24} = 0.50$. Although component 2 has a higher local density ($0.30$ vs $0.20$), component 1's larger mixture weight ($0.60$ vs $0.40$) exactly balances the posterior responsibility to $0.50$.

Q6In the M-step for component 1, three 1D points have values $x_1 = 2.0$, $x_2 = 6.0$, and $x_3 = 10.0$, with corresponding responsibilities $\gamma_{11} = 0.50$, $\gamma_{21} = 0.25$, and $\gamma_{31} = 0.25$. What is the updated mean $\mu_1$?

Why: Total effective sample size is $N_1 = 0.50 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 1.00$. The weighted mean is $\mu_1 = \frac{(0.50 \times 2.0) + (0.25 \times 6.0) + (0.25 \times 10.0)}{1.00} = 1.0 + 1.5 + 2.5 = 5.0$. Notice how point $x_1$ pulls the mean towards itself due to its higher responsibility of $0.50$.

Q7What theoretical guarantee does the Expectation–Maximisation (EM) algorithm provide regarding the log-likelihood of the observed data at each iteration?

Why: By Jensen's inequality and the construction of the evidence lower bound (ELBO), each full E-step and M-step cycle is mathematically guaranteed to increase or keep constant the marginal log-likelihood, guaranteeing convergence to a local maximum (or saddle point).

Q8What is the purpose of the reg_covar hyperparameter (default 1e-6 in scikit-learn's GaussianMixture)?

Why: If a Gaussian component isolates a single point (or collinear points), its variance along that direction collapses to 0, causing the determinant $\det(\Sigma) \to 0$ and the likelihood to explode to infinity (a singularity). Adding reg_covar ensures all eigenvalues of $\Sigma_k$ remain strictly positive.

Q9How can a fitted Gaussian Mixture Model be naturally deployed as an anomaly / novelty detector?

Why: A GMM is a generative probability density model. Normal points in dense cluster regions have high probability density $p(x)$, while anomalies falling in sparse, empty areas receive very low probability densities. Thresholding $\ln p(x)$ (via score_samples) directly scores anomaly severity.

Q10A GMM with $K = 4$ components is fitted to a dataset with $p = 10$ features. How many total covariance parameters must be estimated across all 4 components under covariance_type='full' compared with covariance_type='diag'?

Why: For a $p$-dimensional full covariance matrix, symmetry means only the diagonal and lower triangle are independent, giving $\frac{p(p+1)}{2} = \frac{10 \times 11}{2} = 55$ parameters per component, or $4 \times 55 = 220$ across 4 components. Under diagonal covariance, each component only estimates its $p = 10$ diagonal variances, giving $4 \times 10 = 40$ parameters.

Q11In a 1D GMM, component $k$ has two training points: $x_1 = 2.0$ with responsibility $\gamma_{1k} = 0.75$, and $x_2 = 6.0$ with responsibility $\gamma_{2k} = 0.25$. Its updated mean was computed as $\mu_k = 3.0$. What is the updated variance $\sigma_k^2 = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum \gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)^2$ for this component?

Why: Total responsibility is $N_k = 0.75 + 0.25 = 1.00$. The squared deviations from $\mu_k = 3.0$ are $(2.0 - 3.0)^2 = 1.0$ and $(6.0 - 3.0)^2 = 9.0$. Weighting each deviation gives $\sigma_k^2 = \frac{(0.75 \times 1.0) + (0.25 \times 9.0)}{1.00} = 0.75 + 2.25 = 3.00$.

Q12The Bayesian Information Criterion for a model with log-likelihood $\ln \hat L$, $p_{\text{params}}$ free parameters, and $n$ observations is $\text{BIC} = -2\ln \hat L + p_{\text{params}}\ln(n)$. Why does BIC penalise additional parameters more heavily than AIC ($\text{AIC} = -2\ln \hat L + 2p_{\text{params}}$) when $n \ge 8$?

Why: In AIC the penalty multiplier per parameter is always a constant 2. In BIC the multiplier is $\ln(n)$. Since $e^2 \approx 7.389$, whenever $n \ge 8$ we have $\ln(n) > 2$, meaning BIC penalises complexity more strictly and tends to select fewer components than AIC.
