01 The idea

Instead of one best set of weights, Bayesian regression keeps a whole distribution over them. Predictions come with error bars that are wide where data is scarce and narrow where it is plentiful.

Start with a prior: before seeing any data, you believe the weights are probably small, $\beta \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1}I)$. Each data point then updates that belief using Bayes’ rule. With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise, the posterior is also Gaussian, so the update has a closed form.

The payoff is the predictive distribution. Far from the training data, many different lines are still plausible, so the prediction band fans out. That is the model saying "I don’t know", which plain OLS can never do.

In everyday terms

A detective with a list of suspects (possible lines). Each clue rules some out; the remaining spread is how sure the detective is.

02 How it works

  1. Choose a prior precision $\alpha$ for the weights and a noise precision $\beta$.
  2. Compute the posterior covariance $S_N$ and mean $m_N$ in closed form.
  3. For a new $x$, the predictive mean is $m_N^\top x$ and the variance is $1/\beta + x^\top S_N x$.
  4. Optionally learn $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from the data by maximising the evidence (what BayesianRidge does).

03 Try it

Points arrive one at a time. The shaded band is the 95% predictive interval; thin lines are samples from the posterior over lines.

Things to try
  1. Start with 1–2 points: the sampled lines fan out in every direction.
  2. Add points and watch the band pinch where data is, while staying wide at the edges.
  3. Raise the prior precision $\alpha$: lines are pulled toward flat, even with data.

04 The maths

  1. Posterior over weights

    $$S_N^{-1} = \alpha I + \beta X^\top X, \qquad m_N = \beta S_N X^\top y$$

    The posterior mean $m_N$ equals the ridge solution with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$. The new ingredient is $S_N$, the uncertainty.

  2. Predictive distribution

    $$p(y_* \mid x_*, \mathcal D) = \mathcal N\!\big(m_N^\top x_*,\; \tfrac{1}{\beta} + x_*^\top S_N x_*\big)$$

    Two sources of uncertainty: irreducible noise $1/\beta$, and uncertainty about the weights $x_*^\top S_N x_*$, which shrinks as data accumulates.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • Calibrated uncertainty for every prediction.
  • Regularisation strength learned from the data.
  • Natural for sequential updating as new data arrives.

Limitations

  • Still a linear model.
  • Uncertainty is only as good as the Gaussian assumptions.
  • Full covariance costs $O(p^3)$.

Reach for it when

Small data, decisions that depend on how sure the prediction is, or active learning (sample where the band is widest).

Think twice when

Very high-dimensional data or clearly non-linear signal; see Gaussian process regression for a non-linear Bayesian model.

07 In code

python
from sklearn.linear_model import BayesianRidge

model = BayesianRidge().fit(X_tr, y_tr)
mean, std = model.predict(X_te, return_std=True)
print("learned noise precision:", model.alpha_, "weight precision:", model.lambda_)
print("first prediction: %.2f ± %.2f" % (mean[0], 1.96 * std[0]))

Common pitfalls

  • Mixing up scikit-learn’s names: in BayesianRidge alpha_ is the noise precision and lambda_ the weight precision, the reverse of many textbooks.
  • Trusting the band far outside the data when the true relationship is not linear: the model is confidently wrong about the shape.

09 Check yourself

Q1The posterior mean of Bayesian linear regression with a zero-mean Gaussian prior equals:

Why: Maximising Gaussian likelihood times a Gaussian prior is the same as minimising squared error plus an L2 penalty.

Q2Why does the prediction band widen away from the data?

Why: Weight uncertainty matters more for inputs far from where training data constrained the line.

Q3What mathematical property makes the posterior weight distribution Gaussian in standard Bayesian linear regression?

Why: When a linear model has Gaussian observation noise and a Gaussian prior on the weights, the prior is conjugate to the likelihood, resulting in an exact closed-form Gaussian posterior $\mathcal{N}(m_N, S_N)$.

Q4In the Bayesian linear regression predictive variance $\sigma_*^2 = \frac{1}{\beta} + x_*^\top S_N x_*$, what do the two components represent?

Why: $\frac{1}{\beta}$ is the intrinsic data noise variance ($\sigma^2$), which persists even with infinite data. The second term $x_*^\top S_N x_*$ is the epistemic uncertainty about the true weights, which shrinks towards zero as more training data pins down the parameters.

Q5What does setting a very large prior precision $\alpha \to \infty$ for the weights $\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \alpha^{-1}I)$ express?

Why: Precision is the reciprocal of variance ($\alpha = 1/\sigma_0^2$). As $\alpha \to \infty$, the prior variance collapses to 0 around the origin, exerting infinite shrinkage and forcing posterior weights to 0.

Q6In scikit-learn's BayesianRidge, what do the fitted attributes model.alpha_ and model.lambda_ represent?

Why: In scikit-learn's notation for BayesianRidge, alpha_ denotes the precision of the Gaussian noise and lambda_ denotes the precision of the weight prior (the reverse of Christopher Bishop's PRML notation where $\alpha$ is weight precision and $\beta$ is noise precision).

Q7How does BayesianRidge estimate its regularisation hyperparameters without requiring cross-validation?

Why: Empirical Bayes (or type-II maximum likelihood) integrates out the model weights $\beta$ to compute the marginal likelihood of the observations given the precision parameters, updating them iteratively until convergence.

Q8Why can the predictive uncertainty band of a Bayesian linear regression model be deceptive when extrapolating far from training data?

Why: Bayesian linear regression only models uncertainty over linear slopes and intercepts. It cannot represent uncertainty about whether the linear model family itself is correct.

Q9What makes Bayesian linear regression naturally suited for online (streaming) learning?

Why: By Bayes' theorem, $p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2) \propto p(\mathcal{D}_2 \mid \mathbf{w}) p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathcal{D}_1)$. The posterior after data $\mathcal{D}_1$ becomes the prior for $\mathcal{D}_2$, allowing exact recursive Bayesian updating without storing past raw data.

Q10In a 1D Bayesian linear model without intercept ($y = w x + \epsilon$), the prior on $w$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, \alpha^{-1})$ with prior precision $\alpha = 2.0$. The noise precision is $\beta = 1.0$. You observe two data points: $(x_1=1, y_1=3)$ and $(x_2=2, y_2=5)$. What is the posterior precision $S_N^{-1}$ and the posterior variance $S_N$ of $w$?

Why: In 1D, the posterior precision is $S_N^{-1} = \alpha + \beta \sum x_i^2$. Here $\sum x_i^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 = 5$. With $\alpha = 2.0$ and $\beta = 1.0$, $S_N^{-1} = 2.0 + 1.0(5) = 7.0$. The posterior variance is the inverse of precision: $S_N = 1 / 7.0 \approx 0.143$.

Q11Continuing from the previous problem with prior precision $\alpha = 2.0$, noise precision $\beta = 1.0$, and data points $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 5)$, what is the posterior mean $m_N$ of the weight $w$?

Why: The posterior mean is $m_N = \beta S_N \sum x_i y_i$. The cross-product sum is $\sum x_i y_i = (1)(3) + (2)(5) = 3 + 10 = 13$. With $\beta = 1.0$ and $S_N = 1/7$, $m_N = 1.0 \times \frac{1}{7} \times 13 = \frac{13}{7} \approx 1.857$. (The unregularised OLS slope would be $13/5 = 2.600$).

Q12For the model with posterior mean $m_N = \frac{13}{7}$, posterior variance $S_N = \frac{1}{7}$, and noise precision $\beta = 1.0$ (noise variance $\sigma^2 = 1.0$), what is the predictive distribution (mean and variance) for a new query point at $x_* = 3$?

Why: The predictive mean is $\mu_* = m_N x_* = \frac{13}{7} \times 3 = \frac{39}{7} \approx 5.571$. The predictive variance is $\sigma_*^2 = \frac{1}{\beta} + x_*^2 S_N = 1.0 + 3^2 \times \frac{1}{7} = 1.0 + \frac{9}{7} = \frac{16}{7} \approx 2.286$.
