k-Means Clustering

Splits data into k groups by alternating two steps: assign points to the nearest centre, move each centre to its points’ mean.

01 The idea

Pick $k$ centres. Assign every point to its nearest centre, move each centre to the average of its points, and repeat until nothing changes.

k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS, also called inertia): the total squared distance from each point to its centre. Both steps can only lower it, so the algorithm always converges, though only to a local minimum. Different starting centres can give different answers, which is why it is run several times and why k-means++ seeding matters.

The algorithm implicitly assumes clusters are round, similar in size and similar in spread. It will happily cut a banana-shaped cluster in half. You must choose $k$; the elbow of the WCSS curve and the silhouette score help.

In everyday terms

Placing $k$ shops in a town, letting every resident go to the closest one, then moving each shop to the centre of its customers, and repeating.

02 How it works

  1. Scale the features.
  2. Initialise $k$ centres (k-means++ by default).
  3. Assignment step: each point joins its nearest centre.
  4. Update step: each centre moves to the mean of its points.
  5. Repeat until assignments stop changing; keep the best of several runs.

03 Try it

Step through Lloyd’s algorithm. Each press alternates between the assignment step and the update step.

Things to try
  1. Use "moons": k-means slices the crescents straight across.
  2. Re-seed a few times with random initialisation and compare the final WCSS.
  3. Slide k from 2 to 8 and look at the WCSS curve for an elbow.

04 The maths

  1. Objective (WCSS / inertia)

    $$J = \sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{x_i \in C_k}\lVert x_i - \mu_k\rVert^2$$

    For fixed assignments the best centre is the mean; for fixed centres the best assignment is the nearest centre. Alternating the two is coordinate descent on $J$.

  2. Update

    $$\mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{x_i \in C_k}x_i$$

    The mean is what makes k-means sensitive to outliers; k-medoids uses actual data points instead.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • Fast and scales to large datasets (mini-batch variant too).
  • Simple to explain and implement.
  • Centres are easy to interpret as prototypes.

Limitations

  • You must choose k.
  • Assumes round, equal-sized clusters.
  • Sensitive to scaling, outliers and initialisation.

Reach for it when

Customer segmentation, vector quantisation, a first look at structure in numeric data.

Think twice when

Non-convex shapes, very different cluster sizes, or lots of noise; see DBSCAN or Gaussian mixtures.

07 In code

python
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

df = pd.read_csv("datasets/raw/mall_customers.csv")
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[["Annual_Income_k", "Spending_Score"]])
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

for k in range(2, 9):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(k, round(km.inertia_, 1), round(silhouette_score(X, km.labels_), 3))

Common pitfalls

  • Not scaling features, so one feature decides the clusters.
  • Picking k by inertia alone; it always falls as k grows.
  • Treating cluster labels as stable across runs; they are arbitrary numbers.

09 Check yourself

Q1Why does k-means always converge?

Why: A monotonically decreasing objective over a finite set of partitions must stop.

Q2Which shape does k-means handle worst?

Why: It partitions space into convex Voronoi cells around the centres.

Q3What mathematical objective function does standard k-means clustering minimise?

Why: k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares $J = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{x_i \in C_k} \lVert x_i - \mu_k \rVert^2$, also referred to as inertia.

Q4Why is feature standardisation (e.g. StandardScaler) essential before running k-means?

Why: k-means relies entirely on Euclidean distances. If salary is measured in tens of thousands and age in tens, salary will completely dictate cluster assignments unless features are scaled to equal variance.

Q5In the update step of Lloyd's algorithm, why is the arithmetic mean $\mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{x_i \in C_k} x_i$ chosen as the new cluster centre?

Why: Setting the derivative of $\sum \lVert x_i - \mu \rVert^2$ with respect to $\mu$ to zero yields $\mu = \frac{1}{|C|}\sum x_i$. The mean is the exact mathematical minimiser of squared Euclidean error.

Q6Why can you NOT simply pick the optimal number of clusters $k$ by choosing the value that minimises inertia (WCSS)?

Why: Adding more centres always reduces or preserves squared distances to the nearest centre. At $k = n$, inertia is identically zero. Instead, the "elbow" where reduction rate flattens or silhouette scores are used.

Q7What is the computational time complexity of assigning a single new, unseen query point to its nearest cluster in a fitted k-means model with $k$ clusters in $p$ dimensions?

Why: Assigning a new observation requires computing its Euclidean distance to each of the $k$ centroids in $p$-dimensional space, costing $O(k \cdot p)$ operations.

Q8How does k-means partition the feature space between adjacent cluster centroids?

Why: Assigning points to the nearest centre partitions Euclidean space into Voronoi cells bounded by linear hyperplanes equidistant between pairs of centres.

Q9Why does running KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10) execute the algorithm 10 times?

Why: k-means is only guaranteed to converge to a local minimum, which depends heavily on initial centroid placement. n_init=10 runs 10 independent trials and outputs the best solution.

Q10Consider 4 points on a 1D line assigned to a cluster: $\{2, 4, 6, 8\}$. What is the cluster centroid $\mu$, and what is the within-cluster sum of squared errors (inertia contribution) $\sum (x_i - \mu)^2$?

Why: The centroid is the mean $\mu = 20/4 = 5.0$. The squared deviations are $(-3)^2 + (-1)^2 + 1^2 + 3^2 = 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 = 20$.

Q11Two clusters in 1D have centres at $\mu_1 = 4.0$ and $\mu_2 = 12.0$. At what coordinate $x$ lies the decision boundary between these two clusters, and to which cluster is a new point $x = 7.9$ assigned?

Why: In 1D, the Voronoi boundary between two centres is their exact midpoint: $(4.0 + 12.0)/2 = 8.0$. Because $7.9 < 8.0$, the point is closer to $\mu_1$ ($|7.9 - 4.0| = 3.9$ vs $|7.9 - 12.0| = 4.1$) and is assigned to Cluster 1.

Q12In an iteration of 2-means clustering in 2D, Cluster 1 contains three points: $A = [1, 2]$, $B = [3, 4]$, and $C = [5, 6]$. What are the coordinates of the updated centroid $\mu_1$?

Why: The updated centroid is the component-wise mean: $\mu_x = (1 + 3 + 5)/3 = 3.0$ and $\mu_y = (2 + 4 + 6)/3 = 4.0$, yielding $[3.0, 4.0]$.
