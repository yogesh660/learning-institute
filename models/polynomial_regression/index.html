Polynomial Regression

Still linear regression, but on powers of the input, so the fitted curve can bend. The classic way to see overfitting happen.

01 The idea

Add $x^2, x^3, \dots, x^d$ as new features and run ordinary least squares on them. The model is linear in its weights, but the curve it draws is not.

A straight line cannot follow a hump. Give the model $x^2$ as an extra column and it can learn a parabola; give it $x^3$ and it can learn an S-bend. The fitting machinery does not change at all: it is still OLS, solved with the same normal equations.

The degree $d$ is a dial between underfitting and overfitting. Low degrees are too stiff to follow the signal. High degrees have enough freedom to pass through every noisy point and then swing wildly between them. Watching training error fall while test error rises as you turn this dial is the clearest picture of the Bias–variance trade-off.

In everyday terms

A flexible ruler: the higher the degree, the bendier the ruler, until it can be bent to touch every pin, including the ones that are just noise.

02 How it works

  1. Expand each input into $[1, x, x^2, \dots, x^d]$ (with several inputs, also include interaction terms such as $x_1x_2$).
  2. Scale the expanded features; high powers have wildly different ranges.
  3. Fit ordinary least squares, or ridge if the degree is high.
  4. Choose $d$ with Cross-validation, not with training error, which always falls as $d$ grows.

03 Try it

A noisy sine wave, split into training points (filled) and held-out test points (hollow). Turn the degree up and watch the two error curves part ways.

Things to try
  1. Find the degree where test RMSE is lowest. That is the sweet spot of the bias–variance trade-off.
  2. Push the degree to 15 and look at the edges of the plot.
  3. Switch on ridge with a small $\lambda$ at degree 15: the curve calms down without lowering the degree.

04 The maths

  1. The model

    $$\hat y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \dots + \beta_d x^d = \phi(x)^\top \beta$$

    $\phi(x)$ is the feature map. Because $\hat y$ is linear in $\beta$, the OLS solution $\hat\beta = (\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$ still applies, with $\Phi$ the expanded design matrix.

  2. Why high degree explodes

    $$\text{features for } p \text{ inputs at degree } d: \;\binom{p+d}{d}$$

    With 10 inputs and degree 3 that is already 286 columns. The columns $x^7, x^8, x^9$ are also nearly collinear, which makes $\Phi^\top\Phi$ ill-conditioned and the coefficients huge. Regularisation is the usual cure.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • Captures smooth curvature with a model you already understand.
  • Still closed-form and fast for low degrees.
  • Great teaching tool for under- and overfitting.

Limitations

  • Feature count explodes with many inputs.
  • Wild behaviour at the edges of the data and beyond (Runge’s phenomenon).
  • High-degree terms are collinear and unstable without regularisation.

Reach for it when

One or two inputs with a smooth, curved relationship, and you want something interpretable.

Think twice when

Many inputs, or you need to extrapolate. Splines, GAMs or tree ensembles are safer.

07 In code

python
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

pipe = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(include_bias=False), StandardScaler(), Ridge(alpha=1.0))
search = GridSearchCV(pipe, {"polynomialfeatures__degree": [1, 2, 3, 4]}, cv=5,
                      scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error")
search.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(search.best_params_, -search.best_score_)

Common pitfalls

  • Choosing the degree by training error. It always prefers the highest degree.
  • Forgetting to scale: $x^5$ for $x = 100$ is $10^{10}$, which wrecks numerical precision.
  • Extrapolating. A degree-6 polynomial heads to $\pm\infty$ just outside the training range.

09 Check yourself

Q1Is polynomial regression a linear model?

Why: "Linear" refers to the parameters. The curve in $x$ bends, but $\hat y$ is a weighted sum of fixed features, so all of linear regression’s machinery still works.

Q2You raise the degree from 3 to 12. Training RMSE drops sharply, test RMSE rises. This is:

Why: The model now has enough freedom to fit noise in the training set, and that noise does not repeat in new data.

Q3Why is ridge often paired with high-degree polynomial features?

Why: Columns like $x^8$ and $x^9$ are almost collinear. The $\lambda I$ term keeps $\Phi^\top\Phi + \lambda I$ well conditioned and the curve calm.
