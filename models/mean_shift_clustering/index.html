Mean Shift Clustering

Every point climbs uphill on the estimated density until it reaches a peak. Points sharing a peak form a cluster.

01 The idea

Mean shift places a window around each point, moves it to the mean of the points inside, and repeats. Windows drift toward density peaks (modes); the number of clusters is the number of peaks found.

Imagine the data as a landscape where height is density. Mean shift is a hill-climber: the mean of a neighbourhood is always a step uphill. The bandwidth sets the window size: small finds many little hills, large smooths the landscape into a few big ones.

In everyday terms

Raindrops running downhill into lakes, upside-down: every point flows to the top of its hill.

02 How it works

  1. Choose a bandwidth (estimate_bandwidth helps).
  2. For each point, repeatedly shift it to the kernel-weighted mean of its neighbours.
  3. Stop when shifts are tiny; merge modes closer than the bandwidth.
  4. Label points by the mode they reached.

03 Try it

Press Shift and watch every point climb toward its density peak. Colours are assigned once points settle.

Things to try
  1. Small bandwidth: many tiny peaks. Large bandwidth: everything flows to one or two.

04 The maths

  1. Mean shift update

    $$m(x) = \frac{\sum_i K\big(\tfrac{x_i - x}{h}\big)x_i}{\sum_i K\big(\tfrac{x_i - x}{h}\big)}, \qquad x \leftarrow m(x)$$

    $m(x) - x$ points along the gradient of a kernel density estimate.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • No need to choose k.
  • Finds arbitrarily shaped density peaks.
  • One intuitive parameter.

Limitations

  • Slow: $O(n^2)$ per iteration.
  • Single global bandwidth.
  • Struggles in high dimensions.

Reach for it when

Low-dimensional data such as image colour segmentation or object tracking.

Think twice when

Large or high-dimensional data.

07 In code

python
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

df = pd.read_csv("datasets/raw/mall_customers.csv")
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df[["Annual_Income_k", "Spending_Score"]])
from sklearn.cluster import MeanShift, estimate_bandwidth

bw = estimate_bandwidth(X, quantile=0.2, random_state=0)
ms = MeanShift(bandwidth=bw, bin_seeding=True).fit(X)
print("bandwidth", round(bw, 3), "clusters found:", len(ms.cluster_centers_))

Common pitfalls

  • Choosing the bandwidth by eye and then trusting the cluster count as a discovery.

09 Check yourself

Q1Increasing the bandwidth generally:

Why: A wider kernel smooths away small density peaks.

Q2What statistical feature of the data distribution does each cluster centre represent in mean shift clustering?

Why: Mean shift is a mode-seeking algorithm: points climb the gradient of a kernel density estimate, and cluster centres correspond to local density peaks (modes).

Q3Why does mean shift clustering not require the user to specify the number of clusters $k$ in advance?

Why: In mean shift, each point flows to a stationary density mode. The number of unique modes after merging nearby peaks automatically becomes the number of clusters.

Q4What role does the bin_seeding=True parameter play in scikit-learn's MeanShift?

Why: By default, mean shift starts a hill-climbing trajectory from every single data point ($n$ searches). bin_seeding=True quantises points onto a coarse grid and only seeds from occupied bins, drastically speeding up computation.

Q5What does the mean shift vector $m(x) - x$ represent geometrically at any location $x$?

Why: Comaniciu and Meer proved that the mean shift vector $m(x) - x$ is proportional to the normalised gradient of the kernel density estimate, guaranteeing that moving along it climbs toward the nearest density mode.

Q6How does scikit-learn's estimate_bandwidth(X, quantile=0.2) select a suggested bandwidth parameter?

Why: estimate_bandwidth uses a nearest-neighbour graph to calculate the distance distribution between sample points, returning the distance corresponding to the requested quantile (e.g. the 20th percentile).

Q7What occurs during the post-processing phase of mean shift after all hill-climbing paths have stopped moving?

Why: Multiple points climbing the same broad peak may terminate at slightly different floating-point coordinates. The algorithm merges candidate modes that lie within distance $h$ of each other into a single consolidated mode.

Q8What happens if an engineer sets cluster_all=False in MeanShift?

Why: When cluster_all=True (default), every point is assigned to a cluster, even isolated outliers. Setting cluster_all=False allows sparse orphan points to be marked with label -1 (noise).

Q9Why does mean shift become computationally prohibitive on high-dimensional datasets with many thousands of samples?

Why: Mean shift scales quadratically $O(T \cdot n^2)$ with sample size. In high dimensions, data sparsity (the curse of dimensionality) flattens density gradients, meaning nearly all points become isolated local modes unless the bandwidth is inflated so large that all structure is obliterated.

Q10Consider 3 points in 1D: $x_1 = 2$, $x_2 = 4$, and $x_3 = 10$. A mean shift window uses a flat (uniform) kernel with bandwidth $h = 3$ centred at current position $x = 3$. What is the updated mean position $m(x)$?

Why: The window $[3 - 3, 3 + 3] = [0, 6]$ captures only $x_1 = 2$ and $x_2 = 4$ ($x_3 = 10$ is outside). With a flat kernel, the updated position is the arithmetic mean of points inside the window: $(2 + 4) / 2 = 3.00$.

Q11Suppose two points $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = 2$ have weights $K_1 = 0.75$ and $K_2 = 0.25$ under a Gaussian kernel centred at $x = 0.5$. What is the kernel-weighted mean shift update $m(x) = \frac{\sum K_i x_i}{\sum K_i}$, and what is the mean shift vector $m(x) - x$?

Why: The weighted mean is $m(x) = (0.75 \times 0 + 0.25 \times 2) / (0.75 + 0.25) = 0.50 / 1.00 = 0.50$. The mean shift vector is $m(x) - x = 0.50 - 0.50 = 0.00$, indicating this location is an equilibrium point.

Q12In a 2D image segmentation task with $n = 100,000$ pixels, an engineer runs raw MeanShift with bin_seeding=False. If each hill-climbing trajectory takes an average of $T = 15$ iterations and evaluates all $n$ pixels, approximately how many distance calculations are performed?

Why: Initiating trajectories from all $10^5$ points with 15 iterations each, where each iteration compares against all $10^5$ data points, requires $10^5 \times 15 \times 10^5 = 1.5 \times 10^{11}$ (150 billion) distance evaluations. This quadratic scaling is why bin seeding or downsampling is essential.
