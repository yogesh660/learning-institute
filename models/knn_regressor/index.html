01 The idea

k-NN does not learn a formula. It memorises the training set and, for each new point, averages the targets of its $k$ closest neighbours.

To price a house, look at the five most similar houses that sold recently and average their prices. That is k-NN regression. "Similar" means close in feature space, so the choice of distance and the scaling of features matter a lot.

Small $k$ follows the data closely, noise included (low bias, high variance). Large $k$ averages over a wide neighbourhood and flattens real structure (high bias, low variance). Weighting neighbours by $1/\text{distance}$ lets close points count more.

In everyday terms

Estimating a house price from the five most similar recent sales.

02 How it works

  1. Scale the features (otherwise the feature with the largest range dominates the distance).
  2. Store the training set, usually in a KD-tree or ball tree for fast lookup.
  3. For a query, find the $k$ nearest training points.
  4. Return their mean target, or a distance-weighted mean.

04 The maths

  1. Prediction

    $$\hat y(x) = \frac{\sum_{i \in N_k(x)} w_i\, y_i}{\sum_{i \in N_k(x)} w_i}, \qquad w_i = 1 \;\text{or}\; \frac{1}{d(x, x_i)}$$

    $N_k(x)$ is the set of the $k$ closest training points under a distance $d$, usually Euclidean.

  2. The curse of dimensionality

    $$\text{edge of a cube holding a fraction } r \text{ of the data in } p \text{ dims} = r^{1/p}$$

    To capture 10% of uniformly spread data in 10 dimensions you need a cube spanning 80% of each axis. "Neighbours" stop being local, which is why k-NN struggles with many features.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • No training, trivially updatable with new data.
  • Captures any shape given enough data.
  • Easy to explain: "these are the examples I used".

Limitations

  • Slow prediction on large datasets.
  • Very sensitive to feature scaling and irrelevant features.
  • Degrades in high dimensions.
  • Cannot extrapolate beyond the range of training targets.

Reach for it when

Low-dimensional data with plenty of rows and a non-linear but smooth relationship.

Think twice when

Many features, very large datasets with tight latency budgets, or the need to extrapolate.

07 In code

python
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsRegressor())
grid = {"kneighborsregressor__n_neighbors": [3, 5, 9, 15, 25],
        "kneighborsregressor__weights": ["uniform", "distance"]}
search = GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=5, scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error").fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print(search.best_params_)

Common pitfalls

  • Forgetting to scale. A feature measured in thousands silently decides who the neighbours are.
  • Including irrelevant features, each of which adds noise to every distance.
  • Using k = 1 and reporting training error (which is always zero).

09 Check yourself

Q1What does increasing $k$ do to k-NN regression?

Why: Averaging more neighbours irons out noise, but also real local structure.

Q2Why is k-NN poor at extrapolation?

Why: Averages of observed targets are bounded by the observed targets.

Q3What is the training time complexity of a standard brute-force k-Nearest Neighbours regressor?

Why: k-NN is an instance-based lazy learner; training consists solely of memorising or storing the $n$ training instances with $p$ features, so there is no parameter optimisation phase.

Q4If you train a 1-Nearest Neighbour regressor ($k = 1$) on a dataset with unique input points, what is its training Mean Squared Error (MSE)?

Why: For $k = 1$, each training point queries itself as its closest neighbour, perfectly predicting its own exact target value $y_i$, yielding zero training residuals.

Q5Why does distance weighting (weights='distance') modify the behaviour of k-NN regression compared to uniform weighting?

Why: Under inverse-distance weighting, neighbours immediately adjacent to the query point have much greater influence on the predicted average than distant neighbours on the outer edge of the neighbourhood.

Q6Suppose feature 1 is annual income in pounds (£20,000 to £150,000) and feature 2 is age in years (18 to 70). What happens if k-NN regression is fitted without feature scaling?

Why: Euclidean distance squares coordinate differences. A difference of £5,000 income produces $(5000)^2 = 25,000,000$, which completely dwarfs a 40-year age gap $(40)^2 = 1,600$. Features must be standardised so each dimension contributes fairly.

Q7How does the curse of dimensionality impact k-NN regression in high-dimensional spaces?

Why: In high dimensions, the volume of the space grows exponentially ($r^{1/p}$). To capture even a small fraction of data, the neighbourhood must span almost the entire range of every feature, destroying locality.

Q8When does using a KD-tree or Ball tree provide substantial speedups over brute-force search during k-NN inference?

Why: Spatial partitioning trees achieve $\mathcal{O}(p \log n)$ query time for low dimensions, but their performance degrades to brute-force $\mathcal{O}(n \cdot p)$ as dimension $p$ exceeds roughly 20 because almost all branches must be inspected.

Q9How does the choice of $k$ affect the bias-variance tradeoff in k-NN regression?

Why: A small $k$ (e.g. $k=1$) adapts sharply to every local wiggle and noise point (low bias, high variance). A large $k$ averages over a vast region, smoothing out local peaks and valleys towards the global mean (high bias, low variance).

Q10You have training data points on a 1D line: $(1.0, 3.0)$, $(2.0, 5.0)$, $(4.0, 9.0)$, $(7.0, 15.0)$. For a query point at $x_* = 3.0$, what is the prediction of a uniform $k$-NN regressor with $k = 3$?

Why: Distances from $x_* = 3.0$ are: $|3-2|=1.0$ (target 5.0), $|3-4|=1.0$ (target 9.0), and $|3-1|=2.0$ (target 3.0). The 3 closest points have targets $5.0, 9.0, 3.0$. Their uniform mean is $(5.0 + 9.0 + 3.0) / 3 = 17.0 / 3 \approx 5.67$.

Q11For the same query point at $x_* = 3.0$ and training points $(1.0, 3.0)$, $(2.0, 5.0)$, $(4.0, 9.0)$, $(7.0, 15.0)$ with $k = 3$, what is the prediction using inverse distance weighting $w_i = 1 / d(x_*, x_i)$?

Why: Distances are $d_1 = 1.0$ ($y=5.0$), $d_2 = 1.0$ ($y=9.0$), and $d_3 = 2.0$ ($y=3.0$). Weights are $w_1 = 1.0, w_2 = 1.0, w_3 = 0.5$, summing to $2.5$. Weighted target sum is $1.0(5.0) + 1.0(9.0) + 0.5(3.0) = 15.5$. Prediction is $15.5 / 2.5 = 6.20$.

Q12In a $p$-dimensional unit hypercube $[0, 1]^p$ with uniformly distributed points, what must the edge length $e$ of a sub-cube be to capture a fraction $r = 0.10$ (10%) of the data when $p = 10$? (Given $0.10^{0.1} \approx 0.794$).

Why: The volume of a hypercube of edge length $e$ in $p$ dimensions is $e^p$. To capture a fraction $r$ of the uniform volume, $e^p = r \implies e = r^{1/p}$. For $r = 0.10$ and $p = 10$, $e = 0.10^{1/10} \approx 0.794$, showing that the neighbourhood must span nearly $80\%$ of each coordinate range.
