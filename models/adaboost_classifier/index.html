01 The idea

AdaBoost trains a sequence of weak classifiers, usually one-split trees. After each round it increases the weight of misclassified examples, so the next learner pays more attention to them.

A decision stump on its own is barely better than guessing. AdaBoost combines many of them with weights: stumps that did well get a bigger say. Because each new stump is trained on re-weighted data, the ensemble gradually carves out a complex boundary from simple pieces.

It turns out AdaBoost is gradient boosting with an exponential loss. That loss punishes confident mistakes very hard, which makes AdaBoost sensitive to mislabelled examples and outliers.

In everyday terms

A tutor who notes which questions you got wrong and makes sure the next lesson focuses on them.

02 How it works

  1. Start with equal weights $w_i = 1/n$.
  2. Fit a weak learner on the weighted data; compute its weighted error $\varepsilon_m$.
  3. Give it a say $\alpha_m = \tfrac12\log\frac{1 - \varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}$.
  4. Multiply the weights of misclassified rows by $e^{\alpha_m}$ and renormalise.
  5. Final prediction: the sign of $\sum_m \alpha_m h_m(x)$.

03 Try it

Each round adds one stump. Point size shows the current weight: big points are the ones the ensemble keeps getting wrong.

Things to try
  1. Step through rounds and watch the misclassified points grow.
  2. Turn on label noise: a few flipped points end up with huge weights.

04 The maths

  1. Learner weight

    $$\alpha_m = \frac12\log\frac{1 - \varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}$$

    A learner with 50% error gets zero weight; a perfect one gets infinite weight.

  2. Re-weighting

    $$w_i \leftarrow w_i\,e^{-\alpha_m y_i h_m(x_i)}$$

    Correct points ($y_ih_m = 1$) are down-weighted, mistakes up-weighted. This is the exponential loss $e^{-yF(x)}$ in action.

06 When to use it

Strengths

  • Simple, few hyperparameters.
  • Turns very weak learners into a strong model.
  • Weight history shows which examples are hard.

Limitations

  • Sensitive to label noise and outliers (exponential loss).
  • Sequential training.
  • Usually beaten by modern gradient boosting.

Reach for it when

Clean data and a desire to understand boosting from its roots.

Think twice when

Noisy labels; prefer gradient boosting with log-loss.

09 Check yourself

Q1What happens to the weight of a misclassified training example after a round?

Why: It is multiplied by $e^{\alpha_m} > 1$.

Q2AdaBoost is gradient boosting with which loss?

Why: Its weight update follows from minimising $\sum_i e^{-y_iF(x_i)}$ stage by stage.

Q3What base learner is most commonly used in discrete AdaBoost (SAMME)?

Why: AdaBoost is specifically designed to combine very simple weak learners (classifiers slightly better than random guessing), typically one-split decision stumps (DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1)).

Q4In AdaBoost, what is the weight $\alpha_m$ assigned to a base learner that has a weighted training error rate $\varepsilon_m = 0.5$ (random guess in binary classification)?

Why: The learner weight is $\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2}\log(\frac{1 - \varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m})$. When $\varepsilon_m = 0.5$, $\frac{1 - 0.5}{0.5} = 1$, and $\frac{1}{2}\log(1) = 0.0$, giving a random learner zero influence.

Q5Why is AdaBoost notoriously sensitive to mislabelled data and severe outliers?

Why: In AdaBoost, an outlier that is consistently misclassified receives weight $w_i \propto e^{\sum \alpha_m}$. This exponential weight explosion dominates the sample distribution, causing the algorithm to overfit heavily to corrupted labels.

Q6What is the role of the learning_rate parameter in scikit-learn's AdaBoostClassifier?

Why: The learning rate $\eta \in (0, 1]$ shrinks each tree's stage contribution $\alpha_m$. Smaller learning rates act as regularisation, requiring more estimators (n_estimators) but improving generalisation.

Q7Why are sample weights normalised to sum to 1 ($\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1$) after every boosting round in AdaBoost?

Why: Normalising weights by the partition function $Z_m = \sum_i w_i \exp(-\alpha_m y_i h_m(x_i))$ ensures weights form a proper probability distribution that can be passed to base learners without numerical overflow.

Q8Why does AdaBoost sometimes continue to decrease test set error even after training classification error reaches 0%?

Why: As shown by Schapire et al. (1998), subsequent boosting rounds continue to increase the margins of easy points (the distance from the boundary), creating a wider margin separator similar to SVMs.

Q9What is the main difference between SAMME and SAMME.R in scikit-learn's AdaBoostClassifier?

Why: SAMME.R (Real AdaBoost) utilises probability estimates $P(y=c \mid x)$ produced by base estimators rather than hard categorical decisions, providing continuous confidence updates that converge with fewer iterations.

Q10A decision stump achieves a weighted training error rate of $\varepsilon_m = 0.20$ on a binary classification problem ($y \in \{-1, +1\}$). What is its exact learner weight $\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2}\log(\frac{1 - \varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m})$? (Given $\log(4) \approx 1.3863$).

Why: With $\varepsilon_m = 0.20$: $\frac{1 - \varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m} = \frac{0.80}{0.20} = 4.0$. Then $\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2}\log(4.0) = \frac{1}{2}(1.38629) \approx 0.6931$.

Q11An observation has initial weight $w_i = 0.10$. A weak learner with weight $\alpha_m = 0.6931$ (so $e^{\alpha_m} = 2.0$ and $e^{-\alpha_m} = 0.5$) misclassifies this observation ($y_i h_m(x_i) = -1$). Before global normalisation, what is the unnormalised updated weight $w_i'$?

Why: The unnormalised weight update is $w_i \leftarrow w_i \exp(-\alpha_m y_i h_m(x_i))$. Since the example is misclassified, $y_i h_m(x_i) = -1$, giving $w_i' = w_i \exp(+\alpha_m) = 0.10 \times 2.0 = 0.20$.

Q12An AdaBoost ensemble consists of 3 base learners with weights $\alpha_1 = 0.8, \alpha_2 = 0.5, \alpha_3 = 0.6$. For a test sample, their predictions are $h_1(x) = -1, h_2(x) = +1, h_3(x) = -1$. What is the raw score $F(x) = \sum_{m=1}^3 \alpha_m h_m(x)$ and the final predicted label $\operatorname{sign}(F(x))$?

Why: $F(x) = 0.8(-1) + 0.5(+1) + 0.6(-1) = -0.8 + 0.5 - 0.6 = -0.9$. Since $F(x) = -0.9 < 0$, $\operatorname{sign}(F(x)) = -1$.
